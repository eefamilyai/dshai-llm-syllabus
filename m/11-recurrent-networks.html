Recurrent Networks, LSTMs, and Sequence Models

Sequences before attention — gates, cell states, and the gradient product that killed them.

on this page

Why this module exists

A feedforward network computes a function of fixed-size input. Language is not that. The recurrence is the first architecture in this curriculum that carries state forward in time, and the machinery built to train it — backpropagation through time, gate-by-gate credit assignment, truncated credit, gradient clipping — is not historical trivia. The LSTM's forget gate is a per-channel multiplicative recurrence, which is exactly what a state-space model's discretized transition is (M13); the gated skip connection that rescues gradient flow is the residual connection that every transformer block in M12 is built from; and the encoder-decoder bottleneck is the specific problem that attention was invented to solve. Skip the recurrence and attention looks like an arbitrary arrangement of matrices; read the recurrence and attention looks inevitable: it is what you get when you decide the state should be all previous positions, addressed by content rather than one vector, carried forward. You also get the field's best-documented case study in an architecture losing for reasons that are not about expressiveness. Recurrent networks can represent anything a transformer can, in principle. They lost on wall-clock time, and understanding precisely why is more instructive than any amount of attention algebra.

Prerequisites check

  1. A computation is $h \mapsto \tanh(Wh + b)$. Write $\partial h'/\partial h$ as a matrix. [M02]
  2. What is $\sigma'(z)$ in terms of $\sigma(z)$, and what is its maximum? [M06]
  3. For softmax cross-entropy with logits $\mathbf{o}$ and true class $k$, what is $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{o}$? [M07]
  4. Why does a product of many matrices with spectral norm $< 1$ shrink exponentially? [M01]
  5. What does the chain rule say about $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \mathbf{W}$ when $\mathbf{W}$ is used at many points in the graph? [M07]
Answers 1. $\partial h'/\partial h = \operatorname{diag}(1 - h'^2)\,W$, where $h'$ is the *output* (post-tanh) vector. Two factors: the elementwise derivative of tanh, then the linear map. 2. $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, maximized at $z=0$ where it equals $0.25$. That factor of $0.25$ is why a stack of sigmoid layers attenuates gradients by at least $4\times$ per layer. 3. $\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{e}_k$: the softmax output minus the one-hot target. 4. Because $\|\prod_i A_i\| \le \prod_i\|A_i\| \le \rho^T$, so the product decays geometrically; in the scalar case $0.9^{100} \approx 2.7\times10^{-5}$. 5. The gradient is the *sum* of the contributions from every use of $\mathbf{W}$: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W} = \sum_{t}\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}\big|_{\text{at }t}$. This is the whole content of "weight sharing in time".

Core concepts

Sequences, state, and the recurrence

Intuition. Read a sentence one word at a time, and after each word update a summary — a fixed-size vector that stands for "everything read so far, as far as it matters for what comes next". The same update rule every time, applied to a different word and a different summary.

Formal definition. The Elman (simple) recurrent network is

$$ \mathbf{a}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hx}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_h, \qquad \mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{a}_t), \qquad \mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{W}_{yh}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y . $$

Three weight matrices, shared across all $T$ steps. Parameters: $d_x d_h + d_h^2 + d_h + d_h d_y + d_y$. The map from a sequence of length $T$ to an output sequence of length $T$ is a family of functions indexed by $T$, generated by one set of weights — that is what "handles variable-length input" actually means.

Why it is built this way. Any model of a sequence must decide how the past enters the present. A dense layer over a fixed window of the last $k$ tokens costs $O(k)$ parameters per extra token of context and cannot generalize to a longer window than it was trained on. The recurrence makes the state's size independent of the context length: $d_h$ numbers summarize arbitrarily long history. That is an aggressive compression — and it is precisely the assumption that later fails, because a fixed $d_h$-dimensional state cannot store unbounded information about an unbounded past. The information-theoretic statement: with fp32 state, at most $32 d_h$ bits survive from the past, regardless of $T$.

Worked micro-example. $d_x = 3$, $d_h = 2$, $d_y = 3$: $\mathbf{W}_{hx}$ is $2\times3$ = 6, $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ is $2\times2$ = 4, $\mathbf{b}_h$ is 2, $\mathbf{W}_{yh}$ is $3\times2$ = 6, $\mathbf{b}_y$ is 3. Total 21 parameters, and the network accepts sequences of any length. Compare a dense layer over a 10-token window with one-hot inputs: $10\cdot|V|\cdot d$ parameters, which for $|V| = 50{,}000$ and $d = 256$ is 128M.

Unfolding: weight sharing in time

Intuition. Unrolling is not a change of model. It is drawing the same model in a different layout so that the chain rule becomes visible. The unrolled graph is a very deep feedforward network with $T$ layers that all share one weight matrix.

Formal definition. The unrolled computation is a directed acyclic graph with nodes $\mathbf{h}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{h}_T$ and edges $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\to\mathbf{h}_t$, each carrying the same linear map. In reverse-mode automatic differentiation (M07) the backward pass sums, at every shared parameter, the contributions from every node that used it:

$$ \nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\mathcal{L}\Big|_{\text{via step }t} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^\top, $$

where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{a}_t$ is the gradient at the pre-activation.

Why it is built this way. The unrolled view turns a "sequence model" into an ordinary deep network whose depth equals the sequence length. Everything you know about deep networks — vanishing gradients, the need for residual paths, the cost of depth — transfers directly, with the twist that the depth is a data property (how long the sequence is), not an architectural choice. This is also why the pathology is acute: a 500-token sequence is a 500-layer network with one shared weight matrix, and a 500-layer plain network is hopeless.

Worked micro-example. $T = 50$, $d_h = 256$. The unrolled graph has 50 matrix multiplies of size $256\times256$ in the forward pass, giving an effective depth of 50 for gradient purposes. A 50-layer feedforward net with the same width and He initialization has $\|{\partial\mathbf{h}_{50}}/{\partial\mathbf{h}_0}\| \approx \mathcal{O}(1)$ per unit-variance propagation; the recurrent version instead multiplies by the same $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ fifty times, so its behavior is governed by $\rho(\mathbf{W}_{hh})^{50}$.

Backpropagation through time

Intuition. Run forward, keep every state, then walk backward through time sending a gradient vector from step $t+1$ into step $t$ — while also adding the gradient arriving from the output at step $t$. Two sources per node, exactly like the residual stream.

Formal definition. With $\mathbf{g}_t = \partial L_t/\partial\mathbf{o}_t = \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{e}_t$, define $\delta_t = \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \mathbf{h}_t$ (total, including future steps). Then

$$ \delta_t = \mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_t \;+\; \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1-\mathbf{h}_{t+1}^2\big)\,\delta_{t+1}, \qquad \delta_T = \mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_T, $$

run from $t = T$ down to $1$. Then, with $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t = \operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{h}_t^2)\,\delta_t$,

$$ \nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{yh}}\mathcal{L} = \sum_t \mathbf{g}_t\mathbf{h}_t^\top,\quad \nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\mathcal{L} = \sum_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^\top,\quad \nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hx}}\mathcal{L} = \sum_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top,\quad \nabla_{\mathbf{b}_h}\mathcal{L} = \sum_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t . $$

Why it is built this way. Because it is the ordinary reverse-mode gradient on the unrolled graph — there is nothing recurrent about the algorithm, only about the graph. The recursion is what makes it cheap: computing $\delta_t$ from $\delta_{t+1}$ costs one matrix-vector product and one elementwise multiply, so the whole backward pass is $O(T d_h^2)$, the same order as the forward pass.

Worked micro-example. $d_h = 2$, $\mathbf{h}_1 = (0.5, -0.2)$, $\mathbf{h}_2 = (0.8, 0.3)$, $\mathbf{W}_{hh} = \begin{pmatrix}0.1 & 0.4\\ -0.3 & 0.2\end{pmatrix}$, and suppose $\delta_2 = (1.0, 0.5)$.

$$ \operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{h}_2^2) = \operatorname{diag}(0.36, 0.91), \qquad \operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{h}_2^2)\delta_2 = (0.36, 0.455). $$ $$ \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top(0.36, 0.455) = \begin{pmatrix}0.1 & -0.3\\ 0.4 & 0.2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0.36\\ 0.455\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.036 - 0.1365\\ 0.144 + 0.091\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-0.1005\\ 0.235\end{pmatrix}. $$

Add the local term $\mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_1$ to get $\delta_1$. Two things to notice: the tanh derivative shrank the first coordinate by $0.36$, and the matrix multiply mixed the two coordinates — so a gradient that started purely in coordinate 1 can end up mostly in coordinate 2. That mixing is what makes the recurrent Jacobian's eigenvalues, not its individual entries, the thing to reason about.

The gradient product and the spectral radius

Intuition. To learn that token 1 caused the loss at token 50, the gradient must travel 49 steps. At each step it gets multiplied by the recurrent Jacobian. If that Jacobian shrinks vectors on average, the signal is annihilated; if it expands them, the signal explodes. Repeated multiplication is the entire mechanism.

Formal definition. Unroll the recursion:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_s} = \sum_{t\ge s}\mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_t\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_s},\qquad \frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_s} = \prod_{i=s+1}^{t}\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1-\mathbf{h}_i^2\big)\mathbf{W}_{hh}. $$

Taking norms and using submultiplicativity and $\|1-\mathbf{h}_i^2\|_\infty \le \gamma \le 1$:

$$ \left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_s}\right\| \;\le\; \prod_{i=s+1}^{t}\underbrace{\big\|\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{h}_i^2)\big\|\,\big\|\mathbf{W}_{hh}\big\|}_{\le\;\gamma\,\sigma_{\max}} \;\le\; (\gamma\,\sigma_{\max})^{\,t-s}. $$

Since $\sigma_{\max} \ge \rho(\mathbf{W}_{hh})$, a network whose recurrent matrix has spectral radius well below $1/\gamma$ provably cannot propagate a gradient more than a few dozen steps.

Why it is built this way — and why the argument is incomplete. The bound is one-sided and crude: it is a worst case over directions, not the typical behaviour. The quantity that actually governs a random gradient's growth is closer to the spectral radius (for long products, $\|J^T\|^{1/T}\to\rho(J)$ by Gelfand's formula), which is why $\rho(\mathbf{W}_{hh})$ is the usual diagnostic and why orthogonal or identity-initialized recurrent matrices ($\rho = 1$) train noticeably better. Even so, learning an $h_t$ that depends on $h_1$ requires more than a non-vanishing gradient: the network must also store the dependency in a $d_h$-dimensional state, and no initialization changes that. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi made the first argument precise in 1994; the field's subsequent decade of LSTM work is the engineering response.

Worked micro-example. The per-step factor $r = \gamma\sigma_{\max}$ determines everything:

$r$ after 10 steps after 50 steps after 100 steps verdict
$0.9$ $3.5\times10^{-1}$ $5.2\times10^{-3}$ $2.7\times10^{-5}$ slow decay, ~175 steps to lose 8 digits
$0.8$ $1.1\times10^{-1}$ $1.4\times10^{-5}$ $2.0\times10^{-10}$ unusable past ~80 steps
$0.5$ $9.8\times10^{-4}$ $8.9\times10^{-16}$ $7.9\times10^{-31}$ dead past ~27 steps
$1.2$ $38.3$ $9.1\times10^{3}$ $8.3\times10^{7}$ overflow by step 487 in fp32
$1.5$ $3.3\times10^{3}$ $6.4\times10^{8}$ $4.1\times10^{17}$ overflow by step 219
$2.0$ $1.0\times10^{6}$ $1.1\times10^{15}$ $1.3\times10^{30}$ overflow by step 128

fp32 has roughly seven significant decimal digits, so a term that is $10^{-8}$ relative to the largest term in a sum contributes nothing: the practical horizon at $r=0.9$ is under 200 steps, at $r=0.8$ under 100. The right-hand column is the explosion: at $r = 1.5$ you overflow fp32 within a 219-token sequence, which is why unclipped RNN training on long sequences produces nan loss rather than merely slow learning.

The asymmetry that matters. Vanishing is a capability limit — the model cannot learn the dependency at all. Exploding is a stability problem — the model has the information but the step destroys it. The two need different fixes, and conflating them is the most common error in reading about RNNs.

Truncated BPTT and gradient clipping

Intuition. Two different problems, two different instruments. Truncation decides how far back in time you are willing to assign credit. Clipping decides how large a step you are willing to take.

Formal definition — truncation. Split the sequence into chunks of length $k$. Process chunk $m$ with the hidden state carried in from chunk $m-1$, but treat that carried state as a constant (detach it from the graph). Then

$$ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L} \approx \sum_{m}\sum_{t \in \text{chunk } m}\;\sum_{\substack{s \le t\\ t-s \le k}} \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigg|_{\text{path } s\to t} . $$

The exact gradient sums over all $s \le t$; truncation drops every path longer than $k$. What truncation changes, precisely: the terms with $t - s > k$, which are exactly the terms whose magnitude the bound $( \gamma\sigma_{\max})^{t-s}$ says is smallest. That is why the bias is usually tolerable — you are discarding the contributions the model cannot learn from anyway. What it does not change: the forward pass still carries state across chunks, so the model can use arbitrarily long context; it just cannot learn a dependency spanning more than $k$ steps in a single update.

Formal definition — clipping. With global-norm clipping at threshold $c$:

$$ \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \mathbf{g}\cdot\min\!\left(1, \frac{c}{\|\mathbf{g}\|_2}\right). $$

Derivation: clipping is a projection. Consider the problem of finding the vector $\mathbf{v}$ closest to $\mathbf{g}$ subject to $\|\mathbf{v}\| \le c$:

$$ \min_{\mathbf{v}}\;\|\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{g}\|_2^2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \le c . $$

The objective is $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{g}\rangle + \|\mathbf{g}\|^2$, minimized at $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{g}$ when $\|\mathbf{g}\|\le c$ by Cauchy–Schwarz ($\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{g}\rangle \le \|\mathbf{v}\|\|\mathbf{g}\|$, with equality iff $\mathbf{v} \parallel \mathbf{g}$). When $\|\mathbf{g}\| > c$, the constraint is active and the optimum is on the sphere $\|\mathbf{v}\| = c$ with $\mathbf{v}$ parallel to $\mathbf{g}$:

$$ \mathbf{v} = \frac{c}{\|\mathbf{g}\|}\,\mathbf{g} . $$

So clipping is the Euclidean projection onto the ball of radius $c$: it changes magnitude and preserves direction exactly. That is the property worth remembering — clipping never rotates the update, so it cannot silently substitute one descent direction for another.

Why it is built this way. The loss surface of a recurrent network is steep near "cliff" regions: small changes in a parameter produce enormous changes in the loss (Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio documented this in 2013 and showed clipping is what makes RNN training reliable). A step whose norm scales with the gradient's norm will, on a cliff, jump somewhere arbitrary. Clipping makes the step size bounded by $\eta c$ regardless of the terrain, which is a crude but extremely effective form of trust region. Note what it does not do: it is not a fix for vanishing gradients, and it is not equivalent to lowering the learning rate — it lowers the effective rate only on the steps that need it.

Worked micro-example. $\mathbf{g} = (3, 4)$, so $\|\mathbf{g}\| = 5$. With $c = 2$: $\mathbf{g}\leftarrow (3,4)\cdot(2/5) = (1.2, 1.6)$, norm exactly 2, direction unchanged. With $c = 10$: unchanged. With a per-coordinate ("elementwise") clip at $c=2$: $(2, 2)$, norm $2.83$ — direction changed, and the relative weighting of the two coordinates destroyed. This is why global-norm clipping is the default in every serious RNN or transformer training code you will read.

The LSTM: four gates, each derived from a failure

Intuition. Do not overwrite the memory. Add to it, under a learnable valve; let the memory decay under a second valve; and guard the memory from the read-out with a third.

Formal definition. With $[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t]$ denoting concatenation and $\mathbf{W}_f,\mathbf{W}_i,\mathbf{W}_o,\mathbf{W}_g$ the four gate matrices:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_f[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f\big) &&\text{forget gate}\\ \mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_i[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i\big) &&\text{input gate}\\ \mathbf{g}_t &= \tanh\big(\mathbf{W}_g[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_g\big) &&\text{candidate}\\ \mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_o[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o\big) &&\text{output gate}\\ \mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t\odot\mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t &&\text{cell state}\\ \mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t\odot\tanh(\mathbf{c}_t) &&\text{hidden state} \end{aligned}$$

Parameters: $4\big(d_h(d_h + d_x) + d_h\big)$, four times the Elman network's recurrent block.

Why it is built this way — each gate from the failure it fixes.

(a) The input gate fixes the overwrite conflict. In the Elman network, $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \cdots)$ replaces the state every step. There is no way for the network to say "keep what is there". Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's diagnosis was sharper than "overwriting": a single weight set must simultaneously decide what to store and how to protect the store from the current input — the input-weight conflict. The fix is an additive state plus a multiplicative valve on the write:

$$ \mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t . $$

With $\mathbf{i}_t \approx 0$ the state is untouched; with $\mathbf{i}_t \approx 1$ the candidate is written. The valve is learned and content-dependent, so "store this" is a function of the input rather than a property of a frozen weight.

(b) The forget gate fixes unbounded accumulation. A purely additive state never decreases, so $\tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)$ saturates, the gradient through it dies, and the cell can never be reused for a new memory. Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins added the multiplicative forget term in 2000 and called it "learning to forget":

$$ \mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t\odot\mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t . $$

This one term is what turns the cell into a leaky integrator with a learned, per-channel leak — and it is also, as derived below, the term that creates the gradient highway.

(c) The output gate fixes the read-out conflict. If the cell state were exposed directly, $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)$, then every step's loss would demand that $\mathbf{c}_t$ be immediately interpretable, which fights against it being a long-term store. The output gate decouples the two: $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t\odot\tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)$ lets the cell keep a value that is not currently relevant. In the original 1997 formulation the output gate also served the function of protecting the cell from irrelevant, noisy inputs arriving through the output path.

(d) The tanh on the candidate bounds the write, so no single update can push the cell state far. Combined with (a) and (b) the cell is bounded between $-1$ and $1$ in the limit of repeated writes with $\mathbf{i} = 1$ and $\mathbf{f}=0$.

The cell-state highway

Intuition. The cell state is a conveyor belt. The only things that touch it are a per-channel multiply and a per-channel add. Nothing mixes its coordinates, and nothing squashes it. Gradient flowing backward along the belt is scaled but never rotated.

Formal definition and derivation. Differentiate $\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t\odot\mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t$ with respect to $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$. Both $\mathbf{f}_t$ and $\mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t$ depend on $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ through $\mathbf{h}_{t-1} = \mathbf{o}_{t-1}\odot\tanh(\mathbf{c}_{t-1})$, so

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{c}_t}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}} = \underbrace{\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t)}_{\text{direct path}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}}\mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \frac{\partial(\mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t)}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}}}_{\text{indirect paths, through the gates}} . $$

The indirect terms are usually small (they pass through a sigmoid derivative, bounded by $0.25$, and a $\tanh$ derivative, bounded by $1$). Keeping only the direct path:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{s}} \;\approx\; \left(\prod_{t=s+1}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t)\right)\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{T}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_s}\right\| \approx \left(\prod_{t=s+1}^{T} f_t\right)\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_T}\right\| . $$

Three structural differences from the Elman network, and they are the whole point.

  1. No matrix on the path. The product is of diagonal matrices, so coordinate $j$ of the gradient is scaled by $\prod_t f_{t,j}$ — there is no mixing, hence no rotation toward a small singular direction. In an Elman network the product of full matrices mixes coordinates and it is the smallest singular value that decides survival.
  2. The factors are learned per channel. $f_{t,j} = \sigma(\cdot)\in(0,1)$ and the network can drive it to $1$ for a channel it wants to preserve. A weight matrix cannot do this per-input; a gate can.
  3. The default can be "remember". Initializing the forget bias to $1$ or $2$ (so $\mathbf{f}\approx0.73$ or $0.88$ at step 0) is a standard trick for exactly this reason: it starts the cell as an integrator rather than a forgetter.

The honest caveat. This is a path, not a guarantee. If the task drives $\mathbf{f}\to 0$ (which it will, whenever the past is irrelevant), the path dies — the product is $\prod f_t$, and $0.9^{50} = 5.2\times10^{-3}$. The LSTM did not abolish vanishing gradients; it gave the network a learnable dial on how much of the past to keep, which is a far better place to be than a fixed matrix product, but it is not immunity. Empirically, vanilla LSTMs learn dependencies reliably to a few hundred steps, not to tens of thousands.

Worked micro-example. One channel, $f = 0.99$ every step: after 100 steps the gradient factor is $0.366$; with $f=0.95$, $5.9\times10^{-3}$; with $f=0.9$, $2.66\times10^{-5}$. For comparison the Elman network with $r = 0.9$ has the same $2.66\times10^{-5}$ after 100 steps — so a well-tuned LSTM whose forget gate sits at $0.99$ is ahead by four orders of magnitude, and an LSTM whose forget gate has collapsed to $0.9$ is no better than a well-tuned Elman net. The gate is doing the work, not the architecture name.

The GRU

Intuition. Two gates instead of three, and the update gate does double duty: it is both "how much of the new candidate to write" and "how much of the old state to keep", forced to sum to one.

Formal definition.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_z[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t]\big) &&\text{update gate}\\ \mathbf{r}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_r[\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t]\big) &&\text{reset gate}\\ \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t &= \tanh\big(\mathbf{W}_h[\mathbf{r}_t\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t]\big) &&\text{candidate}\\ \mathbf{h}_t &= (1-\mathbf{z}_t)\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t &&\text{state} \end{aligned}$$

Why it is built this way. Three economy arguments. (i) The LSTM's forget and input gates are complementary in a well-trained cell ($f \approx 1-i$), because a cell cannot both keep and overwrite the same coordinate; the GRU hard-wires $f = 1-z$ instead of learning it. (ii) There is no separate output gate and no cell state: $\mathbf{h}_t$ is the memory, so the read-out conflict is not resolved but eliminated by removing the separate exposed variable. (iii) The reset gate is applied to the previous state before the candidate is computed, letting the cell forget its own history while still writing — a capability the LSTM gets from having two states.

Parameter count: $3(d_h(d_h+d_x) + d_h)$, which is $0.75\times$ the LSTM's. With $d_x = d_h = 256$: LSTM $= 4\cdot(256\cdot512 + 256) = 525{,}312$; GRU $= 393{,}984$.

Worked micro-example. $\mathbf{z}_t = 0.25$, $\mathbf{h}_{t-1} = (2, -1)$, $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = (0.5, 0.5)$. Then $\mathbf{h}_t = 0.75\cdot(2,-1) + 0.25\cdot(0.5,0.5) = (1.5 + 0.125,\; -0.75 + 0.125) = (1.625, -0.625)$. The gradient path is $\partial\mathbf{h}_t/\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{z}_t) + \ldots = \operatorname{diag}(0.75) + \ldots$: a diagonal leaky-integrator path, exactly like the LSTM's forget path with $f = 1-z$.

Skip and highway connections: the bridge to residuals

Intuition. If the problem is that the signal has to pass through many multiplicative stages, give it a bypass that multiplies by nothing.

Formal definition. A highway layer (Srivastava, Greff and Schmidhuber, 2015) is

$$ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{T}\odot H(\mathbf{x}) + \big(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{T}\big)\odot\mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{T} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_T\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_T), $$

with $\mathbf{T}$ the transform gate. Set $\mathbf{T} \equiv \mathbf{1}$ and the parameters of the gate vanish:

$$ \mathbf{y} = H(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}, $$

which is the residual connection of M10 — and, one dimension over, the transformer block of M12.

Why it is built this way. The gated version lets the network choose, per unit and per input, whether to transform or to pass through. The ungated version removes even that choice, on the observation that a gate that can close is a gate that can close badly: if $\mathbf{T}\to0$ the layer is a pure skip and the parameters below receive no gradient through it. Fixing $\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{1}$ guarantees the identity term is present unconditionally, which is exactly the singular-value bound derived in M10. The residual connection is the highway network with the gate deleted because the gate is not worth its failure modes.

Worked micro-example. Scalar, $H(x) = 0.5x$, $T = 0.9$: $y = 0.9\cdot0.5x + 0.1x = 0.55x$. Gradient $\partial y/\partial x = 0.55$. With $T = 1$ (residual): $y = 1.5x$, $\partial y/\partial x = 1.5$, and the identity contributes $1$ regardless of what $H$ does. With $T = 0.1$: $y = 0.05x + 0.9x = 0.95x$, gradient $0.95$ — but the path through $H$ is now weighted by $0.1$ in the forward and its gradient contribution is scaled by $0.1$, so the transform branch learns $10\times$ slower. A gate that can close is a gate that can starve.

Teacher forcing and exposure bias

Intuition. In training you feed the model the true previous token; at generation you must feed it its own. The model is graded on a distribution it never practised on.

Formal definition. Training minimizes

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{TF}} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(y_t \mid y_{

conditioning on the ground-truth prefix $y^{\star}_{

$$ \hat{y}_t \sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\cdot \mid \hat{y}_{

conditioning on the model's own prefix. The two distributions over prefixes coincide only if the model is perfect. This mismatch is exposure bias.

Derivation of the compounding. Suppose that at each step the model deviates from the ground-truth prefix with probability $\epsilon$, independently across steps. The prefix fed at step $t$ is on-distribution only if no earlier step deviated, so

$$ P(\text{prefix at step } t \text{ is on-distribution}) = (1-\epsilon)^{t-1}, $$

which decays geometrically. Concretely, at $\epsilon = 0.05$: 60% of prefixes are on-distribution after 10 steps, 36% after 20, 8% after 50. Because the deviation is not independent — an error tends to make further errors more likely — the real decay is faster.

Why it is built this way, and the honest counter-argument. Teacher forcing makes the training problem a set of independent per-step classifications, which is why it converges so reliably; training on self-generated prefixes is unstable early (the model's samples are garbage, so the gradient teaches it to model garbage) and the usual fix is a curriculum — scheduled sampling anneals the probability of using a model-generated token from 0 to 1. But the standard contemporary view, argued carefully by Holtzman et al. (2019), is that exposure bias is not the main reason generated text is worse than its likelihood suggests. The larger effect is that maximizing likelihood trains a model to assign mass to every plausible continuation, including dull ones, and that decoding by likelihood therefore produces degenerate repetition. The contested part: how much of generation quality is exposure bias versus objective mismatch. Both effects are real; the field now mostly addresses the second (with preference tuning, M18) and lives with the first.

Worked micro-example. Vocabulary of 4, true sequence $(a,b,c,d)$, and a model that assigns probability $0.9$ to the correct token given a correct prefix but $0.4$ given a corrupted prefix. Teacher-forced loss per step: $-\log 0.9 = 0.105$, so the sequence loss is $0.421$. With one early error at step 2, the remaining three steps are scored at $0.4$: the loss becomes $0.105 + 3\cdot0.916 = 2.85$, a $6.8\times$ larger gradient — for a single mistake. Teacher forcing is a training signal that systematically under-weights the cost of the errors a model actually makes at inference.

Sequence-to-sequence and the encoder-decoder bottleneck

Intuition. Read the whole source into one summary, then write the target from that summary. The summary is the bottleneck.

Formal definition. An encoder RNN consumes $\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_S$ and produces a context vector — usually the final state, $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{h}^{\text{enc}}_S$. A decoder RNN is initialized from $\mathbf{c}$ (or has it concatenated at every step) and generates the target:

$$ p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T}p\big(y_t \mid y_{

Why it is built this way. It is the minimal architecture that maps variable-length input to variable-length output, and it works — the only structural weakness is that $\mathbf{c}$ has fixed size. The encoder is being asked to produce a sufficient statistic for the entire source in $d_h$ floats. No fixed-size vector is a sufficient statistic for unbounded input, so the architecture must decide during encoding which source information to throw away, before knowing what the decoder will need. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014) diagnosed exactly this and replaced the single vector with a per-step weighted average over all encoder states — attention — which is the subject of M12. Their paper's empirical signature is the diagnostic to remember: translation quality degraded sharply with source length for the fixed-vector model and flattened out with attention.

Worked micro-example. $d_h = 1{,}024$ in fp32 is 4,096 bytes = 32,768 bits of context. A 60-token sentence whose tokens carry, say, 8 bits of unpredictable information each needs ~480 bits of content — well inside 32,768 — so the bottleneck is not raw capacity. It is that the encoder has no idea which 480 bits the decoder will need, and the fixed vector has no addressing mechanism to store them at a known location. Attention is not primarily a capacity increase; it is the addition of addresses.

Beam search

Intuition. Greedy decoding commits to the locally best token and never reconsiders. Beam search keeps $k$ partial hypotheses alive and re-ranks them as evidence accumulates.

Formal definition. A hypothesis is a sequence $y_{1:t}$ with score $S(y_{1:t}) = \sum_{i=1}^{t}\log p(y_i \mid y_{

Why it is built this way — and why it is not MAP decoding. The exact $\arg\max_{\mathbf{y}} p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})$ requires enumerating $|V|^T$ sequences. Beam search is a heuristic: it is exact only in the limit $k \to |V|^T$, and it can prune a prefix that leads to the globally best sequence because that prefix looked mediocre at step 3. Its cost is $O(k\,|V|\,T)$ scored extensions — for $k=5$, $|V|=50{,}257$, $T=30$ that is 7.5M token scorings, against a search space of $50{,}257^{30}\approx10^{141}$.

Two further problems, both worth knowing. Length bias: sums of log-probabilities are monotonically decreasing in length, so the objective favours short sequences; the standard correction divides by $t^{\alpha}$ (length normalization) or by a length reward. Objective mismatch: maximizing likelihood is not the same as producing good text, so a larger beam can make output worse — more degenerate, more repetitive — which is the empirical finding behind the move to sampling and to preference-tuned objectives.

Worked micro-example. $k=2$, vocabulary $\{a,b\}$, $p(a\mid\text{start}) = 0.6$, $p(b\mid\text{start}) = 0.4$. Step 2: $p(a\mid a) = 0.1$, $p(b\mid a) = 0.9$; $p(a\mid b) = 0.8$, $p(b\mid b) = 0.2$. Scores: $aa: -0.51-2.30 = -2.81$; $ab: -0.51-0.11 = -0.62$; $ba: -0.92-0.22 = -1.14$; $bb: -0.92-1.61 = -2.53$. Beam keeps $\{ab, ba\}$; greedy keeps only $\{aa\}$ and never sees the $0.9$ branch. With $k=1$ you would have prefixed on the wrong token from step 1.

Post-mortem: why recurrence lost to attention

Intuition. The recurrent network has to read the sequence in order, and it has to compress everything it has read into one vector. Attention does neither: it looks at every position at once and it never compresses.

Three precise claims.

(1) Path length. The gradient path from the loss at output position $t$ to the input at position $s$ has length $|t-s|$ in a recurrent network and length $1$ in a self-attention layer (both positions are one edge from the pairwise score). By the bound above, the recurrent path is multiplied by $|t-s|$ Jacobians; the attention path is not multiplied at all in that way — a query at $t$ reads key $s$ directly through a dot product. Everything about long-range dependency learning follows from this one structural difference.

(2) Parallelism. A recurrent network cannot compute $\mathbf{h}_t$ without $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. Training over a sequence of length $T$ therefore requires $T$ sequential kernel launches, each a matrix-vector product of size $d_h\times d_h$ — an operation with $O(d_h^2)$ arithmetic and $O(d_h^2)$ memory traffic, i.e. arithmetic intensity about 1, which on a GPU runs at a small fraction of peak. Self-attention training over the same sequence is a handful of $T\times T$ and $T\times d$ matrix–matrix products, all parallel over positions, all high-intensity. The measured consequence: transformers use the hardware far better per token of sequence processed, which is why the same wall-clock budget buys many more tokens. This is the argument that decided the field, and it is about hardware utilization, not expressiveness.

(3) Content-based addressing. Attention's weights are computed from the data ($\alpha_{ts}\propto\exp\langle\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s\rangle$), so the model chooses where to read for each query. A recurrent state has no address space: it cannot say "retrieve the fourth token". This is the capability that makes in-context learning and copying behaviour possible at all.

What recurrence kept. A fixed-size state means $O(1)$ memory and $O(1)$ compute per token regardless of context length, and no growth of inference cost with history. That property is real, and it is why the field has not abandoned recurrence but re-derived it: linear attention and state-space models (M13) are exactly "recurrence with a matrix-valued state and parallel-scan training", and hybrid stacks keep a minority of attention layers for their exact recall.

The crossover is where the quadratic term starts to dominate: $n^2d$ beats $nd^2$ when $n < d$ roughly, so for $d=4096$ attention is cheaper than the projections up to about 4,000 tokens — and beyond that the quadratic wall arrives, which is the subject of M13.

The algorithm, step by step

Training a recurrent network with truncated BPTT on chunks of length $k$, batch size $B$.

  1. Initialize state. $\mathbf{H}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times d_h}$, zeros (or the detached final state of the previous chunk when streaming).
  2. Forward within the chunk. For $t = 1..k$: $\mathbf{A}_t = \mathbf{X}_t\mathbf{W}_{hx}^\top + \mathbf{H}_{t-1}\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top + \mathbf{b}_h$, then $\mathbf{H}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{A}_t)$, then logits $\mathbf{O}_t = \mathbf{H}_t\mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top+\mathbf{b}_y$. Store $\mathbf{A}_t$ and $\mathbf{H}_t$ (needed by the backward pass).
  3. Loss. $\mathbf{G}_t = \partial L_t/\partial\mathbf{O}_t = \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_t - \mathbf{Y}_t$ (one-hot targets), and $L = \frac{1}{Bk}\sum_t\sum_b L_{t,b}$.
  4. Seed the backward pass. $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_k = \mathbf{G}_k\mathbf{W}_{yh}$ (the $\partial L_k/\partial\mathbf{h}_k$ term). Accumulate $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{yh}} \mathrel{+}= \mathbf{G}_k^\top\mathbf{H}_k$ and $\nabla_{\mathbf{b}_y}\mathrel{+}= \sum_b\mathbf{G}_k$.
  5. Walk backward. For $t = k-1$ down to $1$: $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t = \mathbf{G}_t\mathbf{W}_{yh} + \big(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t+1}\odot(1-\mathbf{H}_{t+1}^2)\big)\mathbf{W}_{hh}$. The second term is the message from the future; the first is the local loss at step $t$.
  6. Accumulate parameter gradients. With $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t = \boldsymbol{\Delta}_t\odot(1-\mathbf{H}_t^2)$: $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\mathrel{+}= \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t^\top\mathbf{H}_{t-1}$, $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hx}}\mathrel{+}= \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t^\top\mathbf{X}_t$, $\nabla_{\mathbf{b}_h}\mathrel{+}= \sum_b\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$. Note $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ is used for all three — compute it once.
  7. Detach and advance. $\mathbf{H}_k$ becomes the carry-in for the next chunk with .detach(): the graph is severed, so no gradient crosses a chunk boundary.
  8. Clip and step. $\|\nabla\| \leftarrow \min(1, c/\|\nabla\|)$; optimizer step.

```text function train_step(chunks, model, c, eta): H = zeros(B, dh) for X, Y in chunks: # X: (B, k, dx), Y: (B, k) A, Hh, O, H_last = forward(X, H) # store every intermediate L, G = softmax_ce(O, Y) # G: (B, k, dy) dW = zeros_like(all parameters) Delta = G[:, -1] @ W_yh # seed at the last step dW_yh += G[:, -1].T @ Hh[:, -1] for t in reversed(range(k)): if t < k - 1: Delta = G[:, t] @ W_yh \ + (Delta * (1 - Hh[:, t+1]**2)) @ W_hh eps = Delta * (1 - Hh[:, t]**2) dW_hh += eps.T @ (H if t == 0 else Hh[:, t-1]) dW_hx += eps.T @ X[:, t] dW_bh += eps.sum(0) dW_yh += einsum over all t # do this in one batched pass g = concat(all dW); g *= min(1, c / norm(g)) params -= eta * g H = H_last.detach() # truncation: no gradient crosses ```

Complexity. Forward: $O(Bk(d_x d_h + d_h^2 + d_h d_y))$ multiply-accumulates, of which $O(Bk\,d_h^2)$ is the recurrent term. Backward: the same order, with a constant factor near 2 for the extra matrix products. Memory: $O(Bk\,d_h)$ for the stored states and pre-activations — and this is the quantity truncation controls, since without it you must keep every state of the full sequence. With $B=32$, $k=100$, $d_h=1024$: $3.4\times10^{6}$ floats per stored tensor, 13 MB in fp32; four such tensors plus the graph overhead is why a 500-step sequence at these sizes does not fit.

Derivations

1. BPTT from the unrolled graph, with the transposes in the right place

Set-up. Batch size 1 to keep the notation clean. Loss $L = \sum_t L_t$, with $L_t$ depending on $\mathbf{o}_t$ only. Define the per-step output gradient

$$ \mathbf{g}_t \equiv \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial\mathbf{o}_t} = \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{e}_t \in\mathbb{R}^{d_y}. $$

Step 1: the output layer. $\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{W}_{yh}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y$ gives

$$ \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}\bigg|_{\text{direct}} = \mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{yh}} = \mathbf{g}_t\mathbf{h}_t^\top . $$

Step 2: the total derivative. $\mathbf{h}_t$ influences the loss through $\mathbf{o}_t$ and through every future state. So define

$$ \delta_t \equiv \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t} = \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t} + \frac{\partial L_{>t}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}, $$

where $L_{>t} = \sum_{k>t}L_k$. Since $\mathbf{h}_t$ enters $L_{>t}$ only via $\mathbf{h}_{t+1}$,

$$ \frac{\partial L_{>t}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t} = \left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}\right)^{\!\top}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}} = \left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}\right)^{\!\top}\delta_{t+1}. $$

Note the transpose: the Jacobian is $d_h\times d_h$ and multiplies the gradient on its left, i.e. as $\mathbf{J}^\top$. Getting this wrong produces a code that runs and trains but with gradients that are wrong in a way finite-difference checking catches immediately.

Step 3: the recurrent Jacobian. With $\mathbf{h}_{t+1} = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_t + \cdots)$,

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t} = \operatorname{diag}\!\big(1 - \mathbf{h}_{t+1}^2\big)\mathbf{W}_{hh} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}\right)^{\!\top} = \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1-\mathbf{h}_{t+1}^2\big). $$

(The diagonal is symmetric, so it is its own transpose; it sits between the two matrices and stays put.)

Step 4: the recursion. Combining,

$$ \boxed{\;\delta_t = \mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1-\mathbf{h}_{t+1}^2\big)\delta_{t+1},\qquad \delta_T = \mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_T\;} $$

Step 5: the parameter gradients. For $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, the pre-activation is $\mathbf{a}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\cdots$, and $\partial L/\partial\mathbf{a}_t = \partial L/\partial\mathbf{h}_t \odot (1-\mathbf{h}_t^2) \equiv \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ by the chain rule through the elementwise tanh. Then

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial(\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ij}} = \sum_t \frac{\partial L}{\partial(\mathbf{a}_t)_i}\frac{\partial(\mathbf{a}_t)_i}{\partial(\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ij}} = \sum_t \epsilon_{t,i}\,h_{t-1,j} \;\Longrightarrow\; \nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}L = \sum_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^\top . $$

Identically $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hx}}L = \sum_t\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top$, $\nabla_{\mathbf{b}_h}L = \sum_t\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$, and $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{yh}}L = \sum_t\mathbf{g}_t\mathbf{h}_t^\top$. Each is an outer product summed over time — which is why weight sharing in time costs nothing extra: it just converts $T$ separate gradient matrices into their sum.

2. Sum over paths, the spectral radius, and Gelfand's formula

Step 1: expand the recursion. Applying Step 4 of the previous derivation repeatedly gives the sum-over-paths form

$$ \delta_s = \sum_{t\ge s}\left(\prod_{i=s+1}^{t}\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1-\mathbf{h}_i^2\big)\right)\mathbf{W}_{yh}^\top\mathbf{g}_t . $$

Every term in the sum is the gradient contributed by one output time via one path back to $s$; the product is the path's Jacobian. This is the precise sense in which "the gradient has to travel through time".

Step 2: bound each factor.

$$ \big\|\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{h}_i^2)\big\|_2 \;\le\; \|\mathbf{W}_{hh}\|_2\,\|1-\mathbf{h}_i^2\|_\infty \;=\; \sigma_{\max}\,\gamma_i \;\le\; \sigma_{\max}\gamma, $$

using $\|\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d})\|_2 = \max_i|d_i|$ and $\|\mathbf{A}^\top\| = \|\mathbf{A}\|$.

Step 3: bound the product. Submultiplicativity gives

$$ \left\|\prod_{i=s+1}^{t}\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{h}_i^2)\right\| \le (\sigma_{\max}\gamma)^{t-s}. $$

Step 4: why the spectral radius, not $\sigma_{\max}$, is the right diagnostic for long products. For a fixed matrix $\mathbf{M}$, Gelfand's formula states

$$ \rho(\mathbf{M}) = \lim_{m\to\infty}\|\mathbf{M}^m\|^{1/m}, $$

so for large $m$, $\|\mathbf{M}^m\| \approx \rho(\mathbf{M})^m$ up to polynomial factors in $m$ — even when $\rho < \sigma_{\max}$, because the non-normal part of $\mathbf{M}$ (transient growth along nearly-parallel eigenvectors) dies out relative to the dominant eigencomponent. Since the dominant eigenvector of a product of the same matrix $T$ times is just the dominant eigenvector, the long-range behaviour of the gradient is governed by the eigenvalue largest in modulus. Setting $\rho(\mathbf{W}_{hh}) = 1$ with an orthogonal $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ (so all singular values are 1 and $\gamma$ is the only source of decay) is the orthogonal initialization trick: it removes the matrix factor from the bound and leaves only the tanh saturation.

Step 5: what the bound is and is not. It is a uniform upper bound on the spectral norm of every path Jacobian. It is not a statement about the gradient of any particular parameter: the parameter gradient sums many paths of different lengths with different signs, so cancellation can make the sum far smaller than the bound. Diagnostically, the quantity to watch during training is the empirical distribution of $\|\delta_t\|$ across $t$: if it decays geometrically with $t$ at a rate near $\gamma\sigma_{\max}$, the bound is tight and long-range learning is impossible; if it is flat, credit is reaching back and the model is fine.

3. The LSTM Jacobian in full, and where the highway comes from

Step 1: write out the cell recursion and its inputs. For step $t$, with $\mathbf{u}_t = [\mathbf{h}_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_t]$:

$$ \mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t\odot\mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \mathbf{f}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{b}_f),\ \ \mathbf{i}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i\mathbf{u}_t+\mathbf{b}_i), $$ $$ \mathbf{h}_{t-1} = \mathbf{o}_{t-1}\odot\tanh(\mathbf{c}_{t-1}). $$

Step 2: differentiate. Treating $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ as the variable and noting that $\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{i}_t,\mathbf{g}_t$ depend on it only through $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{c}_t}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t) + \frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{c}_{t-1}) + \frac{\partial(\mathbf{i}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t)}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}} . $$

Step 3: bound the indirect terms. With $\mathbf{W}_{f\cdot}$ the block of $\mathbf{W}_f$ multiplying $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}} = \operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbf{f}_t\odot(1-\mathbf{f}_t)\big)\mathbf{W}_{f\cdot}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}}{\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}} = \operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbf{o}_{t-1}\big)\operatorname{diag}\big(1-\tanh^2\mathbf{c}_{t-1}\big). $$

Both carry a gate-derivative factor bounded by $0.25$ (sigmoid) times $\|\mathbf{W}\|$ and a tanh factor bounded by $1$. In a trained cell, $\mathbf{i}_t$ and $\mathbf{o}_t$ saturate toward $0$ or $1$ for most units most of the time, which drives those products toward zero — but "most of the time" is exactly why this is an argument about typical behaviour and not a proof.

Step 4: the dominant path. Retaining only the first term and multiplying over a span:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{c}_T}{\partial\mathbf{c}_s} \;\approx\; \prod_{t=s+1}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t) \;=\; \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\prod_{t=s+1}^{T}\mathbf{f}_t\right), $$

where the last equality holds because diagonal matrices commute. This is the constant error carousel: a purely diagonal, commutative product with no mixing of coordinates. For coordinate $j$ the gradient factor is $\prod_t f_{t,j}$, a product of learned scalars in $(0,1)$ rather than a product of a matrix's singular values.

Step 5: why the difference is structural, not quantitative. In the Elman network the path factor is a product of full matrices; its norm is bounded by the product of singular values, and any single step where the relevant direction is contracted destroys that direction forever, because later steps mix coordinates and cannot recover a coordinate that has been annihilated. In the LSTM the coordinates never mix along the highway, so a coordinate survives exactly when its own forget gate stays high — an independent, per-coordinate, learnable decision. That is the entire content of "the LSTM solves the vanishing gradient problem", correctly stated: it changes the geometry of the gradient path from "product of matrices" to "product of scalars".

4. The GRU is an LSTM with the input gate tied to the forget gate

Step 1: impose the tie. In the LSTM, set $\mathbf{f}_t = 1-\mathbf{z}_t$ and $\mathbf{i}_t = \mathbf{z}_t$:

$$ \mathbf{c}_t = (1-\mathbf{z}_t)\odot\mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t . $$

Step 2: remove the separate exposed state. If $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{c}_t$ (no output gate, no tanh on read-out), then

$$ \mathbf{h}_t = (1-\mathbf{z}_t)\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t, $$

which is the GRU update with $\mathbf{z}$ playing the role of the LSTM's input gate. This is a convex combination — the state is always an interpolation between the old state and the new candidate, so it lies in the convex hull of past candidates and cannot grow without bound.

Step 3: the reset gate has no LSTM counterpart. The GRU also computes the candidate from $\mathbf{r}_t\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ rather than $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. Setting $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}$ makes the candidate depend only on $\mathbf{x}_t$, i.e. it lets the cell write a value that is independent of its history — an LSTM can approximate this only by driving $\mathbf{i}$ up and $\mathbf{f}$ down simultaneously, which the GRU gets for free. That, not the parameter savings, is the architectural argument for the GRU.

Step 4: the gradient consequence. $\partial\mathbf{h}_t/\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ contains $\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf{z}_t)$, a diagonal highway identical in form to the LSTM's $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t)$. The two architectures therefore sit on the same gradient geometry; the difference is the parameter count ($0.75\times$) and the convex-combination constraint, which is a mild regularizer.

Common misconceptions

"The LSTM solved the vanishing gradient problem."

It replaced a product of full matrices with a product of learned per-coordinate scalars. That is a change of geometry, not a cure. If the task drives $\mathbf{f}_t \to 0.9$, the 50-step factor is $5.2\times10^{-3}$ and the cell is no better than a well-tuned Elman network. The correct statement is: the LSTM makes the decay learnable and per-channel, so a cell that needs to remember can choose to. Empirically that extends the reliable horizon from tens of steps to hundreds — not to thousands, and certainly not to unbounded context.

"RNNs are slow because of vanishing gradients."

Vanishing gradients are a learning pathology; the slowness is a hardware pathology and they are independent. A recurrent layer's inner loop is $T$ sequential matrix–vector products of size $d_h\times d_h$: arithmetic intensity around 1 FLOP/byte, which leaves a GPU's tensor cores idle. You could fix the gradients perfectly (orthogonal $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, gated units, no saturation at all) and the layer would still be $T$ sequential kernel launches. This conflation is why "the LSTM failed because of gradients" survives as folklore; the actual cause of the transformer's win was parallelism.

"RNNs are less expressive than transformers."

Both are universal approximators of sequence-to-sequence functions under standard assumptions, and a transformer with $n$ layers can be simulated by a recurrent network with adequate width and depth (and vice versa, with more layers). The difference is not in the function class but in three cost dimensions: gradient path length, available parallelism, and content-based addressing. Attention cannot represent anything a recurrence provably cannot; it can reach those functions in far fewer optimization steps and far less wall-clock time.

"Beam search finds the most likely output sequence."

It finds the best sequence among those whose prefixes stayed in the top $k$, which is not the global MAP sequence and can miss it badly. A prefix that looks poor at step 2 may be the only route to the best full sequence, and beam search prunes it permanently. On top of that, the maximum-likelihood sequence is often not the best output — for open-ended generation, higher beams frequently produce more repetitive text, which is why the field moved toward sampling with top-p and toward preference-tuned objectives.

"The gates mean what they are named."

An LSTM's forget gate does not reliably "forget" and its input gate does not reliably "input" in any human-legible sense. Probing studies find units whose behaviour matches the names, units whose behaviour is the opposite, and many that are uninterpretable; the names are mnemonic labels for positions in an equation, adopted because they were the failures the authors were fixing. Treat "this gate is open so the model is remembering" as a hypothesis, not a fact.

"Truncated BPTT means the model cannot use long context."

Truncation cuts the gradient at chunk boundaries; the forward state still carries across them. So a model trained with $k=100$ truncation can condition on a token 5,000 steps back at inference time — it simply never received a gradient that credited that token for the loss. The precise consequence: the forward context is unbounded, the learnable credit-assignment horizon is $k$. In practice models learn to use long context only within roughly the truncation window plus whatever incidental structure generalizes.

9. Extend the NumPy implementation to a full LSTM and gradient-check $\mathbf{W}_f$. What is the most likely place to get the transpose wrong, and how would you detect it?

Beam search keeps only the top-$k$ prefixes and pruning is irreversible, so a prefix that leads to the globally best sequence can be discarded early; exactness requires $k\to|V|^T$. Length bias is corrected by dividing the score by $t^{\alpha}$ (length normalization) or adding a length reward. 8. Path length $O(n)$ vs $O(1)$, hardware parallelism ($n$ sequential matrix–vector products vs parallel GEMMs), and content-based addressing. None is a statement about expressiveness: the function classes overlap.

Further reading

  • Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997), "Long Short-Term Memory", Neural Computation 9(8). Extract §3, the constant error carousel argument — it is the original derivation of the gradient highway, and it is written in exactly the terms this module uses.
  • Gers, Schmidhuber & Cummins (2000), "Learning to Forget: Continual Prediction with LSTM", Neural Computation 12(10). Extract the forget gate's motivation and their demonstration that without it the cell saturates on long sequences. The clearest example in the literature of a gate added to fix a specific, named failure.
  • Bengio, Simard & Frasconi (1994), "Learning Long-Term Dependencies with Gradient Descent is Difficult", IEEE TNN 5(2), and Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013), "On the Difficulty of Training Recurrent Neural Networks", ICML. Read them as a pair: the 1994 paper made "vanishing gradients" a formal claim with a spectral-radius argument, and the 2013 paper added the gradient-norm bound and §4's clipping rule, which is where "cliffs" and global-norm clipping come from.
  • Cho, van Merrienboer, Gulcehre, Bahdanau, Bougares, Schwenk & Bengio (2014), "Learning Phrase Representations using RNN Encoder–Decoder for Statistical Machine Translation", EMNLP. The GRU and the encoder-decoder framing in one paper.
  • Sutskever, Vinyals & Le (2014), "Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks", NeurIPS. Extract §3 on beam search and on reversing the source sequence — an architecturally arbitrary trick that measurably helped, and a good reminder of how much of the era's progress was empirical.
  • Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015), "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate", ICLR. Extract Figure 2 and the BLEU-versus-length plot: the bottleneck critique is empirical, not rhetorical, and this is where it was made.
  • Olah, "Understanding LSTM Networks" (colah's blog, free). The best-known visual explanation of the cell; use it to reinforce the gate diagram, and notice that it presents the gates as a story rather than as a derivation — this module is the derivation.
  • Holtzman, Buys, Du, Forbes & Choi (2019), "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration", ICLR. Extract the argument that likelihood maximization plus likelihood-based decoding, not exposure bias, is the main reason generated text degenerates.
