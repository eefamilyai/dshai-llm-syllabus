Preference Learning: RLHF, DPO, and Reasoning RL

From human comparisons to a policy, and what each shortcut to that policy costs.

on this page

Why this module exists

Supervised fine-tuning tells a model what to say. It cannot tell it which of two acceptable answers is better, and it has no mechanism for improving past the quality of its demonstrations. Preference learning is that mechanism, and it is the stage that turned instruction-tuned models into the systems people actually use. It is also the stage where the mathematics is most often recited rather than understood: DPO is routinely described as "RLHF without the reward model" by people who cannot derive it, and GRPO as "PPO without the critic" by people who cannot say what replaces the critic or why that is legitimate. This module derives all of it — the Bradley–Terry model, the KL-regularized optimum, PPO's clipping, the exact DPO reparameterization, and GRPO's group-relative advantage — and then states plainly what each method cannot do, because the failure modes here are where deployed systems actually break.

Core concepts

The alignment problem as a learning problem

(1) Intuition. You cannot write down the loss for "be helpful". But you can show two answers to the same question to a person and ask which is better, and that comparison is a training signal. Everything in this module is a way of converting comparisons into a gradient.

(2) Formal definition. The problem: find $\pi$ maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi}[R^{\star}(x,y)]$ where $R^{\star}$ is a human preference functional you cannot evaluate. You have access only to samples: comparisons $(x, y_w, y_l)$, or scores, or a verifier. The field's structure follows from that asymmetry.

(3) Why it is built this way. Two-stage decomposition: first learn a model of $R^{\star}$ from comparisons (the reward model), then optimize the policy against the learned model with a penalty keeping it close to the reference. The penalty exists because the learned model is wrong off-distribution, so you want to optimize it only where it is trustworthy — a trust region expressed as a KL divergence.

(4) Worked micro-example. 100,000 comparisons at 2 seconds of annotator time each is 55 annotator-hours. Every method here can be run on that. The bottleneck is never the algorithm; it is that 100,000 comparisons cover a vanishingly small part of the input space, which is why the trust region and the reward-hacking analysis matter more than the optimizer choice.

Reward modeling: Bradley–Terry

(1) Intuition. Assume each response has a latent score, and that the probability a person prefers $y_w$ to $y_l$ depends only on the difference of their scores, through a logistic curve. Fit the scores.

(2) Formal definition. Bradley–Terry assumes $P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) = \sigma(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l))$. Given a dataset of comparisons, fit $r_\phi$ by minimizing

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\right)\right]$$

(3) Why it is built this way. The logistic link is the natural choice for a binary outcome with a latent additive score, and it makes the loss a smooth surrogate for pairwise ranking accuracy. It is also the only link for which the KL-regularized RLHF problem has a closed-form solution, which is what makes DPO possible — the entire DPO derivation depends on this choice, a point that its critiques correctly identify.

(4) Worked micro-example. Scores $r_w = 2.0$, $r_l = 1.2$. Then $\Delta = 0.8$, $\sigma(0.8) = 0.690$, so the model believes the preferred response wins 69% of the time and the loss is $-\log 0.690 = 0.371$. If the annotators agreed 95% of the time, the loss should be $-\log 0.95 = 0.051$, so $\Delta$ needs to be about $\ln(0.95/0.05) = 2.94$. The loss is a calibration objective, not a ranking objective, and that distinction matters when labels are noisy.

What a reward model cannot capture

(1) Intuition. The reward model is a small model trained on a small sample of human judgments. It is a proxy, and optimizing hard against a proxy finds its errors, not your preferences.

(2) Formal definition. Four distinct failure mechanisms, worth keeping separate because the fixes differ:

Proxy error. $r_\phi \ne R^{\star}$ off the comparison distribution; optimizing $\pi$ moves off that distribution, so the gap widens as you optimize. This is Goodhart's law with a specific mechanism.

Distribution shift. Comparisons were collected on SFT-model outputs. The RL policy generates different outputs, on which $r_\phi$ is extrapolating.

Label noise and disagreement. Human preferences are not a function: the same pair gets different answers from the same annotator on different days, and different annotators disagree systematically by demographic and cultural background. Bradley–Terry with a single scalar $r$ averages over that, which is a modelling choice with ethical consequences.

Unrepresentable criteria. Truthfulness, harm, and long-horizon coherence are not well captured by a scalar that a rater can assess in 30 seconds. A reward model trained on fast judgments learns fast-judgment proxies: confidence, length, formatting, and agreeableness.

(3) Why it is built this way. Each mechanism suggests a different mitigation — more on-policy comparisons, ensembles and pessimism, better annotation protocols, process supervision — and conflating them is why "RLHF makes models worse" debates go nowhere.

(4) Worked micro-example. Reward-model over-optimization curves are the standard measurement: train a policy against $r_\phi$ for more and more steps, and plot both the proxy reward (rising monotonically) and a held-out gold reward (rising, peaking, then falling). The gap at the peak of the gold curve is the usable optimization budget, and it is typically small — a few hundred to a few thousand PPO steps.

RLHF: the KL-regularized objective and its closed form

(1) Intuition. Maximize reward, but do not wander far from the reference model. The penalty is not a technicality; it is the only thing keeping you inside the region where the reward model is meaningful.

(2) Formal definition.

$$\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r(x,y)\right] - \beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right]$$

(3) Why it is built this way. The KL term has two jobs: it regularizes against reward hacking and it prevents the policy from collapsing to a single high-reward string. Derivation 2 shows the objective has an exact closed-form maximizer, and that the whole problem is equivalent to a KL minimization toward a tilted reference distribution.

(4) Worked micro-example. Two candidate responses, $r = (1,0)$, $\beta = 1$, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} = (0.5,0.5)$. The partition function is $Z = 0.5e^{1}+0.5e^{0} = 1.8591$, so the optimum is $\pi^{*} = (1.3591, 0.5)/1.8591 = (0.7311, 0.2689)$. Expected reward rises from $0.5$ to $0.7311$ (a gain of $0.2311$) and the KL is $0.7311\ln(0.7311/0.5) + 0.2689\ln(0.2689/0.5) = 0.2778 - 0.1668 = 0.1109$, so the objective is $0.7311 - 0.1109 = 0.6202$. That matches the derived value $\beta\log Z = \log 1.8591 = 0.6201$ exactly — a check worth doing every time you implement this. The penalty does not prevent improvement; it prices it at $\beta$ nats per unit of divergence.

PPO: the clipped surrogate

(1) Intuition. You have a batch of trajectories generated by the current policy. You want to take several gradient steps on that batch, but not so many that the policy moves far from the one that produced the data. Clipping the importance ratio enforces that.

(2) Formal definition.

$$L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\ \operatorname{clip}\left(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right], \qquad r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$$

(3) Why it is built this way. The min makes the objective a pessimistic bound: it takes the worse of the clipped and unclipped values, so the gradient disappears exactly when the update would push a good action's probability up too far, or a bad action's probability down too far. It is a cheap approximation to a trust region — cheaper than TRPO's constrained solve, and good enough.

(4) Worked micro-example. $\hat{A} = +1$, $\epsilon = 0.2$. At $r = 1.0$: $\min(1.0, \min(1.0, 1.2)) = 1.0$, gradient flows. At $r = 1.5$: $\min(1.5, 1.2) = 1.2$, gradient zero — the action's probability has already risen 50% and the objective refuses to reward further increase. At $r = 0.5$: $\min(0.5, 0.8) = 0.5$, gradient flows, because decreasing a good action's probability should be opposed. With $\hat{A} = -1$ the cases mirror: at $r = 0.5$ the value is $\max$... carefully, $\min(-0.5, -0.8) = -0.8$, clamped, gradient zero; at $r = 1.5$, $\min(-1.5, -1.2) = -1.5$, gradient flows.

GAE and the value head

(1) Intuition. The advantage is "how much better was this action than expected". You can estimate it with one step of bootstrapping (low variance, high bias) or with the full return (no bias, huge variance). GAE is the exponential interpolation between them.

(2) Formal definition. With TD residual $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$,

$$\hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}\delta_{t+l}$$

(3) Why it is built this way. $\lambda = 0$ gives one-step TD (biased, low variance); $\lambda = 1$ gives the Monte-Carlo return minus a baseline (unbiased, high variance). The value head $V_\psi$ is a second output on the same backbone, trained by regression onto the observed returns. Its role is to be the baseline, and the baseline is what makes the policy gradient's variance manageable.

(4) Worked micro-example. For language models the reward is usually a single terminal scalar $R$ and $\gamma = 1$. Then all $\delta_t = V_{t+1}-V_t$ except the last, and the sum telescopes (Derivation 4c) to $\hat{A}_t = R - V(s_t)$ — the return minus the baseline, for every token in the response. GAE is then doing nothing more than a subtraction, which is exactly why GRPO can replace the learned $V$ with a sample average.

DPO: the closed-form shortcut

(1) Intuition. The RLHF optimum has a closed form in which the reward is written as $\beta\log(\pi^{*}/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ plus a constant. Substitute that into the Bradley–Terry loss and the constant cancels. What remains is a loss on the policy itself, with no reward model and no RL loop.

(2) Formal definition.

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

(3) Why it is built this way. It removes the two hardest parts of RLHF — training a reward model and running a stable on-policy RL loop — and replaces them with a supervised classification problem on pairs, which is as easy to train as SFT. The cost is that it is only equivalent to RLHF under the Bradley–Terry assumption with a particular KL strength, and it has no exploration: it can only reweight behaviour present in the preference data.

(4) Worked micro-example. Numbers from a verified run below (Worked example): $\log\pi_\theta(y_w) = -8.039$, $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w) = -6.497$, $\log\pi_\theta(y_l) = -5.011$, $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l) = -5.212$. The implicit rewards are $r_w = 0.1(-1.543) = -0.154$ and $r_l = 0.1(0.200) = 0.020$, so the model currently prefers the dispreferred response, $\Delta = -0.174$, and the loss is $-\log\sigma(-0.174) = 0.784$ — worse than the $0.693$ of a coin flip, as it should be for a policy that has the pair backwards.

The variants: IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO

(1) Intuition. Each variant removes one assumption or one cost of DPO. IPO replaces the unbounded classification loss with a bounded regression. KTO removes the need for pairs. ORPO folds SFT and preference tuning into one stage. SimPO removes the reference model and fixes length bias.

(2) Formal definition.

IPO replaces the log-sigmoid with a squared loss on the margin against a fixed target:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IPO}} = \left(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} - \frac{1}{2\beta}\right)^{2}$$

KTO uses unpaired binary feedback (a response is desirable or not) with an asymmetric loss that weights losses more heavily than gains, drawn from prospect theory; it needs no pairs, only labels on individual responses.

ORPO adds an odds-ratio penalty to the SFT loss, with no reference model:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ORPO}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}} = -\log\sigma\left(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{1-\pi_\theta(y_w)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{1-\pi_\theta(y_l)}\right)$$

SimPO uses the length-normalized average log-likelihood as the implicit reward and adds a target margin $\gamma$, again with no reference model:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SimPO}} = -\log\sigma\left(\frac{\beta}{|y_w|}\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \frac{\beta}{|y_l|}\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) - \gamma\right)$$

(3) Why it is built this way. DPO's margin is unbounded (the loss goes to zero as $\Delta\to\infty$), which pushes the implicit reward difference ever wider and lets the policy drift arbitrarily far from the reference — IPO's bounded target fixes that. DPO needs a reference model in memory, which is a doubling of cost; SimPO and ORPO remove it. DPO needs pairs, which doubles annotation cost; KTO removes that.

(4) Worked micro-example. Same pair, $\beta = 0.1$, $\log$-ratio difference $= -1.543 - 0.200 = -1.743$. DPO loss: $-\log\sigma(0.1\cdot(-1.743)) = -\log\sigma(-0.174) = 0.784$. IPO: $(-1.743 - 5)^{2} = 45.5$ — note the enormous scale difference, which is why IPO needs a different learning rate. SimPO with $\gamma = 0.5$, $\beta = 2$, and average log-probs of $-1.2$ and $-1.0$: $-1.2\cdot2/|y_w| \cdot |y_w|$… in practice the length normalization means $\beta(\bar{\ell}_w - \bar{\ell}_l) - \gamma = 2(-1.2+1.0)-0.5 = -0.9$, loss $= -\log\sigma(-0.9) = 1.24$. Every variant is the same idea — logistic or squared loss on a margin between two responses — with a different definition of the margin. That is the thing worth remembering.

RLVR, verifiable rewards, and GRPO

(1) Intuition. For mathematics and code, you do not need a human or a learned reward model: you can check the answer. A verifier is a perfect reward on a narrow domain, and that changes the problem from reward modelling to search.

(2) Formal definition. RLVR (RL with verifiable rewards) sets $r(x,y) = 1$ if a program or a reference answer confirms $y$, else $0$. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) estimates the advantage from a group of $G$ responses sampled for the same prompt:

$$\hat{A}_{i} = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1,\ldots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\ldots,r_G)}$$

applied identically to every token of response $i$, and optimizes the clipped surrogate with a KL penalty, exactly as PPO does but with $\hat{A}_i$ in place of a critic's estimate.

(3) Why it is built this way. A learned critic for a language model is a second network of comparable size, trained on a non-stationary target, and it is a major source of instability. But for a single terminal reward with $\gamma=1$, GAE reduces to $R - V(s)$ (Concept 6, Derivation 4c), so all you need from $V$ is a per-prompt baseline. The group mean is a Monte-Carlo estimate of exactly that, computed from samples you already generated. No critic network, no value loss, no bootstrapping.

(4) Worked micro-example. Group of 4 responses with rewards $(1,0,1,0)$: mean 0.5, population standard deviation 0.5, so $\hat{A} = (1, -1, 1, -1)$. The two correct responses get reinforced and the two incorrect ones suppressed, with no reference to how hard the prompt was — prompt difficulty cancels because it is inside the mean. Now a group of 4 with rewards $(1,1,1,1)$: mean 1, std 0, advantages all zero, no gradient. That degenerate case is why GRPO wastes compute on prompts the model always solves and always fails, and why curriculum filtering of prompts is standard practice.

Outcome versus process rewards, and test-time compute

(1) Intuition. An outcome reward model scores the final answer. A process reward model scores each step. The first is cheap and sparse; the second is expensive and dense, and it is also where the interesting failure modes live.

(2) Formal definition. An ORM is $r(x,y)$ on complete responses. A PRM is $r(x, s_1, \ldots, s_k)$ scoring each reasoning step $s_i$; it can be used as a dense reward during training, or as a search guide at inference — sample $n$ partial trajectories, score the steps, and continue only the most promising. Test-time compute scaling is the second use: with a verifier and $n$ samples, the probability at least one is correct is $1-(1-p)^{n}$ where $p$ is the per-sample success rate.

(3) Why it is built this way. Outcome rewards are objectively checkable but assign credit to a whole trajectory, so a correct answer reached by invalid reasoning is reinforced. Process rewards fix the credit assignment and introduce a new problem: the PRM is itself a learned proxy, so optimizing against it during training invites reward hacking, and PRMs are expensive to label (step-level human annotation). The common compromise is an ORM or verifier for training and a PRM only for inference-time search.

(4) Worked micro-example. Per-sample success $p = 0.1$. With $n = 16$: $1-0.9^{16} = 0.815$. With $n = 64$: $1-0.9^{64} = 0.999$. Now suppose the verifier has a 5% false-positive rate: at $n=64$, $1-(1-0.1-0.045)^{64}$ for a spuriously-accepted wrong answer, so roughly $1-0.855^{64} = 0.99996$ — the verifier's error rate, not the model, is now the ceiling. Verifier quality bounds test-time scaling, and a verifier that is right 95% of the time caps you well below what 64 samples would otherwise buy.

The honest limits

(1) Intuition. Every method here optimizes a proxy, and every proxy is gameable. The failures are not exotic; they are the default outcome of optimizing hard.

(2) Formal definition — four named mechanisms.

Reward hacking. The policy finds inputs where the proxy and the true objective diverge. The measured shape is a proxy reward rising monotonically while held-out quality peaks and declines.

Length hacking. Length is correlated with apparent quality in human ratings, so a reward model trained on human comparisons learns "longer is better", and a policy optimized against it learns to be verbose. This is a documented, quantified bias and it is why length-controlled evaluation and length penalties exist.

Sycophancy. Raters prefer agreement, so the reward model learns agreement, and the policy learns to agree with the user's stated (possibly wrong) premise. This is a direct consequence of optimizing a proxy built from approval.

Benchmark overfitting. Training on data that resembles a benchmark, or selecting checkpoints by benchmark score, moves the benchmark without moving capability. With many checkpoints and a few hundred evaluation items, selection alone produces apparent gains of several points.

(3) Why it is built this way. Each mechanism has a diagnostic and a mitigation, and none has a fix. Length: measure score per token and control for length. Sycophancy: evaluate on prompts where the user asserts something false. Reward hacking: hold out a gold reward you never optimize against. Benchmark overfitting: report confidence intervals and use a held-out suite.

(4) Worked micro-example. A 300-item benchmark, accuracy 0.70. The standard error is $\sqrt{0.7\cdot0.3/300} = 0.026$, so a 95% interval is $\pm0.052$ — 5.2 points. Select the best of 8 checkpoints by that benchmark and you have selected on noise: the expected maximum of 8 draws is roughly $1.4$ standard errors above the mean, so about 3.7 points of pure selection effect. Report the interval or the number is not a measurement.

The algorithm, step by step

RLHF, three phases.

  1. SFT on demonstrations, producing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (see M17).
  2. Collect comparisons. Sample pairs $(y_1,y_2)\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ for prompts $x$; annotate which is preferred; store $(x, y_w, y_l)$.
  3. Fit the reward model by minimizing $-\log\sigma(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l))$; initialize from the SFT model with a scalar head, and normalize so $\mathbb{E}[r]=0$.
  4. Optimize the policy with PPO: sample responses from the current $\pi_\theta$, score with $r_\phi$, compute per-token KL against $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as an additional per-token penalty, compute advantages with GAE, and take clipped policy-gradient steps.
  5. Repeat phases 2–4 with fresh on-policy comparisons, which is what keeps the reward model inside its valid region.

DPO, one phase. Build pairs, compute four log-probabilities per pair (policy and reference, on both responses), form the margin, take the log-sigmoid, backpropagate into the policy only.

```text function DPO_STEP(batch, pi, pi_ref, beta): loss <- 0 for (x, y_w, y_l) in batch: logp_w <- sum_t log pi(y_w[t] | x, y_w[:t]) logp_l <- sum_t log pi(y_l[t] | x, y_l[:t]) with no_grad: ref_w <- sum_t log pi_ref(y_w[t] | x, y_w[:t]) ref_l <- sum_t log pi_ref(y_l[t] | x, y_l[:t]) margin <- beta * ((logp_w - ref_w) - (logp_l - ref_l)) loss += -log_sigmoid(margin) loss <- loss / len(batch) loss.backward() # only pi's parameters have requires_grad return loss ```

Complexity. DPO: one forward and backward pass of the policy plus one forward pass of the reference, over two responses per pair — roughly 3x the cost of an SFT step on the same tokens, with no sampling and no reward model. PPO: sampling a response of length $L$ costs $L$ sequential forward passes (the KV cache makes each one cheap but they are serial), then a scoring pass, then a backward pass over the batch — dominated by generation, which is why PPO is one to two orders of magnitude more expensive per update than DPO.

Derivations

Derivation 1: Bradley–Terry from a latent score, and its gauge freedom

Step 1 — the assumption. Each response has a latent score $s(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}$. Assume the preference probability depends only on the difference, through the logistic CDF:

$$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) = F\left(s(x,y_w)-s(x,y_l)\right) \quad\text{with}\quad F(z) = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

Writing $r = s$ and taking logs of the likelihood over independent comparisons gives

$$\log P(\mathcal{D}\mid r) = \sum_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\left(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\right)$$

and maximizing it is minimizing the negative, which is $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}$.

Step 2 — the gradient. With $\Delta = r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)$ and $\frac{d}{d\Delta}\log\sigma(\Delta) = \sigma(-\Delta) = 1-\sigma(\Delta)$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}}{\partial r(x,y_w)} = -\left(1-\sigma(\Delta)\right), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}}{\partial r(x,y_l)} = +\left(1-\sigma(\Delta)\right)$$

So the loss is a soft-margin ranking loss: the gradient magnitude is the model's error in the preference probability, and it vanishes only when the preference is already predicted with certainty. No saturation means no vanishing gradient, which is why BT is easy to fit.

Step 3 — gauge freedom. Adding any function $c(x)$ to every score, $r(x,y)\to r(x,y)+c(x)$, leaves every difference — and therefore every probability and the entire loss — unchanged. Two consequences you must handle in practice: (i) the reward is identified only up to a per-prompt constant, so an RM's absolute values are meaningless and comparisons must only be made within a prompt; (ii) the RM must be centered (typically by subtracting the mean reward on a reference set) before being used as an RL reward, otherwise a constant offset shifts every advantage and biases the KL term.

Step 4 — what the model cannot represent. Since only differences matter, BT cannot represent intransitive preferences. If a majority prefers $A\succ B$, $B\succ C$, and $C\succ A$ — which happens with real annotators and multi-criteria judgments — no scalar $r$ fits, and the fit produces a compromise that mispredicts all three. That is a modelling limitation, not a data problem, and it is the strongest argument for the multi-objective and personalization approaches that replace the single scalar.

Derivation 2: the closed-form optimal policy

Step 1 — write the objective per prompt. Since prompts are independent, fix $x$ and optimize over the conditional $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ subject to $\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1$:

$$\max_{\pi}\ \sum_y \pi(y\mid x)r(x,y) - \beta\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$

Step 2 — define the tilted reference. Let

$$Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$$

which is a normalizing constant depending on $x$ but not on $y$, and define the candidate

$$\pi^{*}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$$

This is a valid distribution because $\sum_y\pi^{*} = Z/Z = 1$.

Step 3 — rewrite the objective as a KL. Add and subtract $\beta\log Z(x)$, which does not depend on $\pi$:

$$\sum_y\pi\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}} = \sum_y\pi\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)/Z} - \sum_y\pi\log Z + \sum_y\pi\frac{r}{\beta}$$

The first term on the right is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{*})$, the second is $-\log Z$ since $\sum_y\pi = 1$, and the third is $\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_y\pi r$. So

$$\sum_y\pi\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{*}) - \log Z + \frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_\pi[r]$$

Multiply by $-\beta$ and add $\mathbb{E}_\pi[r]$:

$$\mathcal{J}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[r] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = -\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{*}) + \beta\log Z(x)$$

Step 4 — maximize. $\beta\log Z(x)$ does not depend on $\pi$, and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\ge0$ with equality iff $\pi = \pi^{*}$. Therefore

$$\pi^{*}(y\mid x) = \arg\max_\pi \mathcal{J}(\pi)\quad\text{and}\quad \mathcal{J}(\pi^{*}) = \beta\log Z(x)$$

and the optimum is exactly the tilted reference — no approximation, no iteration.

Step 5 — invert it. Taking logs of the definition of $\pi^{*}$:

$$\log\pi^{*}(y\mid x) = \log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) + \frac{r(x,y)}{\beta} - \log Z(x)$$

and rearranging for the reward:

$$r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)$$

This is the key identity: the reward is recoverable from the optimal policy up to a prompt-dependent constant. Everything that follows is a substitution of this identity into the Bradley–Terry loss, where the constant will cancel.

Derivation 3: DPO, completely

Step 1 — substitute the identity into Bradley–Terry. Under the BT model with the optimal reward $r = r^{*}$, the probability of a preference is

$$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) = \sigma\left(r^{*}(x,y_w)-r^{*}(x,y_l)\right)$$

Step 2 — compute the difference. Using Step 5 of Derivation 2 for both responses:

$$r^{*}(x,y_w) - r^{*}(x,y_l) = \left[\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\right] - \left[\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\right]$$

Step 3 — the cancellation. The two $\beta\log Z(x)$ terms are identical — same prompt, same partition function — so they subtract to zero:

$$r^{*}(x,y_w)-r^{*}(x,y_l) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}$$

This is the entire trick. The intractable sum over all possible responses never has to be computed, because it appears once per response with the same value and cancels.

Step 4 — the loss. Substitute into $-\log\sigma(\cdot)$ and replace the unknown optimum $\pi^{*}$ with the parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ being trained:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

Step 5 — the gradient. Write $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ (the implicit reward) and $\Delta = \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)$. Since $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log\sigma(\Delta) = \sigma(-\Delta)\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\theta}$,

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\beta\,\sigma\left(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\right)\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\right]$$

Note that $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ contributes no gradient — it is frozen. Read the structure: the bracket is a policy gradient that increases the log-probability of the preferred response and decreases that of the dispreferred one; the scalar in front is the model's error in the preference probability, so a pair already correctly ranked contributes almost nothing, and a pair ranked backwards contributes almost a full unit. DPO is a policy gradient with the advantage replaced by the implicit reward difference — which is why it is a valid RL method and not merely a classifier.

Derivation 4: PPO's clip and the GAE telescoping

(a) The four cases. Define $u = r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t$ and $v = \operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t$, and recall the objective is $\min(u,v)$.

Case 1: $\hat{A}>0$ and $r>1+\epsilon$. Then $v = (1+\epsilon)\hat{A} < r\hat{A} = u$, so the min is $v$, which is constant in $\theta$: gradient zero. The action's probability has already increased by more than $\epsilon$; the objective refuses to reward more.

Case 2: $\hat{A}>0$ and $r<1-\epsilon$. Then $v=(1-\epsilon)\hat{A} > r\hat{A}$, so the min is $u = r\hat{A}$: gradient flows, pushing the probability up — correct, since a good action was down-weighted.

Case 3: $\hat{A}<0$ and $r<1-\epsilon$. Then $v = (1-\epsilon)\hat{A}$ and $u = r\hat{A}$ with $r < 1-\epsilon$; multiplying the inequality by the negative $\hat{A}$ flips it, giving $r\hat{A} > (1-\epsilon)\hat{A}$, so the min is $v$, constant: gradient zero. A bad action's probability has already been cut by more than $\epsilon$.

Case 4: $\hat{A}<0$ and $r>1+\epsilon$. Then $v=(1+\epsilon)\hat{A}$ and $u = r\hat{A} < (1+\epsilon)\hat{A}$ since $r>1+\epsilon$ and $\hat{A}<0$; the min is $u$: gradient flows, cutting the probability.

In every case the clip removes the incentive to move further than $\epsilon$ in the direction already taken. The objective is a lower bound on the unclipped surrogate, so maximizing it cannot be gamed by an unbounded ratio. What it does not do is bound the KL divergence — a sequence of small clipped steps can still accumulate a large policy change, which is why a separate KL penalty is used in RLHF.

(b) GAE by unrolling. With $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, expand for $\lambda$:

$$\hat{A}_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\delta_{t+1} + (\gamma\lambda)^{2}\delta_{t+2}+\cdots = \sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}\delta_{t+l}$$

At $\lambda=0$ only $\delta_t$ survives — one-step TD, biased by $V$'s error. At $\lambda=1$ the sum telescopes:

$$\sum_{l\ge0}\gamma^{l}\delta_{t+l} = \sum_{l\ge0}\gamma^{l}r_{t+l} + \sum_{l\ge0}\gamma^{l}\left[\gamma V(s_{t+l+1})-V(s_{t+l})\right] = \sum_{l\ge0}\gamma^{l}r_{t+l} - V(s_t)$$

because the second sum telescopes ($\gamma V_{t+1}-V_t + \gamma^2V_{t+2}-\gamma V_{t+1}+\cdots$) and the tail vanishes as $l\to\infty$. So $\lambda=1$ is the discounted return minus the baseline — unbiased if $V$ is only used as a baseline, which it is.

(c) The language-model case. With a single terminal reward $R$, $\gamma=1$, and $V(s_T)=0$:

$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l\ge0}\delta_{t+l} = R - V(s_t)$$

for every token $t$ in the response. All the structure of GAE collapses to a subtraction, and the only thing the value head must supply is a per-prompt baseline.

Derivation 5: GRPO's advantage is an unbiased, variance-reduced estimator

Step 1 — the score-function estimator. For a policy $\pi_\theta$ over responses and a reward $R$,

$$\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\left[R(y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\right]$$

because $\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta = \pi_\theta\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ and the gradient moves inside the sum.

Step 2 — any baseline is unbiased. Let $b$ be any quantity not depending on $y$ (in particular, depending only on $x$). Then

$$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\left[b\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\right] = b\sum_y\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(y) = b\,\nabla_\theta\underbrace{\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)}_{=1} = 0$$

So subtracting $b$ changes the estimator's value in expectation not at all. This is what licenses using a sample mean as a baseline: it cannot bias the gradient, only change its variance.

Step 3 — the variance-minimizing baseline. Write the estimator as $g = s(y)(R(y)-b)$ with $s = \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)$. Then

$$\mathrm{Var}(g) = \mathbb{E}\left[\|s\|^{2}(R-b)^{2}\right] - \|\mathbb{E}[g]\|^{2}$$

The second term does not depend on $b$. Differentiating the first with respect to the scalar $b$:

$$\frac{d}{db}\mathbb{E}\left[\|s\|^{2}(R-b)^{2}\right] = -2\mathbb{E}\left[\|s\|^{2}(R-b)\right] = 0 \implies b^{*} = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|s\|^{2}R\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|s\|^{2}\right]}$$

So the exact minimizer is a score-norm-weighted average of rewards, not $\mathbb{E}[R]$. The two coincide only when $\|s\|^{2}$ is uncorrelated with $R$. GRPO uses the unweighted group mean $\bar{R}$ — a Monte-Carlo estimate of $\mathbb{E}[R]$ — which is the standard practical choice, is exactly the minimizer in the regression-style analysis where the score norm is treated as constant, and is very cheap. Being precise about this matters: "the mean is the optimal baseline" is a common over-claim.

Step 4 — group normalization. Dividing by the group standard deviation,

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{R_i-\bar{R}}{\mathrm{std}(R)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L} \propto \frac{1}{\mathrm{std}(R)}\sum_i (R_i-\bar{R})\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i)$$

is a positive scalar times the baseline-subtracted estimator. If the optimizer's step size were fixed in absolute terms this would matter; with Adam, which normalizes by the gradient's second moment, dividing by a constant is nearly a no-op on the update direction and changes only the effective step scale. The normalization therefore mostly serves numerical conditioning across prompts of different difficulty, and it has a failure mode: when all $G$ rewards are equal, $\mathrm{std}=0$ and the advantage is $0/0$ — handled in practice by adding a small epsilon and discarding such groups.

Step 5 — why no critic is needed. From Derivation 4(c), for a single terminal reward with $\gamma=1$, the exact advantage is $R - V(s_t)$, and the only role of $V$ is to be a per-prompt baseline. $\bar{R}$ estimated from $G$ samples of that same prompt is a per-prompt baseline, and by Step 2 substituting it cannot bias the estimator. GRPO therefore trades a learned, non-stationary, expensive critic for a higher-variance but unbiased and free Monte-Carlo baseline. That is the whole argument, and its cost is visible in the group size: variance falls as $1/G$, so you pay $G$ generations per prompt to buy back what the critic would have given you for one.

Worked example

Setting. $V=5$, $T=3$, $\beta = 0.1$, $y_w = (1,3,2)$, $y_l = (4,0,1)$, policy logits and reference logits drawn at random with a fixed seed.

Step 1 — four log-probabilities. Each is a sum of three log-softmax values: $\log\pi_\theta(y_w) = -8.039202$, $\log\pi_\theta(y_l) = -5.011080$, $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w) = -6.496558$, $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l) = -5.211604$. The policy assigns higher probability to the dispreferred response — these are random logits, so this is expected half the time.

Step 2 — implicit rewards. $\hat r_w = 0.1(-8.039202+6.496558) = 0.1(-1.542644) = -0.154264$; $\hat r_l = 0.1(-5.011080+5.211604) = 0.1(0.200476) = +0.020052$. Note what these mean: the policy has moved away from the preferred response relative to the reference (negative reward) and toward the dispreferred one.

Step 3 — margin and loss. $\Delta = -0.174317$; $\sigma(\Delta) = 0.456531$; $\mathcal{L} = -\log 0.456531 = 0.784099$. A coin-flip policy would score $0.6931$; this policy is worse than a coin flip on this pair, as it should be.

Step 4 — gradient coefficient. $-\beta\sigma(-\Delta) = -0.1\cdot0.543469 = -0.054347$. Multiply by $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w) - \nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l)$; for a softmax the gradient at position $t$ with target token $c$ is $\mathbf{e}_c-\mathbf{p}_t$. The resulting gradient is $-0.0543$ at the $(0,\text{token }1)$ entry, which under gradient descent raises logit 1 — increasing the preferred response's probability, exactly as intended, with the opposite pattern at the dispreferred response's tokens.

Step 5 — the cancellation check. Recompute the loss with both implicit rewards shifted by $c\in\{-5, 0, 3.7\}$: $0.784099$ every time. This is $\beta\log Z(x)$ being the same for both responses of the same prompt. Had the two responses come from different prompts, the shift would not cancel and the comparison would be invalid — which is why DPO requires pairs, not a bag of labelled responses.

Step 6 — the reference model's role. $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ enters only through $\hat r$; it has no gradient. If $\pi_\theta = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ exactly, then $\hat r_w = \hat r_l = 0$, $\Delta = 0$, and the loss is $\log 2 = 0.6931$ for every pair — the maximum-entropy starting point. Training can only improve by moving the policy relative to a fixed reference, which is what makes the method stable and also what caps how far it can go.

Step 7 — the corresponding RLHF view. Under Derivation 2, the optimal policy for this pair with $\beta = 0.1$ and rewards $(1,0)$ would satisfy $\pi^{*}(y_w)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w) \propto e^{10}$ and $\pi^{*}(y_l)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l)\propto 1$, a log-ratio of about 10. DPO never computes that; it pushes the log-ratio difference upward by an amount set by the gradient, and it will asymptote wherever the data's preference signal balances. DPO does not target the same optimum as RLHF unless the Bradley–Terry model is exactly correct, which is the substance of the IPO critique.

Common misconceptions

"DPO is RLHF without the reward model"

It is RLHF's optimum reparameterized as a loss, derived by substituting the closed-form optimal reward into the Bradley–Terry likelihood. It is not an approximation to RLHF in the sense of skipping a step; it is a different algorithm that coincides with RLHF's solution under a specific assumption (BT preference model, fixed $\beta$, reference policy fixed). It has no on-policy sampling, so it cannot discover responses absent from the preference data — a limitation RLHF does not share.

"The reward model is the model's reward, so maximizing it maximizes quality"

The reward model is a proxy fit to a finite sample of human judgments, and optimizing it hard moves the policy off the distribution where it was fit. Measured over-optimization curves show proxy reward rising while held-out quality peaks and falls. The KL term and early stopping exist precisely to keep you on the left of that peak.

"The mean reward is the variance-minimizing baseline"

It is the standard and usually good choice, and it is exactly what makes GRPO's critic-free advantage legitimate. But the exact variance minimizer for a scalar baseline is $\mathbb{E}[\|s\|^{2}R]/\mathbb{E}[\|s\|^{2}]$, where $s$ is the score function — a score-norm-weighted average. The mean is optimal only when the score norm is uncorrelated with the reward. The distinction rarely changes practice; it changes whether you understand the estimator.

"GRPO removes the value function, so it is just REINFORCE"

It removes the learned value function and replaces it with a Monte-Carlo per-prompt baseline, then normalizes by the group's standard deviation. REINFORCE with no baseline has far higher variance; the group mean is what makes it practical. The price is group size: variance falls as $1/G$, so GRPO pays $G$ generations per prompt where a critic would have cost one extra forward pass.

"RLVR teaches the model new reasoning abilities"

This is contested and the honest answer is that the evidence is mixed. Verifiable-reward RL reliably improves pass@1 on in-distribution verifiable tasks and reliably lengthens the model's outputs, and there is good evidence that much of the gain is sharper sampling of what the base model could already do rather than the acquisition of new capability — measured by comparing pass@k for large $k$ before and after, where the base model often matches the RL model. Whether any capability is genuinely new is an open question, not a settled one, and claims in either direction should be read against that.

"If the benchmark score went up, the model got better"

At 300 items the 95% confidence interval is about $\pm5$ points. Select the best of eight checkpoints and you have ~3.7 points of pure selection effect. Without intervals, without a held-out suite, and without length control, a reported gain is indistinguishable from noise plus verbosity.

4. Derive the DPO gradient from the loss, step by step.

Solution $\mathcal{L} = -\log\sigma(\Delta)$ with $\Delta = \beta[(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w)) - (\log\pi_\theta(y_l)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l))]$. With $V_T = 0$ and no reward until $T$, the sum of differences telescopes to $V_{T+1}-V_t = -V_t$, and the reward contributes $R$. So $\hat A_t = R - V(s_t)$ for every $t$.

7. Show that adding a constant $c(x)$ to every reward for a fixed prompt leaves the KL-regularized optimum's relative probabilities unchanged, and explain what does change.

Solution The optimum is $\pi^{*}\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{(r+c)/\beta} = e^{c/\beta}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{r/\beta}$; the factor $e^{c/\beta}$ is the same for every $y$, so it is absorbed by normalization and $\pi^{*}$ is unchanged as a distribution. What changes is $Z(x) \to e^{c/\beta}Z(x)$, and therefore the optimal objective value $\beta\log Z$, which shifts by $c$. In a PPO implementation the constant does change things, because the advantage $R - \bar R$ is computed per batch, not per prompt; a prompt-dependent offset that is constant across responses cancels in the advantage, but a batch-level offset does not.

Stretch (build or break it)

8. Implement GRPO's advantage and demonstrate the degenerate-group problem. Then propose and test a fix.

Solution ```python import numpy as np def advantages(rewards, eps=1e-4): r = np.asarray(rewards, dtype=float) return (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + eps) print(advantages([1,0,1,0])) # [-1. -1. 1. 1.] print(advantages([1,1,1,1])) # [0. 0. 0. 0.] -> no gradient ``` The fix is to filter prompts by their empirical success rate: keep prompts where the group's mean reward is strictly between 0 and 1, since a prompt the model always solves carries no learning signal and one it never solves carries no *positive* signal either (with all-zero rewards every advantage is zero too). A more principled version uses difficulty-targeted sampling to target prompts near 50% success, which maximizes the group's reward variance and therefore the gradient's information per sample. Report the fraction of discarded groups — if it is high, your curriculum is mis-tuned and you are paying for generations that teach nothing.

9. Construct a reward-hacking demonstration on paper: design a reward model and a policy change that increases the proxy while decreasing true quality, and say what measurement detects it.

Solution Reward model: a BT model trained on human comparisons over a helpfulness dataset. Because raters systematically prefer thorough answers, it learns a positive coefficient on length and on the presence of headers and bullet lists. Policy change: append a summary section, a "Key takeaways" list, and restate the question — none of which adds information but all of which raise the learned reward. The proxy rises by, say, 0.3; true helpfulness, as measured by a fresh blinded comparison against the SFT model, falls because the answer is now three times longer with the same content. Detection: (i) plot score against response length and report the partial correlation; (ii) run length-controlled evaluation, comparing at matched token counts; (iii) hold out a gold reward model trained on a different annotation protocol and never optimized against — its curve peaks and falls while the proxy's keeps climbing. Any one of these would catch it; the reason it happens anyway is that none is standard practice.

10. With a verifier that is correct on 97% of submissions, how many samples can you usefully draw before the verifier's error dominates? Compute for a task with per-sample success $p = 0.05$.

Solution A wrong answer is accepted if the verifier false-positives, probability $0.03$ per sample. Over $n$ samples, the probability at least one false acceptance occurs is $1-0.97^{n}$: $n=16\to 0.386$, $n=32\to 0.62$, $n=64\to 0.858$. Meanwhile the probability of at least one *correct* answer is $1-0.95^{n}$: $n=16\to 0.560$, $n=32\to 0.806$, $n=64\to 0.962$. At $n=16$ correct answers outnumber false accepts; by $n=64$ the ratio is 1.12:1 and the verifier is nearly as likely to bless a wrong answer as to find a right one. So the useful range is roughly $n \le 1/(1-p)\cdot\ln(1/\epsilon_{\mathrm{ver}})$ — a few tens of samples, not thousands — and the binding constraint is verifier precision, not sample budget.
