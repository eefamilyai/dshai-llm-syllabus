Multimodality

An image is a token sequence, and the length of that sequence sets the whole design.

on this page

Why this module exists

A transformer consumes a sequence of vectors and nothing else. Multimodality is therefore not a new architecture but a compression problem: an image, an audio clip, or a video frame must be turned into a sequence short enough to afford, dense enough to be useful, and aligned well enough to talk about in the same vocabulary as text. Almost every design decision in the field — patch size, whether to use a projector or cross-attention, whether to tokenise an image discretely, how hard to compress video — follows from the arithmetic of that sequence length. The second half of the module, diffusion, is the mirror image: instead of turning pixels into tokens for a language model, it turns noise into pixels by learning a score. Both halves are about the same underlying object, the transformer, and both are areas where settled results and the moving frontier are routinely confused.

Prerequisites check

  1. What are the shapes of Q, K, V in one attention head, and what is the cost of the score matrix in $T$ and $d$?
  2. What does a cosine similarity of 0.9 between two embeddings mean geometrically?
  3. Write the cross-entropy loss for a classification problem with $K$ classes.
  4. Given $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\epsilon$ with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, what is the distribution of $x_t$ given $x_0$?
  5. What is the difference between a VAE's latent and a VQ-VAE's codebook index?
Answers 1. $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_h}$ per head; the score matrix is $T \times T$, costing $O(T^2 d_h)$ to compute and $O(T^2)$ memory if materialised. 2. The two vectors point in nearly the same direction; normalised, their inner product is 0.9 out of a maximum of 1. 3. $-\sum_{k} y_k \log p_k$ with $y$ one-hot and $p = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$. 4. $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon$ with $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$, so $x_t \mid x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)$ — a sum of independent Gaussians. 5. A VAE latent is a continuous vector; a VQ-VAE codebook index is a discrete symbol from a finite set, so it can be embedded like a token. **M12** covers 1; **M03** covers 3 and 4.

Core concepts

An image is a sequence of patches

Intuition. Cut the picture into a grid of tiles, treat each tile as one "word", and let attention work out which tiles matter to each other. Nothing about the transformer changes; only the tokeniser does.

Formal definition. Split an $H \times W \times C$ image into non-overlapping $P \times P$ patches, giving $N = (H/P)(W/P) = HW/P^2$ patches. Flatten each to a vector of length $P^2C$, apply one learned linear map $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{P^2C \times d}$, then add positional embeddings. A learnable [CLS] token is often prepended when the representation is used for classification.

Why it is built this way. A convolution has locality and translation equivariance built into its weights; a transformer has neither, so it must learn them from data — which is why ViTs need large-scale pretraining or heavy augmentation to match a CNN at small scale. What it buys is scale and uniformity: every other modality can be turned into patches or frames and fed to the same block, and attention has no fixed receptive field to grow.

Worked micro-example. A $224\times224\times3$ image at $P = 16$ gives $14 \times 14 = 196$ tokens, each of dimension $16^2 \cdot 3 = 768$ — coincidentally equal to the model width of ViT-Base, which is why the projection looks like the identity and is often mistaken for one. At $P = 32$ the same image is 49 tokens of dimension 3072; at $1024\times1024$ with $P=16$ it is 4096 tokens.

CLIP: aligning two encoders

Intuition. Train an image encoder and a text encoder so that a picture of a dog and the words "a photo of a dog" land in the same place, then classify by asking which caption's embedding is nearest.

Formal definition. Two encoders produce unit-norm embeddings $\mathbf{e}_I$, $\mathbf{e}_T$; the score is their scaled inner product with a learned temperature. For a batch of $B$ matched pairs, the loss is symmetric InfoNCE:

$$ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i} -\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{s_{ij}}} + \frac{1}{B}\sum_{j} -\log\frac{e^{s_{jj}}}{\sum_i e^{s_{ij}}}\right], \qquad s_{ij} = \frac{\langle \mathbf{e}_{I_i}, \mathbf{e}_{T_j}\rangle}{\tau}. $$

The first sum is image-to-text (a batch of $B$ pairs supplies $B-1$ negatives per image for free), the second is text-to-image.

Why it is built this way. No labels are needed: any (image, caption) pair on the web is a positive, and every other caption in the batch is a negative. The failure mode is the same as any in-batch-negative method — negatives that are actually positives ("a dog" and "a puppy") are pushed apart — and the fix that made CLIP work at scale was simply $B = 32{,}768$, which makes such collisions rare and tightens the mutual-information bound derived in M22.

Worked micro-example. Zero-shot classification with $K$ class names computes $K$ text embeddings, normalises them, and takes the argmax over $K$ cosine similarities with the image embedding. This is a retrieval operation, not a trained classifier: the "weights" of the final layer are text embeddings computed at inference. Templating each class as "a photo of a {class}" and averaging several templates is worth 1–5 points, because it moves the text embedding toward the region captions occupy.

Getting vision into an LLM: three designs

Intuition. You can translate the image into the model's language (a projector), let the model ask the image questions as it goes (cross-attention), or never have two languages at all (native fusion).

Formal definition. Projector (LLaVA-style): run a ViT, take its $N$ output vectors, map each through a linear or two-layer MLP from the ViT width to the LLM width, and prepend them to the text embeddings as ordinary tokens. Cross-attention (Flamingo-style): compress the visual features into a fixed number of latents with a resampler, then insert gated cross-attention layers into a frozen LLM so text tokens attend to those latents. Native early fusion: quantise the image into discrete tokens with a VQ tokeniser and feed them into the same vocabulary as text, so one transformer models everything from the start.

Why it is built this way. The designs differ in who pays. The projector pays context length — every visual token occupies a slot for the whole conversation. Cross-attention pays architectural complexity and a compression bottleneck — the resampler discards information, but the LLM's sequence stays short and the LLM can be frozen. Native fusion pays tokeniser quality and training cost — a discrete image tokeniser is lossy and the model must learn vision from scratch, but generating images becomes just another token sequence and there is no connector to train.

Worked micro-example. A 1024×1024 image at $P=14$ is 5329 ViT tokens. Projected directly, that is 5329 extra slots in every subsequent turn's KV cache. A Perceiver resampler with 64 latents compresses it to 64 — an 83-fold reduction, at the cost of whatever a 64-vector bottleneck cannot represent. That single number, tokens per image, explains most of the practical differences between the architectures.

The visual token budget

Intuition. A picture is worth a thousand words, and at current prices a thousand words is roughly what it costs.

Formal definition. With $N$ visual tokens at width $d$ and $L$ layers, attention costs $4N^2d$ FLOPs per layer (both $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}$) and the KV cache costs $2Ln_{kv}d_hb\,N$ bytes. Adding text of length $M$ makes the sequence $T = N + M$, and cost scales as $T^2$.

Why it is built this way. The quadratic term is why resolution is expensive and why every high-resolution model has a compression stage: dynamic tiling splits the image into crops processed separately and then merged, and pooling or pixel-shuffle reduces the token grid before the LLM sees it. The alternative — accepting $N = 16{,}384$ for a 1024×1024 image at $P=8$ — costs tens of TFLOPs of attention per image and 1.5 GiB of KV cache, which is affordable for one image and ruinous for a fifty-page document.

Worked micro-example. At $d = 1024$ and $L = 24$: 196 tokens cost 3.8 GFLOP of attention and 18 MiB of cache; 4096 tokens cost 1.65 TFLOP and 384 MiB; 16384 tokens cost 26.4 TFLOP and 1.5 GiB. A 437-fold compute increase for an 84-fold token increase — the square, made concrete. Note also that the language model's weights are unchanged by any of this: images do not make the model bigger, they make the sequence longer.

Image tokenisers: continuous or discrete

Intuition. A continuous latent is a compressed picture; a discrete token is a compressed picture plus a finite alphabet, and the alphabet is what lets a language model generate it.

Formal definition. A VAE encoder maps $H\times W\times 3$ to an $H/f \times W/f \times 16$ latent with $f = 8$, a 12-fold reduction in raw values ($1024^2 \cdot 3 = 3.1$M down to $128^2\cdot 16 = 262$K). A VQ-VAE additionally quantises each latent vector to its nearest entry in a learned codebook of size $K$, trained with the reconstruction loss plus a commitment term so the encoder does not drift away from the codebook.

Why it is built this way. Continuous latents are better for reconstruction and for diffusion; discrete codes are better for autoregressive modelling, because a codebook index is a vocabulary entry that can be predicted with a cross-entropy loss and sampled with the machinery of M15. The straight-through estimator, which passes gradients through the rounding operation unchanged, is the trick that makes the discrete version trainable at all.

Worked micro-example. A 1024×1024 image through an $f=8$ VAE becomes a $128 \times 128$ grid of 16-dimensional latents — 262,144 continuous values, or, if quantised spatially into 16×16 blocks, 64 tokens per image at very high compression, or 16,384 tokens at one code per latent cell. The whole design space of image tokenisers is choosing a point on that line.

Diffusion: noising, scoring, and reversing

Intuition. Learn to recognise noise, then start from pure noise and remove what the model thinks is there, one step at a time.

Formal definition. The forward process adds Gaussian noise, $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}, (1-\alpha_t)I)$, which composes in closed form to $q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)$ with $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t}\alpha_s$. A network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ is trained to predict the noise that was added, and generation runs the chain backwards.

Why it is built this way. Predicting noise is equivalent to estimating the score of the data distribution, $\nabla_{x}\log q_t(x)$, and the score is what a reverse diffusion step needs. Formulated that way, generation is a discretised stochastic differential equation and the model is a learned vector field pointing toward higher data density. That is why diffusion models are unstable to over-guidance: you are amplifying a vector field, and amplifying it too far leaves the manifold the data lives on.

Worked micro-example. In the implementation below, a two-dimensional distribution (eight Gaussians on a ring) is sampled using the exact score and 200 reverse steps, with no training at all. The recovered samples have mean radius 3.00 against a true 3.00, and within-mode spread 0.150 against a true 0.15 — the reverse process recovers the distribution to within sampling noise. A trained network simply replaces the analytic score with a learned one.

Audio, video, and what is settled

Intuition. Audio is a high-rate time series and video is audio's problem multiplied by a frame rate. Both are token-count problems before they are modelling problems.

Formal definition. Whisper converts 30 seconds of audio into 80 mel channels at 100 frames per second — 3000 frames — then a two-layer convolution with stride 2 halves that to 1500 encoder positions before a decoder generates text tokens. Discrete audio codecs (EnCodec, SoundStream) go further, producing a finite alphabet of acoustic tokens per timestep, which makes speech generatable exactly as text is. Video multiplies everything by the frame count: one minute at 8 fps with 256 tokens per frame is 122,880 tokens, which is why every practical video model subsamples frames, uses tubelets spanning several frames, or compresses temporally in the codec.

Why it is built this way. The pattern repeats across modalities: convert to a compressed sequence, keep the length affordable, hand it to a transformer. Where the analogy breaks is temporal and perceptual structure — audio has phase and prosody that a bag of frames loses, video has motion that a per-frame encoder cannot represent, and the correct inductive bias for either is still open.

Worked micro-example. A one-hour documentary at 1 fps and 256 tokens per frame is 921,600 tokens — about nine times the largest commonly available context window. Sampling at 0.2 fps gives 14,400 tokens, which fits, at the cost of missing anything that happens between frames.

Settled versus moving. Settled: patch tokenisation of images; CLIP-style contrastive alignment as a pretraining objective; the fact that token count rather than pixel count drives cost; the variational derivation of the diffusion objective; classifier-free guidance as the standard conditioning method. Moving fast: the optimal fusion architecture, whether discrete or continuous image tokens will win, whether autoregressive or diffusion models will dominate image generation, efficient video tokenisation, and native audio-video-text models. Treat any claim about those five as provisional.

The algorithm, step by step

Preparing one image for a language model, the projector route.

  1. Preprocess to the encoder's expected resolution and normalisation; resize and centre-crop, keeping the aspect ratio if tiling.
  2. Patchify: unfold into non-overlapping $P \times P$ patches, flatten each to $P^2C$, apply the learned projection.
  3. Add positions as learned or RoPE-style embeddings; prepend [CLS] if the encoder was trained with one.
  4. Encode through the ViT: $L$ blocks of self-attention and MLP, producing $N \times d_{\text{vis}}$ features.
  5. Select and compress: take the patch tokens rather than just [CLS], optionally drop the last few layers (later ViT layers are tuned for classification), and optionally resample or pool to $N' < N$.
  6. Project through $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{vis}} \times d}$ to the language model's width.
  7. Splice the projected tokens into the sequence where the image placeholder sits, letting the language model's causal attention treat them as ordinary prefix tokens.
  8. Account for the extra $N'$ tokens in the KV cache and context budget for every subsequent turn.

```text patches = unfold(image, P) # (N, P*P*C) x = patches @ E # (N, d_vis) x = x + pos_embed features = vit(x) # (N, d_vis) visual = projector(features) # (N', d) seq = [text_before, visual, text_after] logits = llm(seq) # visual tokens are ordinary prefix tokens ```

Complexity. ViT encoding is $O(N^2 d_{\text{vis}})$ per layer for attention plus $O(N d_{\text{vis}}^2)$ for the MLP; the language model then pays $O((N'+M)^2 d)$ per layer for text length $M$. For $N' = 576$ and $M = 200$, the image contributes 74% of the sequence and therefore about 54% of the attention compute — the practical reason visual token compression is the highest-leverage optimisation in the stack.

Derivations

1. Patchification, and why cost is quadratic in the patch grid

An $H \times W$ image cut into $P \times P$ patches yields $N = (H/P)(W/P)$ patches, each with $P^2C$ numbers. The projection $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{P^2C \times d}$ costs $2N P^2 C d$ FLOPs, linear in $N$ for fixed $P$. Attention is not:

$$ \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top: \; 2N^2d \text{ FLOPs}, \qquad \mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}: \; 2N^2d \text{ FLOPs}, $$

so one layer's attention is $4N^2d$ FLOPs and $L$ layers is $4LN^2d$. Halving the patch size quadruples $N$ and therefore sixteenfolds the attention term, while only doubling the projection term. That asymmetry is why patch size is the single most consequential hyperparameter in a vision transformer: it trades a linear cost against a quadratic one. Concretely, moving from $P=16$ to $P=8$ on a 1024×1024 image takes attention from 1.65 TFLOP to 26.4 TFLOP.

2. The CLIP gradient, and what the temperature does

With $s_{ij} = \langle \mathbf{e}_{I_i}, \mathbf{e}_{T_j}\rangle/\tau$ and the image-to-text term $\mathcal{L}_i = -s_{ii} + \log\sum_j e^{s_{ij}}$, differentiate with respect to a score:

$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_i}{\partial s_{ik}} = p_{ik} - \delta_{ik}, \qquad p_{ik} = \frac{e^{s_{ik}}}{\sum_j e^{s_{ij}}} . $$

The matched pair's score is pushed up with weight $1 - p_{ii}$ and every batch-mate is pushed down with weight $p_{ik}$. Two consequences matter. The gradient is dominated by the hardest negatives — captions that are nearly right — which is why a large batch beats many small ones. And $\tau$ sets the scale of $s$ and hence the sharpness of $p$: a small $\tau$ makes the softmax nearly one-hot early in training, killing the gradient on all but one negative, while a large $\tau$ leaves the distribution nearly uniform and the loss nearly flat. CLIP's learned temperature is clipped precisely so it cannot collapse. Note that the gradient depends on $s$ only through $p$, which means the ratio of similarities is learned, not their absolute values — the geometric content of "aligning two encoders".

3. Forward noising, the score, and the training objective

Closed form. With $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\epsilon_t$ and independent $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, unrolling gives $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\bar\epsilon$ for some $\bar\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$: a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, with variance $\bar\alpha_t \sum_s (1-\alpha_s)/\bar\alpha_s = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, since $\bar\alpha_t/\bar\alpha_s = \prod_{u=s+1}^{t}\alpha_u$ telescopes. So

$$ q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0,\; (1-\bar\alpha_t)I). $$

Score identity. Differentiating $\log q(x_t\mid x_0)$ with respect to $x_t$:

$$ \nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} . $$

Predicting the noise and predicting the score differ by a known, $t$-dependent scalar. That identity is what makes "denoising" and "score matching" the same algorithm in two vocabularies.

The variational bound. The negative log-likelihood of the data is bounded by the ELBO, which decomposes over timesteps:

$$ \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\operatorname{KL}(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)) + \sum_{t>1}\operatorname{KL}(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)) - \log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)\right]. $$

The posterior $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$ is Gaussian with mean $\tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t$ and variance $\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t$. Give $p_\theta$ the same variance and each KL term collapses to a squared distance between means, $\frac{1}{2\tilde\beta_t}\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\|^2$. Substituting $x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ into both means makes the difference proportional to $\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\|^2$, and the coefficient works out to

$$ \frac{\beta_t}{2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\left\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right\|^2 . $$

DDPM then drops that coefficient and trains on $\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\|^2$ with all timesteps equally weighted. This is a reweighting of the bound rather than the bound itself: the exact weights are largest at small $t$, so equal weighting downweights the low-noise terms, and the empirical report is that this improves sample quality. Flagging it explicitly: the per-term KL algebra above is complete, but the constants and the discrete $t=1$ term are taken as standard rather than re-derived.

4. Classifier-free guidance

Start from Bayes: $\nabla_x \log p(x\mid c) = \nabla_x\log p(x) + \nabla_x\log p(c\mid x)$. Define a sharpened conditional by raising the likelihood to a power:

$$ \nabla_x \log\left[p(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{1+w}\right] = \nabla_x\log p(x) + (1+w)\left[\nabla_x\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla_x\log p(x)\right], $$

which is $(1+w)\nabla_x\log p(x\mid c) - w\nabla_x\log p(x)$. Using the score identity, $\epsilon = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_x\log q_t(x)$, the linear combination carries over directly to the noise predictions:

$$ \hat\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing) + s\left[\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing)\right], \qquad s = 1 + w . $$

Training uses a single network for both: the condition $c$ is dropped at random with some probability (typically 10–20%) so the same weights also model the unconditional score. Three regimes follow immediately. $s = 0$ samples the unconditional model, ignoring the condition entirely. $s = 1$ samples the true conditional. $s > 1$ samples a sharpened distribution concentrated on the mode most consistent with $c$ — which raises fidelity and destroys diversity, because the true conditional has been replaced by a power of its likelihood. The failure at very large $s$ is not a sampler bug; it is what happens when you amplify a vector field past the point where its magnitude was calibrated.

Implementation from scratch

A working diffusion sampler with no training, because the data distribution is a small Gaussian mixture whose score is available in closed form. This isolates the reverse process — the part people find hardest to reason about — and shows exactly what a network replaces. The last block demonstrates classifier-free guidance on the same machinery.

```python import numpy as np def beta_schedule(T): """Cosine schedule: noise is added slowly at first, then fast.""" s = 0.008 t = np.linspace(0, 1, T + 1) abar = np.cos((t + s) / (1 + s) * np.pi / 2) ** 2 abar = abar / abar[0] return np.clip(1 - abar[1:] / abar[:-1], 1e-4, 0.999), abar[1:] def mixture(n=8, radius=3.0, sigma0=0.15): """Data p(x_0): 8 Gaussians on a ring. Small enough to score exactly.""" ang = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, n, endpoint=False) return np.stack([radius * np.cos(ang), radius * np.sin(ang)], 1), sigma0 def eps_exact(xt, mu, sigma0, abar_t): """E[eps | x_t] for a Gaussian-mixture q(x_0): closed form, no network.""" var_t = (1 - abar_t) + abar_t * sigma0 ** 2 # q(x_t | x_0) = N(sqrt(abar) x_0, (1-abar) I) m = np.sqrt(abar_t) * mu # (K,2) component means at time t logw = -0.5 * ((xt[:, None, :] - m[None]) ** 2).sum(-1) / var_t w = np.exp(logw - logw.max(1, keepdims=True)) w /= w.sum(1, keepdims=True) # component responsibilities m0 = (np.sqrt(abar_t) * sigma0 ** 2 * xt[:, None, :] + (1 - abar_t) * mu[None]) / var_t x0 = (w[:, :, None] * m0).sum(1) # E[x_0 | x_t] return (xt - np.sqrt(abar_t) * x0) / np.sqrt(1 - abar_t) def eps_component(xt, mu_k, sigma0, abar_t): """The same, conditioned on a single mode: p(x_t | c = mode k).""" var_t = (1 - abar_t) + abar_t * sigma0 ** 2 m0 = (np.sqrt(abar_t) * sigma0 ** 2 * xt + (1 - abar_t) * mu_k) / var_t return (xt - np.sqrt(abar_t) * m0) / np.sqrt(1 - abar_t) def sample(s=1.0, k=0, T=200, n=4000, seed=0): """s=0 ignores the condition, s=1 is the true conditional, s>1 over-sharpens.""" rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) betas, abar = beta_schedule(T) mu, sigma0 = mixture() x = rng.normal(size=(n, 2)) # x_T ~ N(0, I) for t in reversed(range(T)): a, b, ab = 1 - betas[t], betas[t], abar[t] eu = eps_exact(x, mu, sigma0, ab) e = eu + s * (eps_component(x, mu[k], sigma0, ab) - eu) # classifier-free guidance x = (x - b / np.sqrt(1 - ab) * e) / np.sqrt(a) # DDPM posterior mean if t > 0: x = x + np.sqrt(b) * rng.normal(size=x.shape) # posterior noise return x, mu x, mu = sample() r = np.linalg.norm(x, axis=1) print(f"radius: mean={r.mean():.2f} std={r.std():.3f} (true mean 3.00, within-mode std 0.15)") print(f"distance to nearest mode: {np.linalg.norm(x[:,None,:]-mu[None],axis=2).min(1).mean():.3f}") print("guidance scale s | share on mode 0 | mean distance to mode 0") for s in (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0): x, mu = sample(s=s) d = np.linalg.norm(x[:, None, :] - mu[None], axis=2) print(f" {s:4.1f} | {(d.argmin(1)==0).mean():.2f} | {d[:,0].mean():.3f}") ```

What to expect.

```text radius: mean=3.00 std=0.150 (true mean 3.00, within-mode std 0.15) distance to nearest mode: 0.190 guidance scale s | share on mode 0 | mean distance to mode 0 0.0 | 0.13 | 3.737 0.5 | 0.90 | 0.360 1.0 | 1.00 | 0.190 2.0 | 1.00 | 0.188 ```

The unconditional sampler recovers the ring exactly: mean radius 3.00, within-mode spread 0.150, and 99% of samples within 0.5 of a true mode. The guidance table is the point of the exercise. At $s=0$ the condition is ignored and only 13% of samples land on the conditioned mode, which is the 1-in-8 you would expect; at $s=1$ all of them do; and $s=2$ buys almost nothing more, because the conditional distribution has already collapsed to a single mode. Raising $s$ does not make samples more correct, it makes them less diverse.

Worked example

Budgeting a document-understanding system: 50 pages of scanned text, each rendered at 1024×1024, answered across five turns of conversation.

Configuration Tokens / page Prefill FLOPs / page KV / page 50 pages, 5 turns
$P=32$, no compression 1,024 103 GFLOP 96 MiB 51k tokens, 48 GiB cache
$P=16$, no compression 4,096 1.65 TFLOP 384 MiB 205k tokens, 192 GiB
$P=16$, 4× pixel-shuffle 1,024 103 GFLOP 96 MiB 51k tokens, 48 GiB
$P=16$, 64-latent resampler 64 0.4 GFLOP 6 MiB 3.2k tokens, 3 GiB
$P=16$, tiled 4 crops then merged 4,096 1.65 TFLOP 384 MiB 205k tokens, 192 GiB

Read the last column as the binding constraint. Only the resampler configuration fits comfortably in a single request's context; the tiled one is 25× over an 8k window and must be processed page by page with summarisation in between. And note that "no compression at $P=32$" and "pixel-shuffle at $P=16$" reach the same token count by completely different routes — one loses detail at encode time, the other at pooling time — which is why token count alone does not determine quality.

The second-order point is the KV column. At 384 MiB per page, 50 pages is 192 GiB of cache for a single conversation, which exceeds any single device. That is why production document systems do not put fifty pages in one context: they retrieve, or summarise hierarchically, or compress the visual tokens aggressively. The design decision is forced by the arithmetic, not by taste.

Common misconceptions

"A vision-language model has a vision part and a language part"

In the projector and native-fusion designs there is exactly one transformer doing the language modelling; the "vision part" is an encoder plus a linear map. The distinction matters because it predicts where failures come from: most VLM errors are language-model errors, resolved by text-side changes, not by a better ViT.

"Higher resolution always means better understanding"

Resolution buys tokens quadratically, and beyond a point the model's ability to use them does not keep up — the same "lost in the middle" degradation that afflicts long text contexts applies to a long visual prefix. High-resolution models therefore spend most of their engineering on compression, and a well-compressed 1024 image routinely beats a naive 2048 one.

"CLIP compares an image to the class name to classify it"

It compares an embedding to a prompt template's embedding, and the template is worth several points. Prompt ensembling, and the fact that the text encoder was trained on captions rather than single words, are why "a photo of a {}" outperforms "{}". This is a retrieval interaction, not a classifier's decision boundary.

"Guidance scale is a quality dial"

It is a fidelity-versus-diversity dial. Raising $s$ concentrates samples on the mode most consistent with the condition and deletes the rest of the conditional distribution, so a text-to-image system at high guidance produces images that match the prompt more literally, are less varied, more saturated, and more prone to artefacts. There is no setting at which guidance improves both.

"Diffusion models and autoregressive models are different kinds of model"

Both factorise a distribution into a sequence of conditional predictions and both are trained by a variational or cross-entropy bound. The difference is the factorisation — over denoising steps versus over tokens — and it drives the engineering: diffusion samples in parallel over pixels but serially over timesteps, autoregressive models the reverse. That is why hybrids exist and why the boundary is contested.

Exercises

Warm-up (check understanding)

1. How many tokens does a $768\times768$ image produce at $P=16$ and at $P=24$, and what is each token's dimension at $P=16$ with $C=3$?

Solution At $P=16$: $768/16 = 48$, so $48^2 = 2304$ tokens of dimension $16^2 \cdot 3 = 768$. At $P=24$: $768/24 = 32$, so 1024 tokens of dimension $24^2\cdot3 = 1728$. Moving from 24 to 16 is a 2.25-fold token increase and a 5-fold attention increase.

2. Why does zero-shot CLIP classification need the text embeddings of the class names at inference time?

Solution Because the "classifier" is defined by comparing the image embedding to text embeddings; there are no learned classification weights. Computing $K$ text embeddings at inference is what makes the label set changeable without retraining, and it is also why class *names* matter as much as class concepts.

3. State the score identity that links noise prediction to score matching.

Solution $\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0)/(1-\bar\alpha_t) = -\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. So $\epsilon_\theta$ and the score differ only by the known scalar $-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$, and predicting one is predicting the other.

Core (do the math)

4. A 24-layer model with $d = 1024$ receives a 1024×1024 image at $P=16$ plus 500 text tokens. Compute the attention FLOPs per layer and the fraction contributed by the image.

Solution $N = 4096$, $T = 4596$. Per layer, $4T^2d = 4 \times 4596^2 \times 1024 = 8.65 \times 10^{10}$ FLOPs. With text only ($T = 500$) it would be $4 \times 500^2 \times 1024 = 1.02 \times 10^9$. So the image contributes about 98.8% of the attention compute — which is why reducing $N$ is the highest-leverage change available.

5. Derive the noise-prediction KL coefficient and explain what dropping it does.

Solution With matched variances the per-step KL is $\frac{1}{2\tilde\beta_t}\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\|^2$. Substituting the $x_0$ reparametrisation, both means differ by a factor times $(\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta)$, giving coefficient $\beta_t/(2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}))$. Dropping it makes every timestep equally weighted; since the true weights are largest at small $t$, dropping them downweights the low-noise terms, and the DDPM paper reports that this improves sample quality.

6. With $s = 3$ in classifier-free guidance, what distribution is being sampled relative to the true conditional, and what does that predict about diversity?

Solution The score being followed is $\nabla\log p(x) + (1+w)\nabla\log p(c\mid x)$ with $w = s-1 = 2$, which corresponds to sampling $p(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{1+w}$ — the conditional likelihood raised to a power, a sharpened version of $p(x\mid c)$. Diversity falls because the distribution has been concentrated on its dominant mode; at extremes, samples leave the data manifold because the amplified field is no longer calibrated.

7. A one-minute video at 8 fps with 256 tokens per frame. How many tokens, and what two levers reduce it?

Solution $60 \times 8 \times 256 = 122{,}880$ tokens. The levers are frame subsampling (8 fps to 1 fps is an 8× reduction) and spatial or temporal compression per frame (a 4× pixel-shuffle is a 16× reduction; a tubelet spanning 4 frames divides the temporal count by 4). Combining both routinely gets an hour of video into tens of thousands of tokens.

Stretch (build or break it)

8. Modify the diffusion implementation to use 20 steps instead of 200 and report how the sample distribution degrades. Then swap the cosine schedule for a linear one and compare.

Hint With an exact score, 20 steps produce visible discretisation error: the reverse process undershoots the modes and the radius distribution widens or biases. Compare the mean radius and the fraction within 0.5 of a mode. The cosine schedule degrades more gracefully at low step counts because it spends more steps in the high-noise regime where the trajectory is longest — which is precisely why schedulers are a research topic in real systems.

9. Implement the projector route end to end on a small open ViT: patchify, encode, project, splice into a text sequence. Then ablate the visual tokens (replace them with zeros) and confirm the answer changes.

Hint The ablation is the visual analogue of the RAG oracle test: if zeroing every visual token leaves the answer unchanged, the model is answering from text alone, and any evaluation of "vision" is measuring the text prior. Run it on every multimodal benchmark you build, because a surprising number of reported gains survive it.

Self-test

  1. Give the token count and token dimension for a $512\times512$ image at $P=16$.
  2. Write CLIP's symmetric loss and say what supplies the negatives.
  3. Name the three fusion designs and the main cost each pays.
  4. Why does halving the patch size sixteenfold the attention cost?
  5. Derive $\hat\epsilon$ for classifier-free guidance and give the three regimes of $s$.
  6. What is the difference between a VAE latent and a VQ codebook index, and why does the latter enable generation?
  7. Name three settled multimodal results and three contested ones.
Answers 1. $512/16 = 32$, so $32^2 = 1024$ tokens of dimension $16^2 \cdot 3 = 768$. 2. $\frac{1}{2}[\frac{1}{B}\sum_i -\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{s_{ij}}} + \frac{1}{B}\sum_j -\log\frac{e^{s_{jj}}}{\sum_i e^{s_{ij}}}]$. The other items in the batch supply $2(B-1)$ negatives per pair for free — no labels are needed because web captions are the positives. 3. Projector: pays context length. Cross-attention: pays a compression bottleneck and architectural complexity. Native fusion: pays tokeniser lossiness and training cost. 4. $N$ quadruples when $P$ halves (each dimension doubles), and attention is $O(N^2)$ — so $4^2 = 16$. 5. $\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing) + s[\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)]$. $s=0$ ignores the condition, $s=1$ is the true conditional, $s>1$ sharpens and destroys diversity. 6. A VAE latent is continuous, so modelling it requires a continuous density model; a VQ index is one of $K$ discrete symbols, so it can be predicted with cross-entropy and sampled like a token. 7. Settled: patch tokenisation, CLIP-style contrastive alignment, token-count-drives-cost, the variational diffusion bound, CFG. Contested: optimal fusion architecture, discrete versus continuous image tokens, autoregressive versus diffusion for image generation.

Further reading

  • Dosovitskiy et al., "An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale" (ICLR 2021). The ViT paper. Read the ablations on pretraining data scale — they are the evidence that the transformer's weakness on images is a data problem, not an architectural one.
  • Radford et al., "Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision" (ICML 2021). CLIP. Extract the prompt-ensembling result and the batch-size discussion; both are load-bearing and both are easy to skip.
  • Liu et al., "Visual Instruction Tuning" (NeurIPS 2023). LLaVA, the projector route end to end; the two-stage training recipe is the part worth copying.
  • Alayrac et al., "Flamingo: a Visual Language Model for Few-Shot Learning" (NeurIPS 2022). Cross-attention with a resampler and a frozen LLM; read for what the gating does and why freezing the language model is a deliberate trade.
  • Ho, Jain, and Abbeel, "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" (NeurIPS 2020). The variational bound, the reweighting, and the sampling procedure. Work through Appendix A against this module's derivation.
  • Ho and Salimans, "Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance" (2022). Two pages of algebra that changed image generation; read it directly rather than from secondary summaries.
  • Rombach et al., "High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models" (CVPR 2022). Where diffusion moves into a compressed latent space — the direct analogue of patch tokenisation for the generative side.
