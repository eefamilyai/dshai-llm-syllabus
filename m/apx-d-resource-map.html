Resource Map

What to read, watch, and run, what each is best for, and what has gone stale.

on this page

How to use this map

Each entry says what the resource is best for, where it fits against the module ids, and — where it applies — what has dated. The bias throughout is toward free, primary, and still-maintained. A resource that is excellent but six years old is listed with its age stated, because knowing a source predates RoPE, GQA, and instruction tuning is the difference between using it and being misled by it.

Books

Deep Learning — I. Goodfellow, Y. Bengio, A. Courville (2016). Still the most rigorous treatment of the fundamentals: optimisation, regularisation, backpropagation, and the mathematics of representation. Best for M01–M09. Dated: it predates transformers and everything after 2016, so it says nothing useful about attention, tokenization at scale, or alignment.

Mathematics for Machine Learning — M. Deisenroth, A. Faisal, C. Ong (2020, free PDF). The best single source of the linear algebra, calculus, and probability the early modules assume, written for exactly this audience. Best for M01–M04.

Dive into Deep Learning — A. Zhang, Z. Lipton, M. Li, A. Smola (d2l.ai, continuously updated). Runnable notebooks for every concept, which makes it the best companion for actually building M06–M13 rather than reading about them.

Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction and Advanced Topics — K. Murphy (2022, 2023, free PDFs). Comprehensive and current; use the first for M03 and M05, the second for anything involving generative models or inference.

Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction — R. Sutton, A. Barto (2nd ed., 2018, free online). The canonical RL text; the policy-gradient chapter is the foundation for M18, though it says nothing about RLHF or language models.

The Elements of Statistical Learning — T. Hastie, R. Tibshirani, J. Friedman (free PDF). The statistical-learning reference for M05; the bias–variance and model-assessment chapters are still the clearest treatment anywhere.

Speech and Language Processing — D. Jurafsky, J. Martin (3rd ed. drafts, free). Broad NLP coverage; the chapters on neural sequence models and pretraining are useful for M11–M16. Dated in its classical sections, and the LLM chapters move quickly enough that the draft you read may be a year behind.

Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch) — S. Raschka (2024). The clearest end-to-end book-length walkthrough of pretraining, fine-tuning, and instruction tuning a small model; the closest published companion to M28 rungs 3–5.

Introduction to Information Retrieval — C. Manning, P. Raghavan, H. Schütze (free online). Dated (2008) but the foundations of indexing, ranking, and evaluation are exactly what M22 assumes and never explains.

Designing Machine Learning Systems — C. Huyen (2022). Deployment, monitoring, and data engineering; the best bridge from M19–M22 to what a production system actually looks like.

Lecture series and courses

Neural Networks: Zero to Hero — A. Karpathy (2022–2023, free). Builds micrograd, a tokenizer, and a transformer live. The single highest-value video resource in this curriculum: M07, M09, M14, M15. Watch the debugging, not the results.

CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch — Stanford (2024–2025). The closest thing to a university course built for exactly this syllabus: tokenization, transformers, scaling, inference, and systems, with assignments. Best for M14–M19 and M24–M26.

CS224N: Natural Language Processing with Deep Learning — Stanford (updated yearly). Strong on attention, pretraining, and evaluation; the assignment on dependency parsing is optional for this curriculum, the transformer lectures are not. M12, M15.

CS231n: Convolutional Neural Networks for Visual Recognition — Stanford (2016 lectures still posted). Dated in its frameworks but the convolution arithmetic, receptive-field, and backprop-through-conv explanations are still the clearest. M10.

Practical Deep Learning for Coders — fast.ai (J. Howard). Top-down and framework-first: you train a model in lesson one. Excellent for motivation and for M06 intuition, weak on derivations; do not use it as a substitute for M01–M04.

Spinning Up in Deep RL — J. Achiam / OpenAI (2018, free). The cleanest introduction to policy gradients, GAE, and PPO. Best for M18's RL fundamentals. Dated: no language-model RLHF content, and its PPO implementation predates the trl-style LLM variants.

Introduction to Deep Learning — CMU 11-785 (B. Raj). Long, rigorous, math-heavy lectures covering the material in M06–M13 more slowly than any other public course.

Deep Learning lecture series — DeepMind/UCL (2020). Good for the research-frontier framing of M08 and M21; several lectures are now historical.

Essence of Linear Algebra and Neural Networks series — 3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson). The best visual intuition available for M01 and M07, and the animated equivalent of the figures in this curriculum.

Blog posts and explainers

The Illustrated Transformer — J. Alammar (2018). Still the best single explanation of M12's attention mechanism. Dated: no RoPE, no GQA, no pre-LN block, and its positional encoding is sinusoidal.

The Annotated Transformer — A. Rush et al., Harvard NLP. The paper and a line-by-line PyTorch implementation side by side; the best way to move from reading M12 to writing it.

Lil'Log — L. Weng (continuously updated). Survey-quality posts on diffusion, RLHF, agents, hallucination, and evaluation. Use as the map that tells you which primary paper to read next for M18, M22, M23, M29.

Distill.pub (2017–2021, archived). The best interactive explanations ever produced in this field: momentum, feature visualisation, circuits, and the "Why Momentum Really Works" article. Archived, so nothing new, but the archive is permanent and still the standard for explanatory quality.

Transformer Circuits Thread — Anthropic. The superposition, circuits, and induction-head work, written for readers rather than reviewers. Essential for M21.

Neel Nanda's interpretability writing, including his list of concrete open problems. The most actionable entry point into M21 and the interpretability items in M29.

Sebastian Raschka's Ahead of AI — monthly, technically careful, opinionated in a useful way. Good for keeping M16–M19 current without reading everything.

The Bitter Lesson — R. Sutton (2019). One page that frames the entire scaling debate in M16 and M29. Read it as an argument, not a law.

Epoch AI's research notes on data availability, compute trends, and scaling. The best quantitative source for the data-scarcity and energy items in M29, and honest about its own assumptions.

Hugging Face blog and course. Practical, current, and often the first place a new technique is explained accessibly. Use for M17, M19, M26 after the primary source.

Papers to keep by the desk

Two of these are the papers most often cited and least often read, and they are worth reading for opposite reasons. Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (GPT-2, Radford et al., 2019) is the clearest published account of how a modern corpus and tokenizer are assembled, and its zero-shot experiments define the evaluation questions M15 and M20 still argue about; read it for the data pipeline, not the model. Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla, Hoffmann et al., 2022) is the paper whose central result had to be settled by training a model because three fitting methods disagreed — a case study in the method of M27 as much as in the arithmetic of M16.

The full annotated list is Appendix C; these five are the ones you will actually reopen: Attention Is All You Need for shapes, Chinchilla for allocation, LoRA for the parameter arithmetic, Direct Preference Optimization for the objective, and Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving for the serving arithmetic.

Codebases to read

micrograd (Karpathy). 150 lines that implement reverse-mode autodiff. Read it before writing M28 rung 1, then write yours without looking.

nanoGPT (Karpathy). A complete GPT training script in a few hundred lines. The reference implementation for M28 rung 3; extract the training loop, the loss estimation, and the learning-rate schedule rather than copying.

minbpe (Karpathy). Byte-level BPE in ~200 lines with a walkthrough; the companion to M14 and rung 2.

The Annotated Transformer (Harvard NLP). Best for seeing exactly where each tensor is created in M12.

transformers (Hugging Face). Read the model files (modeling_llama.py, modeling_mistral.py) rather than the docs: they are the most accurate specification of what modern blocks actually contain.

litgpt (Lightning AI). A cleaner, more configurable full pretraining pipeline than nanoGPT, with LoRA and evaluation wired in; useful for M16–M17.

TRL (Hugging Face). Reference implementations of SFT, reward modelling, PPO, DPO, and GRPO. Read the DPO loss implementation after writing M28 rung 5 yourself.

vLLM. For M19: read the paged-attention block manager and the scheduler; the rest is engineering you can skim.

llama.cpp / ggml. For M26: the clearest codebase for quantization formats actually used in deployment.

DeepSpeed and Megatron-LM. For M25: ZeRO stage implementations and the tensor-parallel layer structure, respectively.

triton (tutorials and python/triton/language). For M24 and rung 8: the softmax and fused-attention tutorials are the ones that make the roofline concrete.

flash-attention. For M24: read the paper first, then the kernel; the tiling and online-softmax tricks are the whole content.

Tools

PyTorch — the default for everything in this curriculum; its autograd is what M07 explains.

JAX — worth learning for M09 and M25, because its functional view makes sharding and compilation explicit in a way PyTorch hides.

CUDA C++ Programming Guide with the introductory CUDA tutorial — the canonical first kernel: thread indexing, grids and blocks, and shared memory. Do it once in C++ before moving to Triton, because Triton hides exactly the concepts you need to understand M24's occupancy and tiling arguments.

Triton — the practical way to write GPU kernels without CUDA C; the right tool for M28 rung 8.

CUDA toolkit with Nsight Systems and Nsight Compute — profilers, not optional: M24's claims about bandwidth and occupancy are unverifiable without them.

Weights & Biases or TensorBoard — experiment tracking; the difference between having a M28 artefact and having a folder of numbers you cannot reproduce.

lm-evaluation-harness (EleutherAI) — the standard benchmark runner; use it for M20 and rung 9, then read its task implementations to see how much of a score is parsing.

HELM and Inspect (UK AI Safety Institute) — fuller evaluation frameworks; Inspect is the more current of the two for agentic and safety evaluations.

einops — readable tensor reshaping; it makes the shape-tracking habit of M28 rung 1 nearly automatic.

What is dated, and how to tell

Five resources here are older than the techniques they are most often used to explain, and each is worth using with a specific correction.

Goodfellow's Deep Learning (2016) predates attention-based language models entirely. Use it for optimisation, regularisation, and the mathematics; do not use it for architecture.

The Illustrated Transformer (2018) shows a post-LN block with sinusoidal positions. Every production model now uses pre-LN or RMSNorm, RoPE, and often GQA. The attention explanation survives; the block diagram does not.

Spinning Up (2018) teaches RL without language models. The algorithms transfer; the reward-model and KL-penalty machinery of M18 is absent.

CS231n (2016 lectures) uses frameworks that no longer exist and predates transformers in vision. The convolution and backprop arithmetic is unchanged.

fast.ai ties its explanations to its own library's abstractions, which is fast to learn and slow to unlearn. Use it for momentum, not for derivations.

The general rule: a resource is safe for mathematics indefinitely and stale for architectures after about two years. When a source's date is older than the current model generation, check every architectural claim against a recent model file before trusting it.
