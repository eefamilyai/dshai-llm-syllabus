Optimization for Deep Learning

Why descent works on a surface with no guarantees, and what each optimizer actually fixes.

on this page

Why this module exists

Every training run is one optimisation problem solved badly on purpose: nobody computes the minimum, everybody takes a few hundred thousand steps in a direction that is merely downhill. Without this module the details are folklore — why the learning rate must be small to survive the sharpest direction, why momentum helps in ravines and hurts when the learning rate is already maximal, exactly what Adam's bias correction divides by, why warmup exists, and why AdamW is not the same algorithm as Adam with weight decay. Several claims here are genuinely contested, notably whether Adam generalises worse than SGD, and the module marks them as contested rather than picking a side. The material builds directly on the Hessian and conditioning of M02 and M01, and it is exploited by every later module that trains something: M08 (dynamics), M16 (pretraining at scale), M17 (fine-tuning), M18 (policy optimisation).

Prerequisites check

  1. What does $\mathbf{H} > 0$ tell you about a critical point?
  2. What is the largest step $\eta$ for which $x_{k+1} = (1-\eta\lambda)x_k$ converges to zero?
  3. Expand $f(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta})$ to second order.
  4. If $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(1,100)$, what is $\kappa$ and what does it imply for descent?
  5. Write the Newton step for a positive-definite quadratic.
Answers 1. That the point is a strict local minimum — the second-order model is a bowl. 2. $|1-\eta\lambda| < 1$, i.e. $0 < \eta < 2/\lambda$. 3. $f + \nabla f^\top\boldsymbol{\delta} + \frac12\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\delta} + o(\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|^2)$. 4. $\kappa = 100$: the steepest curvature is 100× the shallowest, so the stable step is set by $\lambda = 100$ while progress along $\lambda = 1$ is 100× slower. 5. $\boldsymbol{\delta} = -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$, exact in one step for a quadratic. If you miss 1 or 2, revisit **M02**'s Taylor expansion and **M01**'s conditioning derivation. Nothing here assumes convexity except where it says so.

Notation and conventions

$\eta$ learning rate (step size)
$\mathbf{m}_t$, $\mathbf{v}_t$ first and second moment estimates

Optimisation here means stochastic, first-order, non-convex minimisation. The analysis is done on quadratics because that is where everything is computable; the extension to general smooth functions is local, replacing eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ with those of $\mathbf{H}$ at the current point.

Core concepts

Why non-convex optimisation works at all

(1) Intuition. In two dimensions a non-convex surface is a mountain range you must escape; in ten million dimensions it is the opposite — bad stationary points are rare, and almost every direction leads downhill.

(2) Formal. A point is stationary if $\nabla\mathcal{L} = 0$; it is a local minimum if $\mathbf{H} > 0$, a saddle if $\mathbf{H}$ has both signs, and a maximum if $\mathbf{H} < 0$. Random matrix theory says a random symmetric matrix in $n$ dimensions has, with high probability, eigenvalues on both sides of zero — so a random stationary point is overwhelmingly a saddle, and saddles have escape directions.

(3) Why. The empirical case is now stronger than the theory: overparameterised networks have far more parameters than data, so most directions can be made flat or descending, and stochastic gradient noise supplies the perturbation that rolls off a plateau. The honest statement is that we have good reasons and strong empirical regularities, not a proof. High loss barriers do exist and matter for generalisation, which is why M08 treats loss-landscape geometry as an open research area rather than a settled result.

(4) Micro-example. In $n$ dimensions, a critical point whose Hessian has a random spectrum is a minimum only if all $n$ eigenvalues are positive — probability $2^{-n}$ for symmetric signs. At $n=30$ that is one in a billion, which is the whole argument in one line.

Convexity and its guarantees

(1) Intuition. A convex function is a bowl with one bottom: every local minimum is global, and the straight line between any two points lies above the graph.

(2) Formal. $\mathcal{L}$ is convex if $\mathcal{L}(t\mathbf{x}+(1-t)\mathbf{y}) \le t\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})+(1-t)\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y})$ for $t\in[0,1]$; for twice-differentiable functions, $\mathbf{H} \ge 0$ everywhere. Then any stationary point is global, and gradient descent with $\eta \le 1/L$ (where $L$ is the Lipschitz constant of the gradient) converges at $O(1/k)$, or $O((1-\mu/L)^k)$ for $\mu$-strongly convex functions.

(3) Why. Convexity buys a certificate: the duality gap tells you how far from optimal you are, so you can stop with a guarantee. Deep learning gives that up entirely — the training loss is not convex in the weights, and there is no certificate. What survives is the local theory: near a minimum, the quadratic model is accurate, and the eigenvalue structure of $\mathbf{H}$ governs everything.

(4) Micro-example. $f(w) = \log(1+e^{-w})$ (logistic loss on one example, label $+1$) is convex with $f''(w) = \sigma(w)(1-\sigma(w)) \le 1/4$, so $\eta \le 4$ suffices. Composing it with a two-layer network makes it non-convex, and the same $\eta$ no longer has a proof — only the local curvature bound, which can be arbitrarily large.

Gradient descent as Euler discretisation, and the step-size bound

(1) Intuition. Gradient flow is a ball rolling downhill with velocity set by the slope. Gradient descent is what you get by sampling that continuous motion at intervals of $\eta$ — so $\eta$ is a time step, and too large a time step makes the simulation explode.

(2) Formal. Gradient flow is $d\boldsymbol{\theta}/dt = -\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. Forward Euler with step $\eta$ gives $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$ — gradient descent. For a quadratic $\mathcal{L} = \frac12\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}$ the iteration is $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = (\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$, which converges iff $|1-\eta\lambda_i| < 1$ for every eigenvalue, i.e.

$$0 < \eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} .$$

(3) Why. This single inequality explains most learning-rate behaviour: the step is set by the sharpest direction, even though progress is limited by the shallowest. It also explains why the bound is a cliff rather than a slope — at $\eta = 2/\lambda_{\max}$ the sharpest mode has multiplier exactly $-1$ and oscillates forever without decaying, and just above it, it grows.

(4) Micro-example. $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(1,100)$, so $\eta < 0.02$. At $\eta = 0.019$ after 60 steps the iterate is $(0.316, 0.0018)$; at $\eta = 0.02$ the second coordinate is stuck at exactly its initial value $1.0$ (multiplier $-1$); at $\eta = 0.025$ it reaches $3.7\times10^{10}$ — divergence, from a 25% increase in step size.

Click anywhere on the surface to drop a ball, then raise the learning rate until it diverges and lower it until it crawls. Switch to momentum and watch the trajectory overshoot and then settle, and switch optimizers to compare how each one handles the same valley.

Condition number and ravines

(1) Intuition. A ravine is a valley that is steep across and flat along, so you oscillate across it while creeping forward. The ratio of the two curvatures is the condition number.

(2) Formal. With $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$, the fastest mode decays like $(1-\eta\lambda_{\max})$ and the slowest like $(1-\eta\lambda_{\min})$; at the largest stable step $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$ the slow mode contracts by $(1 - 1/\kappa)$ per step, so iterations to reach error $\varepsilon$ scale as $\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)$.

(3) Why. This is why the shape of the loss matters as much as its value. Batch normalisation, residual connections, and careful initialisation (M06, M07) are all, at bottom, attempts to make the Hessian better conditioned rather than to make the gradient smaller.

(4) Micro-example. $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(1,100)$ with tolerance $\|\mathbf{g}\|<10^{-3}$: gradient descent at $\eta = 0.019$ needs 362 iterations; at $\eta = 0.005$ it needs 1380. The ratio $3.8$ matches the ratio of step sizes, because the iteration count is $\kappa$ times a constant and $\kappa$ is fixed — a smaller step just multiplies the constant.

Momentum, and Nesterov's correction

(1) Intuition. Momentum is a heavy ball: it accumulates velocity along directions that keep pointing the same way (the valley floor) and cancels the alternating directions that flip sign each step (across the ravine). Equivalently it is an exponential moving average of gradients, which reduces the variance of the update.

(2) Formal. Heavy ball: $\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\mathbf{v}_t + \mathbf{g}_t$, $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\mathbf{v}_{t+1}$. Nesterov: evaluate the gradient at the look-ahead point, $\mathbf{g}_t = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\beta\mathbf{v}_t)$, then apply the same recursion.

(3) Why. Along a consistent direction the velocity converges to $\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$, so the effective learning rate is amplified by $1/(1-\beta)$ — with $\beta = 0.9$, tenfold. That is why momentum lets you use a smaller nominal $\eta$ and gain speed, and why adding momentum to an already-tuned $\eta$ diverges: you have silently multiplied the step by ten. Nesterov's look-ahead is a correction term: because it measures the gradient where the ball is going, it damps the overshoot and gives an $O(1/k^2)$ rate for smooth convex functions versus $O(1/k)$ for plain momentum.

(4) Micro-example. $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(1,100)$ at $\eta = 0.019$: plain SGD needs 362 iterations, $\beta = 0.9$ momentum needs 220. Pushed to $\beta = 0.99$ the same $\eta$ becomes unstable and after 2000 iterations the gradient norm is still $0.083$ rather than converging. The optimal coefficient for $\kappa = 100$ is $\beta^{*} = ((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^2 = 0.669$, not a round number — the tuning knob has structure, not a default.

The adaptive family: AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW

(1) Intuition. Instead of one global step, give every coordinate its own. A coordinate whose gradient has been consistently large gets a small step; one with a tiny gradient gets a large one. This is what you want when parameters live at wildly different scales.

(2) Formal. AdaGrad: $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t$, $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\,\mathbf{g}_t/(\sqrt{\mathbf{h}_t}+\epsilon)$. RMSProp replaces the sum by an exponential average, $\mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t$. Adam adds the first moment and bias correction:

$$\mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t,$$

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1-\beta_1^t}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1-\beta_2^t}.$$

(3) Why. AdaGrad's cumulative denominator only grows, so its step decays like $1/\sqrt{t}$ and stops learning in non-stationary problems — fixed by RMSProp's exponential window. Adam's bias correction exists because both moment estimates are initialised at zero: $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = (1-\beta_1^t)\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}]$, so dividing by $1-\beta_1^t$ makes the estimate unbiased at every step. Without it, early steps are damped by a factor of about $1-\beta_1 = 0.1$ and the first update is roughly $1/\sqrt{1-\beta_2}\approx 31.6$ times too large in the second-moment normalisation.

(4) Micro-example. $\mathcal{L}(w) = \frac12w^2$, $w_0 = 1$, $\eta = 0.1$, $\beta_{1,2} = 0.9, 0.999$. Step 1: $\mathbf{m} = 0.1$, $\mathbf{v} = 0.001$, $\hat m = 1.0$, $\hat v = 1.0$, update $0.1$, $w = 0.9$. Step 2: $\mathbf{m} = 0.18$, $\hat m = 0.9474$; $\mathbf{v} = 0.001809$, $\hat v = 0.90495$; update $0.0996$, $w = 0.8004$. Step 3: update $0.0988$, $w = 0.7016$. Without bias correction the first three updates are $0.316$, $0.414$, $0.432$ — the walk overshoots past the minimum to $w = -0.16$ by step 3.

Weight decay versus L2, and the AdamW algebra

(1) Intuition. "Shrink the weights a little each step" and "add a penalty on weight size to the loss" sound like the same idea and are not, once the optimiser rescales gradients.

(2) Formal. L2 regularisation adds $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$ to the loss, so the gradient becomes $\mathbf{g}_t + \lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$. Weight decay multiplies the parameters directly: $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = (1-\eta\lambda)\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\cdot(\text{optimizer step on } \mathbf{g}_t)$. These are identical for plain SGD and differ for any adaptive method.

(3) Why. Under Adam the L2 gradient $\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}$ enters $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, so the effective shrinkage is $\eta\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}/\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}$, which is smaller for parameters with large gradients and can even change sign when the first moment is dominated by $\mathbf{g}$. Decoupled decay shrinks every parameter by the same relative amount per step, independent of the adaptive denominator — which is what "weight decay" was supposed to mean.

(4) Micro-example. A parameter with $\hat v = 1$ gets shrinkage $\eta\lambda\theta$ under Adam+L2 and $0.01\eta\lambda\theta$ under Adam+L2 if $\hat v = 10^4$ — a 100× difference in regularisation for two parameters in the same layer. With decoupled decay both shrink by $\eta\lambda\theta$ per step. The practical consequence, reported by Loshchilov and Hutter, is that AdamW's optimal $\lambda$ is roughly $\eta$-independent while Adam+L2's is not.

Schedules and warmup

(1) Intuition. A fixed step size is wrong at both ends: too big at the start when curvature is unknown, too big at the end when you want to settle into a minimum. A schedule spends a large step early and a small one late.

(2) Formal. With peak $\eta_{\max}$, warmup steps $T_w$, and total steps $T$:

$$\text{cosine: } \eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\left(1+\cos\pi\frac{t-T_w}{T-T_w}\right), \qquad \text{linear: } \eta_t = \eta_{\max}\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right),$$

$$\text{inverse sqrt: } \eta_t = \eta_{\max}/\sqrt{t}, \qquad \text{warmup: } \eta_t = \eta_{\max}\,t/T_w \text{ for } t \le T_w .$$

(3) Why. Three separate reasons, often conflated. Inverse-square-root is the schedule that stochastic approximation theory justifies (the noise in the gradient estimate shrinks as $1/\sqrt{t}$, so the step should too). Cosine and linear decay are empirical: they reliably improve final loss and are nearly equivalent in practice. Warmup exists because at $t=0$ Adam's second-moment estimate is built from one or two gradients and is wildly inaccurate, because large initial updates can destroy randomly initialised features, and because adaptive preconditioners are unstable before their statistics have accumulated. Warmup is best understood as protecting the optimiser's own estimates, not the model.

(4) Micro-example. $\eta_{\max} = 3\times10^{-4}$, $T_w = 2000$, $T = 100{,}000$: at $t = 1000$ warmup gives $1.5\times10^{-4}$; at $t = 51{,}000$ cosine gives $1.5\times10^{-4}$ again; at $t = 100{,}000$ it gives $\eta_{\min}$. The same learning rate at three very different points in training, which is why "what learning rate did you use" is an incomplete question.

Drag the warmup length and peak learning rate and watch both the schedule and the resulting step-size series. Push warmup to zero and watch the first updates spike, which is the failure warmup prevents.

Saddle points, plateaus, and high-dimensional intuition

(1) Intuition. Saddles are more common than minima in high dimension, plateaus are regions where the gradient is near zero but the loss is not low, and both are more dangerous than the local minima that textbooks warn about.

(2) Formal. A saddle has $\mathbf{H}$ with mixed signs; Newton's method is attracted to it rather than repelled, since the step $-(\mathbf{H}+\epsilon\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{g}$ can point toward it. Plateau escape time for gradient descent scales as $1/\sqrt{\epsilon}$ in the worst case for perturbation $\epsilon$, and stochastic noise provides the perturbation.

(3) Why. This is why second-order methods are not automatically better, and why stochastic gradient noise is a feature: it is the mechanism that escapes flat regions. It is also why batch size and learning rate interact with the shape of the noise, not just its magnitude (M08).

(4) Micro-example. $\mathcal{L}(x,y) = x^2 - 0.1y^2$: at the origin $\mathbf{H} = \operatorname{diag}(2,-0.2)$, a saddle. Newton's step from $(0.1, 0.1)$ is $-(0.2, -0.02)/(2,-0.2) = (-0.1, 0.1)$, which moves toward the origin along $x$ and away along $y$ — converging to the saddle in one coordinate while the loss decreases.

Second-order methods: Newton, Gauss–Newton, natural gradient, K-FAC

(1) Intuition. First-order methods see slope only and must infer scale by trial; second-order methods see curvature and take the step that would be right for a quadratic. They are invariant to rescaling of parameters, which is exactly what gradient descent is bad at.

(2) Formal. Newton: $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{g}$ — dimensionless $\eta$, one step exact on a quadratic. Gauss–Newton for a least-squares loss $\frac12\|\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^2$: $\mathbf{H}\approx\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$ with $\mathbf{J} = \partial\mathbf{r}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}$, which is positive semi-definite and needs no second derivatives. Natural gradient: $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{g}$ with $\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p\,\nabla\log p^\top]$ the Fisher information — the steepest descent direction under the KL geometry of distributions rather than the Euclidean geometry of parameters. K-FAC approximates $\mathbf{F}$ per layer as $\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{G}$ (Kronecker factors), inverting each small factor instead of the huge one.

(3) Why. Newton's method is affine-invariant: it gives the same trajectory for $\mathbf{x}$ and $2\mathbf{x}$, which is precisely what a badly scaled network needs. The problem is cost — $\mathbf{H}$ has $n^2$ entries, $n$ Hessian-vector products to build, $O(n^3)$ to invert. Gauss–Newton halves the work and guarantees positive definiteness; natural gradient replaces the arbitrary parameter metric with a statistical one; K-FAC makes the Kronecker structure exploitable. All three are answers to "how do we afford curvature", and all three succeed on modest models and lose to well-tuned Adam at frontier scale.

(4) Micro-example. $n = 10^8$: $\mathbf{H}$ has $10^{16}$ entries (80 petabytes in fp64), building it needs $10^8$ backward passes, and inverting it costs $10^{24}$ operations. Even a Hessian-vector product at ~2× the cost of a gradient is only affordable a few times per update, which is why practical preconditioners are diagonal (Adam) or Kronecker-factored (K-FAC) or low-rank.

Lagrange multipliers

(1) Intuition. When you must minimise subject to a constraint, the gradient at the optimum need not vanish — it only needs to be a combination of the constraint gradients. The multipliers say how much the objective would improve if you relaxed each constraint.

(2) Formal. For $\min f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $h_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, form $\mathcal{L} = f + \sum_i\lambda_ih_i$; stationarity gives $\nabla f + \sum_i\lambda_i\nabla h_i = 0$ together with $h_i = 0$. For inequality constraints $g_j(\mathbf{x})\le0$ the KKT conditions add $\lambda_j \ge 0$ and complementary slackness $\lambda_jg_j = 0$.

(3) Why. Constrained formulations are everywhere in this curriculum: the PCA variance maximisation in M01 is constrained ($\|\mathbf{w}\| = 1$); the maximum-entropy derivation in M03 is constrained (normalisation plus two moments); trust-region methods constrain the KL between successive policies in M18; and the dual of the SVM in M05 is a Lagrangian relaxation that produces the kernel trick. The multiplier is also an economic quantity — the shadow price of the constraint.

(4) Micro-example. Minimise $x^2+y^2$ subject to $x+y = 1$. $\mathcal{L} = x^2+y^2+\lambda(x+y-1)$; stationarity gives $2x+\lambda = 0$, $2y+\lambda = 0$, so $x = y = -\lambda/2$; the constraint gives $x = y = 0.5$ and $\lambda = -1$. The objective is $0.5$, and $\lambda = -1$ says each unit by which the constraint constant rises decreases the objective by 1.

Implicit regularisation of SGD

(1) Intuition. Gradient descent does not merely find a minimum; among the many minima a network has, it prefers some over others, without any explicit penalty. That preference is a regulariser you did not ask for.

(2) Formal. For an underdetermined linear least-squares problem, gradient descent from $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0 = 0$ converges to the minimum-norm solution $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*} = \mathbf{X}^+\mathbf{y}$ — the same solution as the pseudoinverse (M01), without any norm penalty in the objective. For logistic loss on separable data, gradient descent diverges in norm while its direction converges to the max-margin separator; the finite step count acts as the effective regularisation.

(3) Why. This is the cleanest explanation for why training loss and test loss decouple: the algorithm's implicit bias chooses among interpolating solutions, and small batches, early stopping, and small learning rates all change which one. It is also why "the model has enough capacity to memorise the data" is not the same claim as "it will", and why the generalisation story in M08 is about the algorithm and not only the hypothesis class.

(4) Micro-example. $\mathbf{X} = (1,1)^\top$ in one dimension, target $y = (1,1)$: every weight $w$ with $w = 1$ fits exactly, and there is a unique interpolating solution, so nothing to choose. Use two features and one example, $\mathbf{x} = (1,1)$, $y = 1$: the solutions are all $\mathbf{w}$ with $w_1+w_2 = 1$, an affine set. Gradient descent from $\mathbf{0}$ converges to $(0.5,0.5)$ — the minimum-norm point — while a different initialisation converges elsewhere. Same data, same loss, different function.

The algorithm, step by step

The algorithm is the modern default: AdamW with warmup and cosine decay, plus global-norm gradient clipping. Every line below is something you set, and every one of them has a failure mode.

  1. Initialise $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$, moment buffers $\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, step counter $t = 1$.
  2. Compute the minibatch gradient $\mathbf{g}_t = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$.
  3. Clip by global norm: if $\|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2 > c$, scale $\mathbf{g}_t \leftarrow \mathbf{g}_t\,c/\|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2$. This bounds the step but does not change its direction.
  4. Update the moments: $\mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t$ and $\mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t$.
  5. Bias-correct: $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \mathbf{m}_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$, $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \mathbf{v}_t/(1-\beta_2^t)$.
  6. Get $\alpha_t$ from the schedule: warmup for $t \le T_w$, then cosine from $\eta_{\max}$ down to $\eta_{\min}$.
  7. Decoupled decay and the adaptive step: $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \alpha_t\left(\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon) + \lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}_t\right)$.
  8. Increment $t$ and repeat. Log $\|\mathbf{g}\|$, the update norm $\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\|$, and the ratio of the two — that ratio, not the loss, is what tells you whether the step size is sane.

```text theta, m, v <- init, 0, 0 for t = 1 .. T: g <- mean over batch of grad L_i(theta) if ||g||_2 > c: g <- g * c / ||g||_2 # global-norm clipping m <- b1*m + (1-b1)*g # first moment v <- b2*v + (1-b2)*g*g # second moment mh <- m / (1 - b1**t); vh <- v / (1 - b2**t) # bias correction a <- schedule(t) # warmup then cosine theta <- theta - a*(mh/(sqrt(vh) + eps) + wd*theta) # decoupled decay ```

Complexity. Per step: one forward and one backward pass, $O(\text{model cost})$, plus $O(|\boldsymbol{\theta}|)$ for the elementwise moment updates and the decay. Memory: $2|\boldsymbol{\theta}|$ extra for $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ — so Adam doubles optimizer-state memory relative to SGD, and around triples total training memory once gradients and activations are counted (M25 covers the sharding consequences). No matrix is factorised, which is exactly why this is the default at scale.

Derivations

1. Gradient descent is Euler's method, and the step bound comes from the spectrum

Gradient flow is the ODE $d\boldsymbol{\theta}/dt = -\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. Discretise with forward Euler, $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t + \eta\,(d\boldsymbol{\theta}/dt)_t$, giving $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$: gradient descent is the Euler scheme with time step $\eta$, and the loss is the Lyapunov function whose decay we are trying to guarantee.

For $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac12\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}$ with $\mathbf{A}$ symmetric positive definite, $\nabla\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}$, so

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = (\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{\theta}_t .$$

Diagonalise $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^\top$ and change coordinates $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{Q}^\top\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Then $\mathbf{y}_{t+1} = (\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{\Lambda})\mathbf{y}_t$, which is $n$ independent scalar recursions $y^{(i)}_{t+1} = (1-\eta\lambda_i)y^{(i)}_t$, so $y^{(i)}_t = (1-\eta\lambda_i)^t y^{(i)}_0$.

Convergence for every initial condition requires every mode to decay, i.e. $|1-\eta\lambda_i| < 1$ for all $i$, which is $-1 < 1-\eta\lambda_i < 1$. The left inequality gives $\eta\lambda_i < 2$ and the right gives $\eta\lambda_i > 0$; $\lambda_i > 0$ makes the second automatic. Hence

$$0 < \eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} .$$

Iteration count. Take $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$, the largest stable step. Then the fastest mode has multiplier $0$ (dies immediately) but the slowest has

$$1 - \frac{\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max}} = 1 - \frac1\kappa,$$

so after $k$ steps the residual in the slow direction is $(1-1/\kappa)^k$, and reaching relative error $\varepsilon$ needs $k \approx \kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)$. Substituting $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$ into the general bound shows this is optimised: the worst-case contraction is $\max_i|1-\eta\lambda_i| \ge (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ for any $\eta$, attained at $\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min})$. For $\kappa = 100$ the optimal per-step factor is $99/101 = 0.9802$, and $\log(10^{-3})/\log(0.9802) \approx 345$ iterations — matching the 362 measured, and confirming that $\kappa$ is a multiplier on the cost, not a rounding detail.

2. Momentum amplifies the learning rate, and the optimal coefficient follows from $\kappa$

Heavy ball: $\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\mathbf{v}_t + \mathbf{g}_t$, $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\mathbf{v}_{t+1}$.

Effective learning rate. Consider a direction in which the gradient is roughly constant, $\mathbf{g}_t \approx \mathbf{g}$. Then the velocity converges to a fixed point: $\mathbf{v} = \beta\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{g}$, so $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$, and the parameter update is $-\eta\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$. The effective step is therefore $\eta_{\text{eff}} = \eta/(1-\beta)$, an amplification of $1/(1-\beta)$: $2\times$ at $\beta = 0.5$, $10\times$ at $\beta = 0.9$, $100\times$ at $\beta = 0.99$. This is the single most common cause of divergence when momentum is added to a tuned learning rate.

Stability and the optimal coefficient. On the quadratic, combine the two recursions into

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \left[(1+\beta)\mathbf{I} - \eta\mathbf{A}\right]\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \beta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1},$$

whose characteristic roots per eigen-direction are $\lambda_{1,2} = \frac12\left[(1+\beta-\eta\lambda_i) \pm \sqrt{(1+\beta-\eta\lambda_i)^2 - 4\beta}\right]$. Inside the square root being negative gives complex roots with modulus $\sqrt{\beta}$ — a decaying oscillation with rate $\beta^{k/2}$. Optimising over $\beta$ for a spectrum spanning $\kappa$ gives

$$\beta^{*} = \left(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\right)^2, \qquad \text{rate} \approx 1 - \frac{2}{\sqrt\kappa},$$

so momentum turns the $\kappa$ dependence of plain descent into $\sqrt\kappa$ — for $\kappa = 100$, $\beta^{*} = 0.669$ and the rate $1-0.2 = 0.8$ per step, against $0.98$ for the best plain step. Nesterov's variant attains the same asymptotic rate with a look-ahead gradient and is provably no worse for smooth convex objectives; the empirical difference in deep learning is small and often inside run-to-run noise, so it is a reasonable default rather than a requirement.

3. What bias correction corrects

Both moment buffers start at zero, so early estimates are biased toward zero. Unroll the first-moment recursion from $\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{0}$:

$$\mathbf{m}_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta_1)\beta_1^{t-i}\mathbf{g}_i .$$

The coefficients sum to $(1-\beta_1)\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\beta_1^j = (1-\beta_1)\frac{1-\beta_1^t}{1-\beta_1} = 1-\beta_1^t$, not 1. So if the gradient were constant at $\mathbf{g}$, then $\mathbf{m}_t = (1-\beta_1^t)\mathbf{g}$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = (1-\beta_1^t)\mathbf{g}$ — an underestimate by exactly the factor $1-\beta_1^t$. Dividing by it makes $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$ an unbiased estimate of $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}]$ at every $t$:

$$\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1-\beta_1^t} = \frac{\sum_i(1-\beta_1)\beta_1^{t-i}\mathbf{g}_i}{1-\beta_1^t}, \qquad \sum_i\frac{(1-\beta_1)\beta_1^{t-i}}{1-\beta_1^t} = 1 .$$

The same argument with $(1-\beta_2)\beta_2^{t-i}$ and $\mathbf{g}_i\odot\mathbf{g}_i$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}_t] = (1-\beta_2^t)\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}\odot\mathbf{g}]$, so the second moment needs the same correction.

Why it matters numerically. At $t=1$, $\mathbf{m}_1 = (1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_1 = 0.1\mathbf{g}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}_1^2 = 0.001\mathbf{g}_1^2$. Their ratio uncorrected is $0.1|\mathbf{g}|/0.0316|\mathbf{g}| = 3.16|\mathbf{g}|$ — the update is not $\eta\,\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{g})$ but $3.16\eta|\mathbf{g}|$, scaled by the gradient's own magnitude. With both corrections the ratio is exactly $\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{g})$, the intended scale-free behaviour. Corrected, the step is $\approx\eta$ in every direction from the first update; uncorrected, it is large and gradient-dependent, which is the runaway seen in the micro-example.

4. Where L2 and weight decay diverge: the AdamW algebra

Write the two objectives on the same footing. With L2, the loss is $\mathcal{L} + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$, so the gradient fed to the optimiser is $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_t = \mathbf{g}_t + \lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$, and Adam's update becomes

$$\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{L2}} = -\eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})}+\epsilon},$$

where both moments now include the decay term. With decoupling, the same decay is applied outside the adaptive normalisation:

$$\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{W}} = -\eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t(\mathbf{g})}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t(\mathbf{g})}+\epsilon} \;-\; \eta\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}_t .$$

Where they agree. For plain SGD, $\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{g}$, so

$$\Delta^{\text{L2}} = -\eta(\mathbf{g}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}), \qquad \Delta^{\text{W}} = -\eta\mathbf{g} - \eta\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta},$$

identical. The classical statement "L2 regularisation is weight decay" is true exactly here, and it is stated for SGD.

Where they diverge. Suppose the gradient is small relative to the decay term, as it is for a well-fit parameter. Then $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\approx\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{v}}\approx(\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta})^2$ (ignoring momentum), so the L2 update is $-\eta\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}/(|\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}|+\epsilon) \approx -\eta\,\operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ — a constant-magnitude shrinkage independent of $\lambda$ and of the parameter's size. Enlarging $\lambda$ barely changes the shrinkage; it changes the direction toward $\operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. With decoupled decay the shrinkage is $\eta\lambda|\boldsymbol{\theta}|$, proportional to the parameter and to $\lambda$. Two parameters with different gradient scales therefore receive different regularisation under L2 but the same relative regularisation under decoupling, which is what "weight decay" was always supposed to mean.

5. Natural gradient from a KL constraint

Steepest descent depends on how you measure distance. In parameter space the natural metric is not Euclidean; it is the divergence between the distributions the parameters induce.

Constrain the step by a fixed KL between the model before and after: $\operatorname{KL}(p_\theta\|p_{\theta+d\theta}) = \varepsilon$. Expand the divergence to second order. For a smooth family, $\operatorname{KL}(p_\theta\|p_{\theta+d\theta}) = \frac12 d\theta^\top\mathbf{F}\,d\theta + o(\|d\theta\|^2)$, where

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\left[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)^\top\right]$$

is the Fisher information matrix. (The first-order term vanishes because both distributions are normalised.) Now minimise the linearised loss $d\theta^\top\mathbf{g}$ subject to $\frac12 d\theta^\top\mathbf{F}d\theta = \varepsilon$. Lagrange multipliers:

$$\mathcal{L}(d\theta,\lambda) = d\theta^\top\mathbf{g} + \lambda\left(\frac12 d\theta^\top\mathbf{F}d\theta - \varepsilon\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{g} + \lambda\mathbf{F}d\theta = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; d\theta = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{g}.$$

So the natural-gradient direction is $\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{g}$, and the update is $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{g}$. Two facts make this more than a curiosity: when $\mathbf{F}$ is a multiple of the identity it reduces to ordinary gradient descent, and for a Gaussian model the Fisher is the Hessian of the loss, so natural gradient is Newton's method in distribution space. K-FAC approximates $\mathbf{F}$ layerwise by $\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{G}$ with $\mathbf{A}$ the input second moment and $\mathbf{G}$ the output-gradient second moment, inverting each factor at $O(d^{1.5})$ rather than $O(d^3)$ for the full Kronecker product.

6. Lagrange multipliers and the KKT conditions

For $\min f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $h_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0$: at a solution, the constraint surface is a level set, and moving within it changes $f$ only through the component of $\nabla f$ tangent to the surface. At the constrained optimum that tangential component must vanish, so $\nabla f$ is entirely normal to the surface — a combination of the constraint normals:

$$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{*}) + \sum_i\lambda_i\nabla h_i(\mathbf{x}^{*}) = 0, \qquad h_i(\mathbf{x}^{*}) = 0 .$$

These are exactly the stationarity conditions of $\mathcal{L} = f + \sum_i\lambda_ih_i$ with respect to both $\mathbf{x}$ and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, which is why "add the constraint times a multiplier and differentiate" works. For inequalities $g_j \le 0$, KKT adds feasibility, $\lambda_j \ge 0$, and complementary slackness $\lambda_jg_j = 0$: a constraint that is not active has zero multiplier, and a constraint with a positive multiplier must be active. The multiplier's value is the rate of change of the optimum with respect to the constraint constant — the shadow price.

7. Why inverse-square-root decay is the principled schedule

Stochastic approximation with decreasing step sizes: if $\mathbf{g}_t = \nabla\mathcal{L} + \boldsymbol{\xi}_t$ with $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_t] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Cov}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_t] = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, then the iterate $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta_t\mathbf{g}_t$ converges to a neighbourhood of the minimum whose size is governed by $\eta_t^2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, while the price of moving from the current position is governed by $\eta_t$. With $\eta_t \propto 1/\sqrt{t}$ the accumulated noise contribution $\sum_t\eta_t^2$ grows like $\log t$ while the remaining distance shrinks like $1/\sqrt{t}$ — the standard rate for stochastic approximation, and the reason $\eta_t \propto t^{-1/2}$ is the schedule with a theorem behind it. Cosine and linear decay have no such theorem; they are empirical improvements that hold up across many settings, and should be described as such.

Warmup, conversely, has three partial explanations rather than one theorem: Adam's second-moment estimate at small $t$ is a poor estimate of curvature; large early updates can destroy randomly initialised features irreversibly; and the loss surface near a random initialisation has large, poorly estimated curvature. It is standard because it is robust, not because it is derived.

Implementation from scratch

Three optimisers on the same ill-conditioned quadratic, so the comparison is exact rather than anecdotal: $f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac12\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ with $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(1,100)$, $\kappa = 100$.

```python import numpy as np def loss_grad(A, x): # f(x) = 0.5 x^T A x, grad = A x return 0.5 * x @ A @ x, A @ x def sgd(A, x0, lr, steps): x = x0.copy() for _ in range(steps): _, g = loss_grad(A, x) x = x - lr * g return x def momentum(A, x0, lr, beta, steps): # heavy ball x, v = x0.copy(), np.zeros_like(x0) for _ in range(steps): _, g = loss_grad(A, x) v = beta * v + g # velocity accumulates gradients x = x - lr * v return x def adamw(A, x0, lr, steps, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8, wd=0.0): x, m, v = x0.copy(), np.zeros_like(x0), np.zeros_like(x0) for t in range(1, steps + 1): _, g = loss_grad(A, x) m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g mh = m / (1 - b1 ** t) # unbiased first moment vh = v / (1 - b2 ** t) # unbiased second moment x = x - lr * (mh / (np.sqrt(vh) + eps) + wd * x) # decoupled decay return x A = np.diag([1.0, 100.0]) # kappa = 100 x0 = np.array([1.0, 1.0]) for name, run in [("sgd", lambda: sgd(A, x0, 0.019, 200)), ("momentum", lambda: momentum(A, x0, 0.019, 0.9, 200)), ("adamw", lambda: adamw(A, x0, 0.1, 200))]: x = run() print("%-9s x=%-24s f=%.3e" % (name, np.round(x, 6), 0.5 * x @ A @ x)) ```

What to expect. After 200 steps SGD leaves $x \approx (0.0216, 0)$ with loss $2.3\times10^{-4}$ — the first coordinate has barely moved because the step is pinned by the $\lambda = 100$ direction. Momentum reaches $f \approx 3.5\times10^{-8}$, three orders better. AdamW reaches $f\approx2.6\times10^{-9}$ because its per-coordinate normalisation removes the scale mismatch entirely. If you raise SGD's rate to $0.02$ nothing converges and the second coordinate oscillates with multiplier exactly $-1$; at $0.025$ it reaches $3.7\times10^{10}$ within 60 steps. Reproduce that boundary yourself — it is the most useful single experiment in this module.

Worked example

Adam's first three steps, minimising $f(w) = \frac12w^2$ from $w_0 = 1$, with $\eta = 0.1$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$.

$t$ $g_t = w_{t-1}$ $m_t$ $v_t$ $\hat m_t$ $\sqrt{\hat v_t}$ update $w_t$
1 1.0000 0.1000 0.001000 1.0000 1.0000 0.1000 0.9000
2 0.9000 0.1800 0.001809 0.9474 0.9513 0.0996 0.8004
3 0.8004 0.2420 0.002448 0.8931 0.9037 0.0988 0.7016

Read the second row carefully: $\hat m_2 = 0.18/0.19 = 0.9474$ and $\sqrt{\hat v_2} = \sqrt{0.001809/0.001999} = 0.9513$, so the update is $0.1\times0.9474/0.9513 = 0.0996$ — almost exactly $\eta$, because the ratio of a first moment to the square root of a second moment is scale-free. That is Adam's entire design intent: every step is about $\eta$ regardless of gradient magnitude.

The same three steps without bias correction: updates $0.316$, $0.414$, $0.432$, taking $w$ to $0.684$, $0.270$, $-0.162$. The walk overshoots the minimum on step 3. The uncorrected ratio is $\mathbf{m}/\sqrt{\mathbf{v}} = 3.16\,|\mathbf{g}|$, not $\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{g})$ — off by a factor of $1/\sqrt{1-\beta_2}$ at $t=1$.

Stability boundary, $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(1,100)$, 60 steps from $(1,1)$:

$\eta$ multiplier for $\lambda=100$ second coordinate after 60 steps
0.005 $0.500$ $8.7\times10^{-19}$ (converged)
0.019 $-0.900$ $0.0018$ (decaying, alternating)
0.020 $-1.000$ $1.0000$ (never decays)

The boundary $2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.02$ is exact to the digit, and the failure at exactly $0.02$ is oscillation rather than blow-up — the distinction matters, because an oscillating loss curve is the signature of being at the boundary rather than over it.

Momentum amplification, $\lambda = 1$, $\eta = 0.1$, $\beta = 0.9$, from $x_0 = 1$: the iterates are $0.9000$, $0.7200$, $0.4860$, $0.2268$, $-0.0292$, $-0.2566$, $-0.4357$, $-0.5532$, $-0.6037$, $\ldots$ — overshoot, oscillation, and a much larger step than the same $\eta$ gives without momentum. Plain SGD at $\eta = 0.1$ on $\lambda = 1$ decays monotonically as $0.9^k$. The effective rate is $10\times$ larger, exactly as $1/(1-\beta)$ predicts.

Common misconceptions

"Adam is strictly better than SGD"

This is contested, and the honest statement is that it depends on the problem and the tuning budget. On many vision benchmarks, well-tuned SGD with momentum and a good schedule matches or beats Adam on final test accuracy while being cheaper per step; on transformers and most large-scale language work, Adam/AdamW is the default because per-coordinate scaling handles embedding gradients and rare features far better. Proposed explanations — sharper minima, adaptive methods shrinking effective learning rates over time, the interaction with weight decay — remain disputed. Treat "Adam generalises worse" as a live hypothesis, not an established fact, and always compare under equal tuning effort (M08).

"The learning rate is just a number you tune"

It is a time step with units of inverse curvature, so its correct value is set by the sharpest eigenvalue of the Hessian and therefore by architecture, depth, normalisation, batch size, and precision. Two models with the same nominal $\eta$ can be in completely different regimes. The number to log is not $\eta$ but the ratio $\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\|/\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|$ per step: when it is near $10^{-3}$ the run is calm, and when it is oscillating at $10^{-1}$ you are at the stability boundary regardless of what $\eta$ looks like.

"Momentum always speeds things up"

Momentum amplifies the effective step by $1/(1-\beta)$, so adding it to an already-tuned learning rate usually diverges. It also helps only where gradients are consistent along a direction; in a region where the gradient direction changes quickly it overshoots and takes longer. And a coefficient too large for the spectrum (0.99 at $\kappa=100$) is worse than none, as the measured 2000-iteration non-convergence shows.

"Weight decay and L2 regularisation are the same thing"

Identical for plain SGD, different for every adaptive method, as the algebra in derivation 4 shows. Under Adam, an L2 penalty on a parameter with a large second moment produces almost no shrinkage, while under decoupled decay every parameter shrinks by the same relative amount. If you switch from SGD to Adam and keep the L2 coefficient, you have changed the regularisation without changing the hyperparameter.

"The learning-rate schedule is cosmetic"

The schedule decides the final loss, because the last few thousand steps are where the model settles. A constant step large enough to make fast early progress is too large to converge tightly, and one small enough to converge tightly wastes the first half of training. Cosine and linear decay are consistently worth a meaningful fraction of a nat on language-modelling benchmarks; inverse-square-root has the stochastic-approximation theory behind it.

Exercises

Warm-up (check understanding)

4.1 For $\mathbf{A} = \operatorname{diag}(2,50)$, what is the largest stable step size for gradient descent, and how many iterations does the slowest mode need to shrink by $10^3$?

Solution $\lambda_{\max} = 50$, so $\eta < 2/50 = 0.04$. At $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max} = 0.02$ the slow mode contracts by $1 - 2/50 = 0.96$ per step, so $0.96^k = 10^{-3}$ gives $k = \log(10^{-3})/\log(0.96) \approx 169$ iterations. Note $\kappa = 25$ and the count scales linearly in it.

4.2 With $\beta = 0.95$ momentum, what is the effective learning-rate amplification, and what should you do to $\eta$ when adding it?

Solution $1/(1-0.95) = 20\times$. So $\eta$ should be reduced by roughly that factor to keep the same effective step — in practice less, because the amplification applies only along directions with consistent gradients; a factor of 5–10 reduction is typical.

4.3 Adam with $\beta_2 = 0.999$: what is the second-moment bias-correction factor at $t = 1$, $t = 100$, $t = 1000$?

Solution $1-\beta_2^t$: at $t=1$ it is $0.001$ (a correction of $1000\times$), at $t=100$ it is $0.0952$ ($10.5\times$), at $t=1000$ it is $0.632$ ($1.58\times$). The correction matters most precisely where the estimate is built from few samples.

Core (do the math)

4.4 Derive the largest stable $\eta$ for momentum on a single quadratic mode.

Solution The recursion is $x_{t+1} = (1+\beta-\eta\lambda)x_t - \beta x_{t-1}$ with characteristic equation $r^2 - (1+\beta-\eta\lambda)r + \beta = 0$. Stability needs both roots inside the unit circle. For complex roots the modulus is $\sqrt{\beta} < 1$, so that case is always stable. For real roots, the condition is $r_{1,2} \in(-1,1)$, which reduces to $\eta\lambda < 2(1+\beta)$ — larger than the $2$ of plain descent by the factor $(1+\beta)$, i.e. momentum tolerates a step up to twice as large at $\beta\to1$, at the cost of oscillation while the multiplier is near $-1$.

4.5 Show that Adam's update is bounded by $\eta$ per coordinate (ignoring $\epsilon$ and decay).

Solution The update is $\eta\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}$. With $\hat m$ and $\hat v$ bias-corrected, $\hat m$ is a weighted average of gradients and $\sqrt{\hat v}$ is the square root of a weighted average of squared gradients. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $|\hat m| \le \sqrt{\hat v}$, so $|\eta\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}| \le \eta$. Hence Adam's per-coordinate step is at most $\eta$, which is why it is called scale-free and why a very large $\eta$ produces a bounded but possibly useless step rather than an overflow.

4.6 For $\mathcal{L} = \frac12\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|^2$, write the Gauss–Newton Hessian and say when it equals the true Hessian.

Solution $\nabla\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y})$ and the exact Hessian is $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$. Since the residual is linear in $\mathbf{w}$, the Gauss–Newton approximation $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$ with $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{X}$ equals it exactly. For a nonlinear model $\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{w})$, $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \sum_i r_i\nabla^2r_i$, and Gauss–Newton drops the second term — exact when residuals are zero at the solution, a good approximation when they are small, and the reason it is used for least-squares fitting but not for general losses.

Stretch (build or break it)

4.7 Construct a two-parameter loss where Adam's per-coordinate normalisation is actively harmful and plain SGD does better.

Solution Take $f(w_1,w_2) = \frac12(10^{-4}w_1^2 + w_2^2)$ with the optimum at the origin and a gradient that is $10^{-4}w_1$ in the first coordinate. Adam normalises each coordinate by its own gradient scale, so both coordinates move at about $\eta$ per step *regardless of curvature* — it takes $w_1$ about $1/\eta$ steps to travel its distance, and near the optimum Adam keeps taking steps of size $\sim\eta$ (its second moment shrinks along with the gradient), oscillating around the minimum. SGD, with a step set by the *largest* curvature, moves $w_1$ slowly but converges monotonically and to higher precision. This is the "Adam does not converge" phenomenon: with a constant $\eta$, Adam's noise floor scales like $\eta$, which is why decay schedules matter so much for it. Any problem with very different per-coordinate scales but a benign condition number shows the effect.

4.8 Show that decoupled weight decay on a purely quadratic loss adds a bias to the fixed point, and compute it.

Solution For $f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac12\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}$ with decay, the gradient step plus shrink gives $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = (1-\eta\lambda)\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \left[(1-\eta\lambda)\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{A}\right]\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$. The fixed point is still $\mathbf{0}$ — decay does not bias the minimum of a quadratic whose optimum is at the origin. For a loss with optimum $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*} \ne \mathbf{0}$, the fixed point solves $\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \lambda\boldsymbol{\theta} = 0$ only if $\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*} = 0$; in general the stationary condition becomes $\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \lambda\boldsymbol{\theta} = 0$, so the optimum is pulled toward the origin by exactly the ridge solution $(\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ — a bias of order $\lambda/\lambda_{\min}$ in the worst-conditioned direction. Weight decay is not free; it trades bias for variance, as the estimator decomposition in **M03** says.

4.9 With $\mathcal{L} = \frac12\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|^2$ and the constraint $\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 \le c$, derive the ridge solution from the Lagrangian.

Solution $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},\lambda) = \frac12\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|^2 + \lambda(\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - c)$. Stationarity: $\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}) + 2\lambda\mathbf{w} = 0$, so $\mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+2\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ — ridge regression. Complementary slackness says $\lambda = 0$ when the unconstrained solution already satisfies the constraint, and $\lambda > 0$ otherwise. The multiplier is the shadow price of the weight budget, which is exactly why the constraint and penalty formulations are equivalent with a one-to-one map between $c$ and $\lambda$ (**M05** derives the ridge solution from the other direction).

Answers 1. $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = (\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$ has per-mode multipliers $1-\eta\lambda_i$; $|1-\eta\lambda_i|<1$ for all $i$ gives $0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$. At $\eta = 2/\lambda_{\max}$ the sharpest mode has multiplier $-1$ and oscillates forever without decaying. 2. Because the stable step is pinned by $\lambda_{\max}$ while progress is limited by $\lambda_{\min}$: at $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$ the slow contraction is $1-1/\kappa$, so steps to a given accuracy scale as $\kappa$. 3. $\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$ on a constant-gradient direction, so the update is $-\eta\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$. $\beta^{*} = ((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^2 = 0.669$ for $\kappa = 100$. 4. By $1-\beta_1^t$ and $1-\beta_2^t$, because both buffers start at zero so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = (1-\beta_1^t)\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}]$. At $t=1$ the uncorrected ratio is $m/\sqrt{v} = 3.16|\mathbf{g}|$ rather than $\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{g})$. 5. $\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\eta(\hat{\mathbf{m}}/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}+\epsilon) + \lambda\boldsymbol{\theta})$. It differs from Adam+L2 whenever $\hat v$ varies across parameters: a parameter with $\hat v = 10^4$ receives $1/100$ of the shrinkage under L2 that a parameter with $\hat v = 1$ does, whereas decoupled decay shrinks both by the same relative amount. 6. $\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{g}$ with $\mathbf{F}$ the Fisher information; it is the steepest-descent direction under a KL constraint between successive distributions, derived by a second-order expansion of the divergence. It needs an $n\times n$ inverse, so at scale it is approximated (diagonal, block-Kronecker as in K-FAC, or low-rank). 7. Adam's second-moment estimate is unreliable for small $t$; large early updates can destroy randomly initialised features; and initial curvature is large and poorly estimated. Warmup protects the optimiser's own statistics before the model's.

Further reading

  • Goodfellow, Bengio, Courville, Deep Learning (2016), chapter 8 (free at deeplearningbook.org). Extract the sections on momentum, AdaGrad, and RMSProp — the cleanest statement of each rule before the modern variants.
  • Kingma and Ba, "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization," 2014 (arXiv:1412.6980). Extract section 2's algorithm and the bias-correction argument; read the update equations against derivation 3 above.
  • Loshchilov and Hutter, "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization," 2017 (arXiv:1711.05101). Extract section 2's algebra showing where L2 and decoupled decay diverge under Adam, and the experimental claim that the optimal $\lambda$ becomes $\eta$-independent.
  • Nesterov, Introductory Lectures on Convex Optimization (Springer, 2004), chapters 2–3. Extract the $O(1/k^2)$ accelerated rate and its proof sketch; it explains why Nesterov's look-ahead is a correction rather than a heuristic.
  • Martens, "Deep Learning via Hessian-free Optimization," ICML 2010, and Martens and Grosse, "Optimizing Neural Networks with Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature," ICML 2015. Extract the Kronecker factorisation of the Fisher and the cost argument for small models.
  • Sutskever, Martens, Dahl, Hinton, "On the importance of initialization and momentum in deep learning," ICML 2013. Extract the momentum phase portraits — the best visual account of how $\beta$ and $\eta$ interact.
  • Bottou, Curtis, Nocedal, "Optimization Methods for Large-Scale Machine Learning," SIAM Review 60(2), 2018 (free preprint). Extract the stochastic-approximation and schedule discussion that underpins the inverse-square-root schedule.
