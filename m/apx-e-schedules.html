Schedules

Three routes through the curriculum, costed in hours, with the gaps named.

on this page

How these plans are costed

A "study hour" here is focused work: about 20 minutes reading, 40 minutes deriving or implementing, and a checkpoint attempt at the end. It excludes setup, installation, and the hour you lose when a CUDA driver update breaks your environment — add roughly 30 percent if you are provisioning your own hardware, and another 30 percent if you are not yet comfortable writing Python daily.

Each module in this curriculum declares a cost in its front matter in weeks at about 10 hours per week. The declared costs sum to 63 module-weeks, which is 630 hours. That number is the anchor for everything below: any plan that promises the whole curriculum in less than about 600 hours is promising something other than the whole curriculum.

Plan 1 — the 12-week sprint (240 hours)

Twenty hours a week for twelve weeks. This is for a working engineer who needs to be dangerous with language models quickly, and it is honest about being a reading plus one artefact per week plan, not a mastery plan. It covers the spine — the mathematics you cannot reason without, the transformer, the training loop, and the modern LLM stack — and skips every branch that is not on that spine.

Week Modules Hours Artefact by the end of the week
1 M01, M02 20 A notebook that applies a 2×2 matrix to a grid and hand-derives the gradient of a quadratic form
2 M03, M04 20 A confidence-interval calculator and a plot of SGD / momentum / Adam on an ill-conditioned quadratic
3 M05, M06 20 A k-fold cross-validation harness and a two-layer MLP in NumPy, no framework
4 M07, M08 20 An autodiff engine that passes a finite-difference check, plus an initialisation-variance experiment
5 M09 20 A minimal training framework with a config file and a reproducible run log
6 M12, M13 20 A transformer block from scratch, a shape trace, and a parameter count reconciled against a real model file
7 M14, M15 20 A BPE tokenizer with a bits-per-byte table, and a 10M-parameter language model trained on your own corpus
8 M16, M17 20 A scaling fit from three runs and a LoRA adapter with both task gain and retention measured
9 M18 20 DPO implemented from the loss up, with preference accuracy and reward margin plotted
10 M19, M20 20 A KV-cache server reporting TTFT/TPOT percentiles, and an eval harness that prints intervals
11 M27, M29 20 Three-pass notes on eight papers, and one published claim audited against its evidence
12 Review and write-up 20 A four-page write-up of your strongest artefact, with its claim, evidence, and limitations

Totals: 12 weeks × 20 hours = 240 hours. Skipped entirely: M10, M11, M21, M22, M23, M24, M25, M26, and the full M28 ladder (you touch rungs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and a partial 6 as the weekly artefacts above). That is a real cost: you will not be able to read a systems paper, a convolution result, or an interpretability claim with any confidence, and you will have no kernel or serving optimisation experience.

Plan 2 — the 6-month standard (338 hours)

Thirteen hours a week for twenty-six weeks, one module per week with a consolidation week in the middle and the last week for writing. This is the plan most learners should take. It covers M01 through M22 in order, plus M26 and M27 and M29.

Add M23 and M25 later if your work touches multimodal input or multi-GPU training; both are two-week modules and neither is a prerequisite for anything else.

Plan 3 — the 12-month thorough track (624 hours)

Thirteen hours a week for fifty-two calendar weeks, of which forty-eight are working weeks and four are rest weeks. Every module is covered, in dependency order, and the capstone ladder is its own six-week block at the end rather than being threaded through. This is the plan for someone who intends to contribute rather than consume.

Block Weeks Modules Hours Artefact at the end of the block
B1 1–6 M01–M04 78 A derivation notebook you can hand to someone else, plus an optimiser study
B2 7–9 M05, M06 39 A classical baseline and an MLP from scratch, both with intervals
B3 10–14 M07–M09 65 An autodiff engine and a small framework, both yours
B4 15–19 M10–M12 65 Three architectures from scratch: conv, recurrent, attention
B5 20–22 M13, M14 39 RoPE, GQA, and a tokenizer with a compression table

B7 26–29 M17, M18 52 LoRA with retention measured, and DPO with margins plotted
B8 30–33 M19–M21 52 A served model with latency tables, an eval harness, and a logit-lens study
B9 34–36 M22, M23 39 A RAG system, and a patch-based vision model or a diffusion sampler
B10 37–41 M24–M26 65 A Triton kernel with a roofline account, plus a quantisation study
B11 42–43 M27, M29 26 A reproduction with a discrepancy note, and a written survey of one frontier
B12 44–48 M28 65 The ladder: ten rungs, each with an acceptance criterion met, and a paper

Totals: 48 working weeks × 13 hours = 624 hours; plus four rest weeks at zero new material, making 52 calendar weeks. The 6-hour gap against the nominal 630 is absorbed by the rest weeks' review sessions, which count as consolidation rather than new material.

The ladder block deserves a note. M28's front matter says six weeks and its rung table sums to 68 hours at minimum-pass depth; 65 hours in five working weeks is deliberately a little tighter than the module's own budget, on the assumption that by this point you have already built most of the rungs once as block artefacts — B3 gives you rung 1, B5 rung 2, B6 rung 3, B7 rungs 4 and 5, B8 rungs 6 and 9, B10 rung 8, B11 rungs 7 and 10. The capstone block is where they get hardened and written up, not where they are first attempted.

Rest weeks

Four of them in the thorough track (after working weeks 12, 24, 36, and 48), one in the standard plan (week 13), and none in the sprint — which is one of the reasons the sprint is not a beginner's plan.

A rest week is not a holiday from the material; it is a week with no new module, spent on:

  • Re-deriving, from memory, the checkpoints of the two modules you found hardest. This is the single most effective activity in the whole schedule, and it is the one everyone skips.
  • Cleaning up artefacts: the notebook that half-runs, the config file that is out of date, the results table without intervals.
  • Reading one paper properly using the three-pass method from M27, with no pressure to implement anything.
  • Writing a short retro: what you believed at the start of the block, what you believe now, and which belief changed because of a number.

Without rest weeks, the topics you learned in week 3 are gone by week 20. The spaced re-derivation is cheaper than relearning, and the evidence for that is your own checkpoints.

Review cadence

Three rhythms, nested:

Weekly (inside the study hours, 30 minutes). Attempt the checkpoint of the module you just finished, cold, without notes. Anything you cannot do goes on a list. That list is what the rest week consumes.

Per block or per five modules (2 hours, inside the block's hours). Re-attempt the oldest items on the list, then re-run one artefact from the block with a fresh seed and check that it still passes. Artefact rot is real: environments drift, and a script that ran in month two often does not run in month eight.

Quarterly or at each rest week (half a day, outside the counted hours). Re-read the tables of contents of the modules you have completed and write, from memory, a one-page map of how they connect. If you cannot place a module on the map, it is not learned, whatever your notes say.

A cadence you will actually keep beats a better one you will not. Thirty minutes weekly and two hours per block is enough; the failure mode is not insufficient review, it is abandoning review entirely by week six.

Choosing between them

Take the sprint if you have a deadline that is twelve weeks away, you already write production code, and you need breadth of understanding rather than artefacts you can show. Accept in advance that you will skip systems, convolutions, recurrent networks, and interpretability.

Take the standard if you want the whole conceptual arc and are willing to defer three branches. It is the best value per hour of the three and the one most learners should pick.

Take the thorough track if you intend to produce research or work on model internals, and can protect thirteen hours a week for a year. The capstone block is the part that matters; the first 43 weeks exist to make it possible.

If you have fewer than ten hours a week, do not compress a plan — stretch it. The courses are sequential in a way that punishes skipping: everything after M07 assumes you can differentiate a computational graph, and everything after M12 assumes you can track tensor shapes. A stretched standard plan at six hours a week is roughly eighteen months and is a better use of that time than a sprint at six hours a week, which would be a survey course.

What each plan does not give you

The sprint gives no systems experience and no interpretation skill; you will be able to explain attention and unable to explain why your serving stack is slow.

The standard plan gives no kernel experience, no multi-GPU experience, and no multimodal experience. If your job involves training anything larger than a single GPU can hold, add M25 immediately after M26; the two together are about 40 hours and they change what you can attempt.

The thorough track gives you artefacts and no audience. A paper you wrote with no reader is a rehearsal; submit it somewhere — a workshop, a reading group, a colleague who will argue — because the review response is the part of M27 you cannot simulate.
