Backpropagation and Automatic Differentiation

How a network learns the derivative of its own mistakes.

on this page

Why this module exists

Backpropagation is the algorithm that made deep learning possible, and it is the one piece of machinery most learners carry around as a black box: they know PyTorch calls backward() and that something called the chain rule happens. That is enough to run code and not enough to do anything else. Without it you cannot tell why a custom layer's gradient is wrong, why training memory scales with depth rather than with parameter count, why half-precision training needs a loss scale, why activation checkpointing trades compute for memory, or why a detach() in the wrong place silently trains nothing. This module derives the backward pass completely — scalars first, then matrices — and then builds a working reverse-mode autodiff engine, so that the algorithm stops being a library feature and becomes something you own.

Prerequisites check

If you miss more than one, revisit the module named in the answer.

  1. State the multivariate chain rule for $f(g(x), h(x))$.
  2. For $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}$ with $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ and $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h}$, what are the shapes of $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{A}$ and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}$, given $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{Z}$?
  3. What is the Jacobian of $\mathbf{z}\mapsto \mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}$, and of softmax?
  4. Why is a topological order, rather than just any order, required to propagate derivatives?
  5. Write the forward pass of a 2-layer MLP and name the quantities you must keep to differentiate it.
Answers 1. $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}f(g,h) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial g}g'(x) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial h}h'(x)$ — a sum over the paths by which $x$ influences $f$. Every "sum over paths" in this module is this identity. 2. Same shapes as the arrays themselves: $(n,d)$ and $(d,h)$. That shape preservation is what makes reverse mode practical. 3. $\mathbf{W}$ (shape $h\times d$), and $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^\top$ respectively (**M06**). 4. Because a node's adjoint depends on the adjoints of every node downstream of it; processing in reverse topological order guarantees those are complete before you read them. 5. $\mathbf{z}^{(1)}=\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{(1)}$, $\mathbf{a}^{(1)}=\phi(\mathbf{z}^{(1)})$, $\mathbf{z}^{(2)}=\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{a}^{(1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(2)}$, $\mathcal{L}=\ell(\mathbf{z}^{(2)},y)$. You must keep $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{z}^{(1)}$, $\mathbf{a}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{z}^{(2)}$ — the pre-activations, because $\phi'$ needs them.

Learning objectives

  • Build the computational graph of an arbitrary expression and attach the local derivative to every node.
  • Derive the forward-versus-reverse cost ratio from the number of Jacobian-vector against vector-Jacobian products, and choose the right mode for a given input/output shape.
  • Derive the backward pass of an $L$-layer MLP in full scalar form, contract it to matrix form, and state the delta recursion with every shape.
  • Implement a reverse-mode autodiff engine with topological sort and gradient accumulation at fan-out, and validate it against finite differences.
  • Explain the adjoint/VJP formulation and why each primitive supplies only one vector-Jacobian product.
  • Compute the memory and recomputation cost of activation checkpointing for a given depth and checkpoint interval.
  • State the truncation-versus-roundoff trade-off in finite-difference checking and choose a step size.
  • Explain why backpropagation is neither a model of the brain nor the learning algorithm.

Core concepts

Computational graphs

(1) Intuition. Any expression, however nested, is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are values and whose edges are primitive operations. Once the graph exists you never differentiate the whole expression again: you differentiate the primitives and let the graph do the bookkeeping.

(2) Formal definition. A computational graph has a node set $V$, each node holding a value, a record of its parents, and the primitive that produced it. The derivative of each primitive with respect to each argument is its local derivative. For $f(x,y) = xy + \sin x$ the nodes are $x$, $y$, $m=xy$, $s=\sin x$, $f=m+s$, with local derivatives $\partial m/\partial x = y$, $\partial m/\partial y = x$, $\partial s/\partial x=\cos x$, and derivative $1$ on both edges into $f$.

(3) Why it is built this way. The number of distinct primitives is small — add, multiply, matmul, exp, log, tanh, ReLU, softmax, reshape — while the number of distinct expressions is unbounded. A graph turns "differentiate this model" into "differentiate these twenty operations and multiply".

(4) Worked micro-example. At $x=2$, $y=3$: $m=6$, $s=\sin 2=0.9093$, $f=6.9093$. Local derivatives $\partial f/\partial m=\partial f/\partial s=1$, $\partial m/\partial x=3$, $\partial m/\partial y=2$, $\partial s/\partial x=\cos 2=-0.4161$. The graph is now fixed; every derivative below is a traversal of it.

The chain rule as graph traversal

(1) Intuition. An input influences the output along several paths; the total derivative is the sum over paths of the product of local derivatives. Backprop is only an efficient order in which to evaluate that sum.

(2) Formal definition. For a node $v$ with children $\{u : v\to u\}$ (the nodes it feeds), define the adjoint $\bar{v}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial v$. Then

$$ \bar{v} = \sum_{u \in \text{children}(v)} \bar{u}\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial v}, $$

the multivariable chain rule with the sum over paths collapsed by dynamic programming. A topological order lists every node after all of its parents; processing nodes in reverse topological order guarantees a node's adjoint is complete before it is read.

(3) Why it is built this way. Naively expanding "sum over all paths" is exponential in depth, because paths multiply combinatorially. The recursion reuses each node's adjoint instead of re-deriving it per path, collapsing the cost to one pass. That is the entire algorithmic content of backpropagation.

(4) Worked micro-example. With $f=xy+\sin x$ at $x=2,y=3$: the two paths from $x$ give $\partial f/\partial x = y\cdot1+\cos x\cdot1 = 2.5839$, and the single path from $y$ gives $\partial f/\partial y = x = 2$. Note the fan-out: $x$ feeds two nodes, so its adjoint is a sum of contributions. That is why the engine below writes += and never =.

Step through a tiny network one node at a time: the forward pass fills values left to right, then the backward pass fills gradients right to left. Watch two contributions arrive at a fan-out node and sum into the same slot — that accumulation is the chain rule's sum over paths, made mechanical.

Forward mode, reverse mode, and mixed

(1) Intuition. Forward mode carries a derivative forward alongside every value; reverse mode carries a sensitivity backward. Which is cheap depends only on which side of the function is bigger.

(2) Formal definition. For $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ with Jacobian $\mathbf{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$:

  • Forward mode computes Jacobian-vector products $\dot{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}$ for a seed $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n$: one pass answers "how does the output move if I perturb one input direction". All of $\mathbf{J}$ costs $n$ passes.
  • Reverse mode computes vector-Jacobian products $\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{J}$ for a seed $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^m$: one pass answers "how sensitive is one output combination to every input". All of $\mathbf{J}$ costs $m$ passes.

Each pass costs a small constant multiple of one forward evaluation $c_f$, so forward mode costs $\approx n\,c_f$ and reverse mode $\approx m\,c_f$ for the full Jacobian. Training has $m=1$ (a scalar loss) and $n=|\boldsymbol{\theta}|$, so reverse mode wins by a factor of $|\boldsymbol{\theta}|$; that is why nobody trains a network in forward mode.

(3) Why it is built this way. Mixed mode exists for awkward shapes. A Hessian-vector product is forward-over-reverse: seed a tangent on the parameters, take one reverse pass to obtain the gradient expression, then one forward pass through that computation to obtain $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}$ at $O(|\boldsymbol{\theta}|)$ rather than $O(|\boldsymbol{\theta}|^2)$. Use forward mode when $n\ll m$ (a tall Jacobian), when you need derivatives while streaming with no memory for a tape, or for directional derivatives and Newton-type steps.

(4) Worked micro-example. A layer with 1024 inputs and 8 outputs: forward mode costs 1024 passes for the full Jacobian, reverse mode 8. A function with 3 inputs and 10,000 outputs: forward mode 3, reverse 10,000. The Jacobian is the same object either way; the mode decides only how many passes you pay.

The adjoint: reverse mode as a sequence of VJPs

(1) Intuition. Reverse mode never forms a Jacobian. Each node is handed the sensitivity of the loss to its output and returns the sensitivity of the loss to its inputs.

(2) Formal definition. Every primitive $\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{x})$ defines a vjp: given $\bar{\mathbf{y}}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{y}$ it returns $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = (\partial\mathbf{y}/\partial\mathbf{x})^\top\bar{\mathbf{y}}$. The backward pass composes these vjps along the graph. Since $\bar{\mathbf{y}}$ has the shape of $\mathbf{y}$ and $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ the shape of $\mathbf{x}$, the engine never reasons about shapes — only each primitive's vjp does.

(3) Why it is built this way. It localizes the mathematics: adding an op to a framework never touches the graph machinery, only one cotangent-to-cotangent function. It is also why the backward pass is cheap — the vjp of a matmul is two more matmuls, not a $10^6\times10^6$ Jacobian.

(4) Worked micro-example. For $\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ with $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$, $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h}$ and cotangent $\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times h}$: $\bar{\mathbf{X}}=\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{W}^\top$ and $\bar{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{X}^\top\bar{\mathbf{Z}}$, both the same size as the forward matmul — the constant factor of ~2 in "backward costs twice forward". Shapes: $(n,h)(h,d)\to(n,d)$ and $(d,n)(n,h)\to(d,h)$, both correct.

Backprop is neither the brain nor the learning algorithm

(1) Intuition. Backprop is a derivative calculator. It computes $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}$ and stops; what you do with that gradient is a separate decision.

(2) Formal definition. The learning algorithm is the update rule, e.g. $\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\eta\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}$ (M04). Backprop supplies the second factor only. Change the optimizer and backprop is untouched; change the architecture and the optimizer is untouched.

(3) Why it is built this way. Conflating the two produces concrete errors: "backprop is unstable at depth" is really about the gradient's magnitude interacting with a step size, and "backprop overfits" is not a sentence about derivatives at all. The biological claim fails on the weight-transport problem (M06), which is why feedback alignment and local losses are active research rather than settled knowledge.

(4) Worked micro-example. Two runs with identical gradients and different rules: with $\eta=0.1$ and gradient $-2.0$, SGD moves the parameter by $+0.2$; with momentum 0.9 the same gradient produces a larger, history-dependent step. Backprop returned $-2.0$ in both cases and knows nothing about either.

What you must store, and activation checkpointing

(1) Intuition. Backprop is cheap in arithmetic and expensive in memory: the backward pass needs the forward values, so training costs memory proportional to depth in a way inference does not.

(2) Formal definition. With $s$ bytes of activations per layer and $L$ layers, storing everything costs $O(Ls)$. Checkpointing stores only every $k$-th layer permanently — $L/k$ tensors — and recomputes the $k-1$ intermediate activations of one segment when the backward pass reaches it. Peak memory is $O(Ls/k + ks)$, minimized at $k=\sqrt{L}$, giving $O(\sqrt{L}\,s)$.

(3) Why it is built this way. Because memory, not FLOPs, is usually what stops you training something deeper or wider (M26). Recomputation costs one extra forward pass: baseline $1$ forward $+2$ backward $=3$ units, checkpointed $2$ forward $+2$ backward $=4$, so a 33% compute increase for memory falling from $L$ to $\sqrt{L}$.

(4) Worked micro-example. $L=64$, $s=10$ MB per layer. Storing all: 640 MB. With $k=8$: 8 checkpoints (80 MB) plus one segment's 8 layers (80 MB), so 160 MB — a 4× reduction for 33% more compute, and $k=\sqrt{64}=8$ is exactly the optimum.

Gradient checking with finite differences

(1) Intuition. If you cannot trust the derivative your code computed, compare it against one you can compute by brute force: nudge a parameter and watch the loss move.

(2) Formal definition. The central difference

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_i} \approx \frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta_i + h) - \mathcal{L}(\theta_i - h)}{2h} $$

has truncation error of order $h^2$ (specifically $\frac{h^2}{6}$ times the third derivative of $\mathcal{L}$) and roundoff error of order $\epsilon/h$, where $\epsilon$ is the floating-point precision of $\mathcal{L}$. They balance at $h^\star\approx(3\epsilon)^{1/3}$. The standard acceptance test is the relative error $|\bar{\theta}_i - g_i|/\max(10^{-8}, |\bar{\theta}_i|+|g_i|) < 10^{-7}$.

(3) Why it is built this way. It is the only check that catches a transposed matrix, a missing sum over the batch axis, or a detach() in the wrong place — failures that make a network train slowly rather than not at all, and are therefore invisible without a check. The central difference is preferred to the forward difference because its error is $O(h^2)$ instead of $O(h)$.

(4) Worked micro-example. In float64, $\epsilon\approx2.2\times10^{-16}$, so $h^\star\approx(6.6\times10^{-16})^{1/3}\approx8.7\times10^{-6}$ and the achievable relative error is near $10^{-11}$: correct and incorrect gradients separate cleanly. In float32, $\epsilon\approx1.2\times10^{-7}$, giving $h^\star\approx7\times10^{-3}$ and a noise floor near $10^{-5}$ relative — barely usable. Hence: gradient checks in float64, on a small model, never on the real one.

The algorithm, step by step

For an $L$-layer MLP with a scalar loss on a batch of $n$ examples:

  1. Forward, caching pre-activations. For $l=1..L$: $\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}=\mathbf{A}^{(l-1)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)\top}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}^{(l)\top}$; $\mathbf{A}^{(l)}=\phi(\mathbf{Z}^{(l)})$ for $l
  2. Seed the adjoint. $\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(L)}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{Z}^{(L)}$, shape $(n,d_L)$. For softmax plus cross-entropy this is $(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Y})/n$ — one subtraction, derived in the exercises.
  3. Loop backwards, $l=L$ down to $1$: - $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}^{(l)} = \bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l)\top}\mathbf{A}^{(l-1)}$, shape $(d_l,d_{l-1})$; - $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{b}^{(l)} = \mathbf{1}^\top\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l)}$, shape $(d_l,)$, a sum over the batch; - if $l>1$: $\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l-1)} = \left(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\right)\odot\phi'(\mathbf{Z}^{(l-1)})$, shape $(n,d_{l-1})$.
  4. Accumulate into the gradient buffers with +=; fan-out and weight sharing both require it.
  5. Hand the gradients to the optimizer, which is a separate algorithm (M04).

```text forward: for l in 1..L: Z[l] = A[l-1] @ W[l].T + b[l]; A[l] = phi(Z[l]); cache Z[l] backward: dZ[L] = dL_dZ_last(P, Y) # seed, shape (n, d_L) for l in L..1: dW[l] += dZ[l].T @ A[l-1] # (d_l, d_{l-1}) db[l] += dZ[l].sum(axis=0) # (d_l,) if l > 1: dZ[l-1] = (dZ[l] @ W[l]) * phi_prime(Z[l-1]) ```

Complexity. Time: forward $O(n\sum_l d_ld_{l-1})$, backward the same twice over (two matmuls per layer), so a training step costs about $3\times$ the forward pass. Memory: $O(n\sum_l d_l)$ for cached pre-activations plus $O(\sum_l d_ld_{l-1})$ for parameters and gradients — which is why training memory grows with batch size and depth, not with parameter count alone.

Derivations

1. The cost asymmetry, derived

Let $\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}_L\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{f}_1$ with each $\mathbf{f}_l:\mathbb{R}^{d_{l-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_l}$ and Jacobians $\mathbf{J}_l\in\mathbb{R}^{d_l\times d_{l-1}}$. The chain rule gives

$$ \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}_L\mathbf{J}_{L-1}\cdots\mathbf{J}_1 \ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{d_L\times d_0}. $$

A JVP $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}$ is computed by pushing $\mathbf{v}$ forward: $\mathbf{v}_1=\mathbf{J}_1\mathbf{v}$, then $\mathbf{v}_l=\mathbf{J}_l\mathbf{v}_{l-1}$, at the cost of one pass. A VJP $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{J}$ is computed by pulling $\mathbf{u}$ back: $\mathbf{u}_{L-1}=\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{J}_L$, then $\mathbf{u}_{l-1}=\mathbf{u}_l^\top\mathbf{J}_l$, also one pass. For a linear layer $\mathbf{J}_l=\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$, so a JVP is one matvec and a VJP is one matvec: both cost about $c_f$.

Obtaining all $m\cdot n$ entries of $\mathbf{J}$ therefore costs $n\,c_f$ forward or $m\,c_f$ reverse, and

$$ \frac{\text{forward cost}}{\text{reverse cost}} = \frac{n\,c_f}{m\,c_f} = \frac{n}{m} = \frac{\text{number of inputs}}{\text{number of outputs}}. $$

For training, $m=1$ and $n=|\boldsymbol{\theta}|$. A 125-million-parameter model computes its full gradient in one reverse pass at roughly $3\times$ the cost of one forward pass, where forward mode would need 125 million passes — a factor of $1.25\times10^8$. Reverse the shape and the conclusion reverses: a model with one input and a million outputs is cheaper to differentiate forward. The asymmetry is about shape, not about backprop being fast in the abstract.

2. The complete scalar form

One example, one scalar loss $\mathcal{L}$, arbitrary elementwise $\phi$. Write $a^{(0)}_i=x_i$ and define the per-unit adjoint

$$ \delta^{(l)}_j := \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(l)}_j}. $$

Forward, for completeness:

$$ z^{(l)}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{l-1}} W^{(l)}_{ji}a^{(l-1)}_i + b^{(l)}_j, \qquad a^{(l)}_j = \phi\!\left(z^{(l)}_j\right). $$

Step A — the loss derivative. $\delta^{(L)}_k = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z^{(L)}_k$ is supplied by the loss. For cross-entropy with softmax, $\delta^{(L)}_k = p_k - y_k$.

Step B — propagate to the previous activation. For $l

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{(l)}_j} = \sum_{k=1}^{d_{l+1}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(l+1)}_k}\cdot\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_k}{\partial a^{(l)}_j} = \sum_{k=1}^{d_{l+1}}\delta^{(l+1)}_k W^{(l+1)}_{kj}, $$

because $\partial z^{(l+1)}_k/\partial a^{(l)}_j = W^{(l+1)}_{kj}$ is read directly off the forward sum.

Step C — through the nonlinearity. Since $a^{(l)}_j=\phi(z^{(l)}_j)$ depends on $z^{(l)}_j$ alone,

$$ \delta^{(l)}_j = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{(l)}_j}\cdot\frac{\partial a^{(l)}_j}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} = \left(\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l+1}}\delta^{(l+1)}_k W^{(l+1)}_{kj}\right)\phi'\!\left(z^{(l)}_j\right). $$

Step D — the parameter derivatives. $W^{(l)}_{ji}$ appears in exactly one place, the sum for $z^{(l)}_j$, with coefficient $a^{(l-1)}_i$; $b^{(l)}_j$ appears with coefficient 1:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(l)}_{ji}} = \delta^{(l)}_j\,a^{(l-1)}_i, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial b^{(l)}_j} = \delta^{(l)}_j . $$

Steps A–D are the complete algorithm. Written out with no abbreviation for $L=2$, the first-layer weight gradient is the nested sum

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(1)}_{ji}} = \phi'\!\left(\sum_{i'}W^{(1)}_{ji'}x_{i'}+b^{(1)}_j\right)\cdot x_i \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{d_2}\delta^{(2)}_k W^{(2)}_{kj}, $$

where the outer factor is the local slope at the unit, the middle factor is the input that multiplied the weight, and the inner sum is the sensitivity arriving from above.

3. Contracting to matrix form

The sum in Step B is a matvec: $\sum_k \delta^{(l+1)}_k W^{(l+1)}_{kj}$ is component $j$ of $\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}$. Stacking over $j$ gives the vector recursion

$$ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}\right)\odot\phi'\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right), $$

and Step D stacks into an outer product, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}^{(l)} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)\top}$, with shapes $(d_l,1)(1,d_{l-1})\to(d_l,d_{l-1})$ — exactly $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$. The bias gradient is $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{b}^{(l)}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$.

For a batch, let $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_l}$ have $\Delta^{(l)}_{ij}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial Z^{(l)}_{ij}$. The same three statements become

$$ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)} &= \left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l+1)}\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)\odot\phi'\!\left(\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}\right), \\ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(l)}} &= \boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)\top}\mathbf{A}^{(l-1)}, \\ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^{(l)}} &= \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)} . \end{aligned} $$

Check every shape: $(n,d_{l+1})(d_{l+1},d_l)\to(n,d_l)$; $(d_l,n)(n,d_{l-1})\to(d_l,d_{l-1})$; $(1,n)(n,d_l)\to(d_l,)$. The bias gradient being a sum over the batch is the most common bug in hand-written backprop: writing db = dZ silently trains on the last example only.

4. Why fan-out forces accumulation

Suppose $v$ is used by two downstream nodes, $u_1=g(v,\cdot)$ and $u_2=h(v,\cdot)$. Then $\mathcal{L}$ depends on $v$ through both, and the multivariable chain rule gives

$$ \bar{v} = \bar{u}_1\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial v} + \bar{u}_2\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial v}, $$

a sum, not an assignment. A graph node can be reached by many paths (weight tying, a residual stream read twice, one parameter used in two places), so every gradient write must be += onto a zero-initialized buffer. An engine that writes = returns the gradient of one path and drops the rest — a bug that yields plausible-looking but wrong training curves. The scalar engine below is correct by construction: each primitive's closure adds into .grad, and .grad starts at zero.

5. Checkpointing: minimizing $Ls/k + ks$

With $L$ layers of $s$ bytes each and checkpoint interval $k$, peak memory is

$$ M(k) = \underbrace{\frac{L}{k}s}_{\text{permanent checkpoints}} + \underbrace{ks}_{\text{one recomputed segment}} . $$

Differentiating and setting to zero:

$$ M'(k) = -\frac{Ls}{k^2} + s = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ k^2 = L \ \Longrightarrow\ k=\sqrt{L}, \qquad M(\sqrt{L}) = 2s\sqrt{L}. $$

Memory falls from $Ls$ to $2s\sqrt{L}$: a factor of 5 at $L=100$ and 16 at $L=1000$. The compute price is fixed — you redo each segment's forward pass once, so work rises from $1+2=3$ units to $2+2=4$, a 33% increase independent of $k$. That independence is why checkpointing is nearly free to decide on: a constant-factor tax for an asymptotic memory win.

6. Where the finite-difference error comes from

Taylor expand around $\theta$:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta+h) = \mathcal{L} + h\mathcal{L}' + \tfrac{h^2}{2}\mathcal{L}'' + \tfrac{h^3}{6}\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime} + O(h^4), \qquad \mathcal{L}(\theta-h) = \mathcal{L} - h\mathcal{L}' + \tfrac{h^2}{2}\mathcal{L}'' - \tfrac{h^3}{6}\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime} + O(h^4). $$

Subtracting cancels the even terms:

$$ \frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta+h)-\mathcal{L}(\theta-h)}{2h} = \mathcal{L}' + \frac{h^2}{6}\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime} + O(h^4), $$

so the truncation error is $\frac{h^2}{6}|\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}|$. Separately, evaluating $\mathcal{L}$ costs a rounding error near $\epsilon|\mathcal{L}|$, so the difference has error about $2\epsilon|\mathcal{L}|$, which the division by $2h$ amplifies to $\epsilon|\mathcal{L}|/h$. The total

$$ E(h) = \frac{h^2}{6}|\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}| + \frac{\epsilon|\mathcal{L}|}{h} $$

is minimized where $\frac{h}{3}|\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}| = \epsilon|\mathcal{L}|/h^2$, i.e.

$$ h^\star = \left(\frac{3\epsilon|\mathcal{L}|}{|\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}|}\right)^{1/3}, $$

and at that step size the two terms are comparable, giving $E(h^\star)=O(\epsilon^{2/3})$: about $10^{-11}$ in float64 and $10^{-5}$ in float32. That is why a float32 gradient check cannot distinguish a correct implementation from one with a subtly wrong reduction.

Implementation from scratch

A scalar reverse-mode engine in about sixty lines: the Value class, five primitives, and a backward pass built on a topological sort. This is the same algorithm PyTorch runs on tensors; only the container changes.

```python import math class Value: def __init__(self, data, parents=(), op=""): self.data = float(data); self.grad = 0.0 self._parents = parents; self._op = op self._backward = lambda: None # local vjp, filled by each op def __add__(self, other): other = other if isinstance(other, Value) else Value(other) out = Value(self.data + other.data, (self, other), "+") def _backward(): self.grad += out.grad # d(a+b)/da = 1 other.grad += out.grad # d(a+b)/db = 1 out._backward = _backward; return out def __mul__(self, other): other = other if isinstance(other, Value) else Value(other) out = Value(self.data * other.data, (self, other), "*") def _backward(): self.grad += other.data * out.grad # d(ab)/da = b other.grad += self.data * out.grad # d(ab)/db = a out._backward = _backward; return out def tanh(self): t = math.tanh(self.data) out = Value(t, (self,), "tanh") def _backward(): self.grad += (1.0 - t * t) * out.grad out._backward = _backward; return out def sigmoid(self): s = 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-self.data)) out = Value(s, (self,), "sigmoid") def _backward(): self.grad += s * (1.0 - s) * out.grad out._backward = _backward; return out def log(self): out = Value(math.log(self.data), (self,), "log") def _backward(): self.grad += out.grad / self.data out._backward = _backward; return out def __neg__(self): return self * -1 def __sub__(self, other): return self + (-other) def __radd__(self, other): return self + other def __rmul__(self, other): return self * other def backward(self): topo, seen = [], set() def build(v): # DFS postorder gives topological order if id(v) in seen: return seen.add(id(v)) for p in v._parents: build(p) topo.append(v) build(self) self.grad = 1.0 # dL/dL = 1, the seed cotangent for v in reversed(topo): # reverse topological order v._backward() ```

Run the worked example below with it. Two checks worth doing immediately: the fan-out test

```python d = Value(2.0) e = d * d + d # dL/dd = 2d + 1 = 5 when d = 2 e.backward() print(e.data, d.grad) # 6.0 5.0 ```

and the finite-difference comparison from the worked example, which agrees to $2.4\times10^{-7}$ at $h=10^{-6}$ in float64. If the fan-out test prints 6.0 4.0 you wrote = instead of += and dropped one path.

Worked example

A complete backward pass over a 2-2-1 network, every intermediate visible. Inputs $\mathbf{x}=(1,-2)^\top$, target $y=1$, hidden activation $\tanh$, output sigmoid, loss $\mathcal{L}=-\log p$ (binary cross-entropy at $y=1$).

$$ \mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.8\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{W}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -0.7\end{pmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.05 . $$

Forward.

quantity computation value
$z^{(1)}_1$ $0.5(1)+(-0.3)(-2)+0.1$ $1.20000000$
$z^{(1)}_2$ $0.2(1)+0.8(-2)+(-0.1)$ $-1.50000000$
$a^{(1)}_1$ $\tanh(1.2)$ $0.83365461$
$a^{(1)}_2$ $\tanh(-1.5)$ $-0.90514825$
$z^{(2)}$ $1.5(0.83365461)+(-0.7)(-0.90514825)+0.05$ $1.93408569$
$p$ $\sigma(1.93408569)$ $0.87370095$
$\mathcal{L}$ $-\log(0.87370095)$ $0.13501712$

Backward, step by step.

Seed. The loss is $\mathcal{L}=-\log p$, so $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial p = -1/p = -1.14455638$. The sigmoid has $\sigma'=p(1-p)=0.11034760$, so

$$ \delta^{(2)} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}} = -1.14455638 \times 0.11034760 = -0.12629905 = p - y . $$

That the two factors collapse to $p-y$ is not a coincidence: it is the softmax/sigmoid-plus-cross-entropy cancellation holding in the scalar case.

Output layer weights. $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(2)}_j = \delta^{(2)}a^{(1)}_j$:

$j$ $\delta^{(2)}$ $a^{(1)}_j$ $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(2)}_j$
1 $-0.12629905$ $0.83365461$ $-0.10528978$
2 $-0.12629905$ $-0.90514825$ $0.11431936$

and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b^{(2)} = \delta^{(2)} = -0.12629905$.

Into the hidden layer. $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^{(1)}_j = W^{(2)}_j\delta^{(2)}$:

$j$ $W^{(2)}_j$ $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^{(1)}_j$ $1-a_j^2$ $\delta^{(1)}_j$
1 $1.5$ $-0.18944857$ $0.30502000$ $-0.05778560$
2 $-0.7$ $0.08840933$ $0.18070664$ $0.01597615$

Input layer weights. $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(1)}_{ji} = \delta^{(1)}_j x_i$, so each row of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ gets the hidden adjoint times the input vector:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(1)}} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.05778560 & 0.11557120 \\ 0.01597615 & -0.03195231\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^{(1)}} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.05778560 \\ 0.01597615\end{pmatrix}. $$

Verify one entry two ways. Chain the four local derivatives for $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(1)}_{11}$ (the coefficient $0.5$ on $x_1=1$):

$$ (-0.12629905)\times(1.5)\times(0.30502000)\times(1) = -0.05778560, $$

which matches $\delta^{(1)}_1 x_1$. And for $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(1)}_{12}$ (the coefficient $-0.3$ on $x_2=-2$): $(-0.12629905)(1.5)(0.305020)(-2) = 0.11557120$, matching $\delta^{(1)}_1x_2$.

One SGD step, $\eta=0.5$: $\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\leftarrow(1.55264489,\,-0.75715968)$, $b^{(2)}\leftarrow 0.11314953$, $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\leftarrow\begin{pmatrix}0.52889280 & -0.35778560\\ 0.19201192 & 0.81597616\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b}^{(1)}\leftarrow(0.12889280,\,-0.10798808)$, and the loss falls from $0.13501712$ to $0.10809095$. Continuing at that step size: $0.091579$ at step 2, $0.063943$ at step 5, $0.042985$ at step 10, $0.026079$ at step 20, $0.018734$ at step 30, with $p$ rising from $0.8737$ to $0.9814$ — a monotone approach to the target, exactly as the gradient direction promises for a small enough step.

Gradient check. Central differences on all nine parameters at $h=10^{-6}$ agree with the table above to a maximum absolute error of $2.4\times10^{-7}$, which is the expected truncation error of order $h^2$ plus roundoff of order $\epsilon/h$ from the derivation above.

Common misconceptions

"Backpropagation is how the brain learns."

Backprop requires the backward signal to be multiplied by the transpose of the forward weight. No known biological mechanism transports a synaptic weight into a feedback pathway, so the algorithm as written is not implementable by the cortex in any obvious way. Whether some approximation of it is implemented is a live question, and the honest state of the art is: unknown.

"Backpropagation is the learning algorithm."

It computes $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}$ and stops. The learning algorithm is the update rule — SGD, momentum, Adam, or something else (M04). Every claim of the form "backprop is unstable / slow / forgets" is really a claim about a gradient interacting with an optimizer and a data distribution.

"Backpropagation needs a different derivation for each architecture."

It needs one rule: the local vjp of each primitive. A CNN, an RNN unrolled over time, an attention block, and an MLP differ in which primitives appear and in how the graph is shaped, not in the differentiation algorithm. If you find yourself deriving a new backward pass for a new architecture, you have probably skipped the vjp step and started from the loss again.

"Reverse mode forms the Jacobian and then contracts it."

It never forms it. For a matmul with a $10^6\times10^6$ Jacobian, the vjp is two matmuls of the forward size. The whole point of the adjoint formulation is that cost scales with the forward computation, not with the Jacobian's size.

"zero_grad() is optional bookkeeping."

Gradients accumulate because the chain rule sums over paths, so without zeroing you are accumulating across steps and silently training with a growing, stale gradient. The same += that makes fan-out correct makes forgetting to zero a bug. Frameworks differ in whether zeroing is implicit, which is a reliable source of cross-framework confusion.

"If the loss goes down, the gradients must be right."

A wrong gradient that is roughly aligned with the true one still decreases the loss. Transposed matrix gradients in small layers, missing batch reductions, and detached detours all produce monotone-looking loss curves at reduced speed. Only a finite-difference check in float64 distinguishes "correct" from "approximately correct".

Exercises

Warm-up (check understanding)

  1. Compute $\partial f/\partial x$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ for $f=(xy+z)^2$ at $x=1,y=2,z=3$ by traversing the graph, then confirm with the engine.
  2. For widths $784\to128\to64\to10$, count the matmuls in one forward and one backward pass and state the ratio.
  3. Why must db be a sum over the batch axis rather than a copy of dZ?
Solutions 1. $xy=2$, $xy+z=5$, $f=25$. $\partial f/\partial(xy+z)=2(5)=10$, $\partial(xy)/\partial x=y=2$, $\partial(xy)/\partial y=x=1$, so $\partial f/\partial x=10\cdot2=20$ and $\partial f/\partial y=10\cdot1=10$; $\partial f/\partial z=10$. The engine reproduces these exactly. 2. Forward: 3 matmuls. Backward: 2 per layer (input gradient and weight gradient) $=6$, so the ratio is 2, and a full step is $3\times$ the forward cost. 3. Because $b^{(l)}_j$ is added to every row of $\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}$, so it influences the loss through all $n$ examples: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b^{(l)}_j=\sum_{i=1}^n\Delta^{(l)}_{ij}$. Copying `dZ` uses only the last row and trains on one example per step.

Core (do the math)

  1. Derive $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(L)}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Y}$ (per-example) for softmax followed by cross-entropy, starting from $\mathcal{L}=-\frac1n\sum_i\log P_{i,y_i}$.
  2. A primitive broadcasts: $\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times4}$ and $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^4$. Given $\bar{\mathbf{C}}$, derive $\bar{\mathbf{A}}$ and $\bar{\mathbf{b}}$ and explain the reduction.
  3. A 12-layer network costs $s=8$ MB of activations per layer. Give peak activation memory storing everything, with checkpointing at $k=4$, and at the optimal $k$.
Solutions 4. $\log P_{i,y_i}=Z_{i,y_i}-\log\sum_k e^{Z_{ik}}$, so $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial Z_{ij}=-\frac1n\left(\mathbf{1}[j=y_i]-P_{ij}\right)=\frac1n(P_{ij}-Y_{ij})$. Uniformly, $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(L)}=(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Y})/n$: the softmax Jacobian and the log cancel exactly, which is why frameworks implement them as one fused op. 5. $\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\bar{\mathbf{C}}$ (shape preserved) and $\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{1}^\top\bar{\mathbf{C}}$, i.e. the sum of $\bar{\mathbf{C}}$ down the broadcast axis. The forward operation *replicated* $\mathbf{b}$ three times, so the backward operation must *sum* the three copies: each copy contributed a separate path, and paths add. 6. Storing all: $12\times8=96$ MB. With $k=4$: $12/4=3$ checkpoints (24 MB) plus one 4-layer segment (32 MB) $=56$ MB. Optimal $k=\sqrt{12}\approx3.46$, so $k=3$ or $4$: $2\times8\sqrt{12}\approx55$ MB. The theoretical win over storing everything is a factor of $\sqrt{12}\approx3.5$.

Stretch (build or break it)

  1. Add a relu and exp primitive to the engine, then write a finite-difference harness that checks every parameter of a small MLP and prints the maximum relative error. Confirm you see $<10^{-7}$ in float64 and explain what changes in float32.
  2. Construct the minimal graph in which a detach() (replacing a node by a constant) yields exactly zero gradient for a parameter that clearly affects the loss, and explain which edge of the chain rule was cut.
Hints and solutions 7. `relu` needs `self.grad += (1.0 if self.data > 0 else 0.0) * out.grad`, and `exp` needs `self.grad += math.exp(self.data) * out.grad`. The harness perturb one parameter at a time by $h=10^{-6}$ and compares $(\mathcal{L}(\theta+h)-\mathcal{L}(\theta-h))/2h$ with `.grad`. In float64 the noise floor is around $10^{-10}$; in float32 the roundoff term $\epsilon|\mathcal{L}|/h$ rises to about $10^{-4}$ relative, and no step size fixes it, because the truncation term wants $h$ large and the roundoff term wants $h$ small. 8. Take `loss = (x * 2).data` as a float, or in a framework `y = x.detach() * 2; loss = y.sum()`. The multiplication happens outside the graph, so `x` has no children that reach the loss: the path from `x` to `loss` through the cut edge is removed from the sum over paths, and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x$ is exactly zero even though the loss numerically depends on `x`. Detach is precise and silent, which is why it is dangerous.

Self-test

  1. State the chain rule for a node with fan-out in a computational graph, and say what += implements.
  2. Why does reverse mode cost $m$ passes and forward mode $n$ passes for a full Jacobian?
  3. Write the three lines of the backward loop for an MLP, with shapes.
  4. Where does the seed $(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Y})/n$ come from, and why is it so simple?
  5. Give the memory and compute cost of checkpointing with interval $k=\sqrt{L}$.
  6. Why must gradient checking use float64, and what step size should it use?
Answers 1. $\bar{v}=\sum_{u\in\text{children}(v)}\bar{u}\,\partial u/\partial v$: the total derivative is a sum over outgoing paths. `+=` implements that sum, and it is why gradients must be zeroed between steps. 2. One reverse pass produces one row of $\mathbf{J}$ (a cotangent for all inputs), so $m$ rows need $m$ passes; one forward pass produces one column (a tangent for all outputs), so $n$ columns need $n$ passes. Each pass costs about one forward evaluation. 3. $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}^{(l)}=\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l)\top}\mathbf{A}^{(l-1)}$ of shape $(d_l,d_{l-1})$; $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{b}^{(l)}=\mathbf{1}^\top\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l)}$ of shape $(d_l,)$; $\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l-1)}=(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)})\odot\phi'(\mathbf{Z}^{(l-1)})$ of shape $(n,d_{l-1})$. 4. The softmax Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^\top$ multiplied by $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{s}=-\mathbf{y}/\mathbf{s}$ collapses to $\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{y}$, because $\mathbf{y}$ is one-hot. Frameworks fuse the two ops to avoid both the cancellation and the numerical error. 5. Memory $2s\sqrt{L}$ instead of $Ls$; compute rises from 3 units to 4 per step, a 33% increase independent of $k$. 6. Because the finite-difference error is $h^2$-truncation plus $\epsilon/h$ roundoff, minimized at $h^\star\approx(3\epsilon)^{1/3}$ with an error floor near $\epsilon^{2/3}$ — around $10^{-11}$ in float64 but only $10^{-5}$ in float32, where a wrong reduction is indistinguishable from a correct one.

Further reading

  • Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors" (1986). The paper that made backprop famous; extract the worked examples of the delta rule, not the philosophy.
  • Linnainmaa, "The representation of the cumulative rounding error of an algorithm as a Taylor expansion of the local rounding errors" (1970). The first description of reverse-mode differentiation, a decade and a half before the neural-network version. Worth seeing how the same idea appears independently in numerical analysis.
  • Griewank and Walther, Evaluating Derivatives (2008). The reference text for automatic differentiation: checkpointing, the cost model for forward versus reverse, and the correctness arguments behind the "cheap gradient" principle.
  • Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul, and Siskind, "Automatic Differentiation in Machine Learning: a Survey" (2018). The clearest short account of forward, reverse, and mixed mode with the same cost accounting used here. Free on arXiv.
  • Chen, Xu, Zhang, and Guestrin, "Training Deep Nets with Sublinear Memory Cost" (2016). Checkpointing as the $\sqrt{L}$ trade-off, with the recomputation schedule spelled out.
  • Karpathy, "micrograd" (2020, open source). A scalar autograd engine in about a hundred lines of Python, and the direct ancestor of the implementation above. Reading it after writing your own is the fastest way to find what you missed.
