Training Dynamics and Generalization

Why deep networks train at all, and why they generalize when theory says they should not.

on this page

Why this module exists

M07 gave you a correct gradient. Almost nothing about whether training works follows from that: the same gradient, the same architecture, and the same data produce a model that trains in one configuration and diverges, stalls, or memorizes in another. The difference is made by initialization scale, normalization placement, learning-rate schedule, regularization, and numerical precision — a set of interventions that look like folklore until you derive what each one does to the distribution of activations and gradients. This module supplies those derivations, and then confronts the part of deep learning that has no settled explanation: overparameterized networks are large enough to memorize random labels, yet they fit real data in a way that generalizes. Double descent, grokking, and the flat-minima story are all attempts at that explanation, and you should leave this module knowing which parts are theorems, which are empirical regularities, and which are contested.

Two conventions to fix immediately. First, $g$ (init gain) is a variance multiplier, not a spectral norm: for a weight matrix with iid entries of variance $\sigma_w^2$ in $d$ dimensions, the squared singular values concentrate near $d\sigma_w^2$, so $\sigma_w=\sqrt{g^2/d}$ gives a spectral norm near $g$. Second, "normalization" always means an affine map after a standardizing transform: $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ then $\gamma\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\beta$.

Core concepts

What SGD actually sees

(1) Intuition. The loss is a surface in a space with $10^8$ dimensions. You never see the surface; you see one minibatch's noisy tangent plane, and you move along it. Whatever "the loss landscape" means, what your optimizer experiences is the minibatch's gradient plus noise.

(2) Formal definition. With full-batch gradient $\mathbf{g}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}$ and minibatch estimate $\hat{\mathbf{g}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell_i$, the estimate is unbiased, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{g}}]=\mathbf{g}$, with covariance $\Sigma/B$, so $\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}\|$ scales like $1/\sqrt{B}$. SGD is a stochastic approximation whose stationary distribution has width set by the ratio of the learning rate to the noise: the "temperature" is $\eta B$ relative to the gradient noise scale. That is why large batches and large learning rates can behave alike, and why both flatten the trajectory's effective noise.

(3) Why it is built this way. Because computing the exact gradient on $10^8$ examples per step is not affordable, and because the noise turns out to be useful: it is what lets a stochastic process escape sharp directions, and it is the mechanism behind the batch-size/learning-rate coupling you will use when scaling training (M25).

(4) Worked micro-example. A quadratic loss with curvature matrix having eigenvalues $\lambda_{\max}=10$ and $\lambda_{\min}=0.1$ is trained by gradient descent. The step is stable only for $\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}=0.2$, while the slowest direction needs $\eta\approx1/\lambda_{\min}=10$ to converge in reasonable time. The condition number 100 means no single learning rate is comfortable for both directions — the origin of the elongated-valley picture and of momentum's usefulness.

Vanishing and exploding gradients

(1) Intuition. The backward pass multiplies one Jacobian per layer. If each multiplier has norm slightly below 1, the product vanishes exponentially; slightly above 1, it explodes. Depth is the exponent.

(2) Formal definition. With $\mathbf{a}^{(l)}=\phi(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})$ and $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}=\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}$,

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{a}^{(L)}}{\partial\mathbf{a}^{(0)}} = \prod_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{D}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)}, \qquad \mathbf{D}^{(l)}=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\phi'(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})\right), $$

so taking operator norms, $\|\partial\mathbf{a}^{(L)}/\partial\mathbf{a}^{(0)}\|\le\prod_l\|\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\|\,\|\phi'\|_\infty$. With $\|\mathbf{W}\|\approx g$ and $\|\phi'\|_\infty=1/4$ for sigmoid, the per-layer factor is $g/4$; with $g=1$ the factor is $1/4$ and the product after 20 layers is $4^{-20}\approx9\times10^{-13}$.

(3) Why it is built this way. Because it explains why the sigmoid era required either very careful initialization or very shallow nets, and why ReLU changed the arithmetic: $\|\phi'\|_\infty=1$ on the active half, so the per-layer factor becomes $g$ and the critical gain is $g=1$ instead of $g=4$. The same product governs the forward pass's activation scale, so "activations vanish" and "gradients vanish" are the same disease seen from two directions.

(4) Worked micro-example. Simulated 20-layer tanh and sigmoid stacks, width 64, measuring the ratio $\|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{h}_0\| / \|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{h}_{20}\|$:

$L$ tanh, gain 1 sigmoid, gain 1 tanh, gain 0.5
4 $8.0$ $7.3\times10^{-2}$ $2.3\times10^{-1}$
8 $4.9$ $2.1\times10^{-4}$ $1.2\times10^{-3}$
12 $5.0$ $4.4\times10^{-7}$ $8.2\times10^{-6}$
16 $6.0$ $1.8\times10^{-9}$ $5.2\times10^{-8}$

The healthy tanh column is flat because gain 1 sits near the edge of chaos for tanh; the sigmoid column loses nine orders of magnitude by depth 20, and the subcritical tanh column loses ten. Nothing about the data or the optimizer caused this — it is the exponent in the product.

Residual connections as identity-gradient highways

(1) Intuition. A residual connection adds the input to the output, so the backward pass gets a copy of the gradient that skips the layer entirely. One additive path is enough to keep the signal alive; you no longer need every layer's Jacobian to be well conditioned, only the sum to be.

(2) Formal definition. For $\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}=\mathbf{h}^{(l)}+\mathbf{f}_l(\mathbf{h}^{(l)})$,

$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(0)}} = \prod_{l=0}^{L-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{J}_l\right) = \mathbf{I} + \sum_l \mathbf{J}_l + \sum_{l

where $\mathbf{J}_l=\partial\mathbf{f}_l/\partial\mathbf{h}^{(l)}$. The leading $\mathbf{I}$ means $\|\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}/\partial\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\|\ge 1-\|\sum_l\mathbf{J}_l\|-\cdots$: as long as the branch Jacobians are small, the identity dominates and the gradient passes through essentially unchanged.

(3) Why it is built this way. It converts an exponential sensitivity to depth into a linear one. Each term of the expansion corresponds to a path that "uses" a subset of the layers, so the gradient is a sum over $2^L$ paths, most of which are short. That is why a 100-layer residual network trains at all, and why removing residual connections from a transformer makes it untrainable past a few blocks.

(4) Worked micro-example. The same simulation with $\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}=\mathbf{h}^{(l)}+\tanh(\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)})$ and a small branch gain of 0.2 keeps the input gradient norm between $60$ and $122$ for $L=4$ to $20$ — no decay at all, in contrast to the $10^{-10}$ collapse of the vanilla stack. The residual changes the exponent of the problem, not its constant.

Normalization: what is normalized, over what

(1) Intuition. Normalization rescales each layer's inputs to a fixed distribution before the next layer sees them, so no layer has to compensate for another layer's scale drift. The three variants differ only in which axis they average over.

(2) Formal definition. For activations $\mathbf{z}$:

  • Batch norm, over the batch axis, per feature: $\mu_j=\frac1B\sum_i z_{ij}$, $\sigma_j^2=\frac1B\sum_i(z_{ij}-\mu_j)^2$, $\hat z_{ij}=(z_{ij}-\mu_j)/\sqrt{\sigma_j^2+\varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}$, $y_{ij}=\gamma_j\hat z_{ij}+\beta_j$. During training $\mu,\sigma$ are the batch statistics and running estimates are accumulated; during evaluation the running estimates are used.
  • Layer norm, over the feature axis, per example: $\mu_i=\frac1d\sum_j z_{ij}$, $\sigma_i^2=\frac1d\sum_j(z_{ij}-\mu_i)^2$, then the same affine map. Identical at train and eval, no batch coupling.
  • RMSNorm, per example, without centring: $y_{ij}=\gamma_j z_{ij}/\sqrt{\frac1d\sum_k z_{ik}^2+\varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}$.

The batch-norm backward pass is derived in full below; the result is

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_{ij}} = \frac{\gamma_j}{\sigma_j}\left(g_{ij} - \frac1B\sum_k g_{kj} - \hat z_{ij}\cdot\frac1B\sum_k g_{kj}\hat z_{kj}\right), $$

with $g_{ij}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_{ij}$.

(3) Why it is built this way. Batch norm decouples the scale of one layer from the scale of the next, which stabilizes the per-layer gain without you having to compute it; it also injects noise through the batch statistics, which acts as a mild regularizer. The price is three couplings: the output for example $i$ depends on the other examples in the batch; train and eval use different statistics, so they compute different functions; and small batches make the statistics noisy, in the extreme (batch size 1) degenerate. Layer norm removes all three at the cost of one pass over features per example, and it handles variable-length sequences, which is why it, not batch norm, is in every transformer (M12). RMSNorm removes the mean subtraction on the argument that the shift is carried by the following bias anyway, saving one reduction pass.

(4) Worked micro-example. $\mathbf{z}=(1,2,3,4)$ with $\gamma=1,\beta=0$: $\mu=2.5$, $\sigma^2=1.25$, $\sigma=1.1180$, $\hat{\mathbf{z}}=(-1.3416,-0.4472,0.4472,1.3416)$, so $\mathbf{y}=\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Backward with $g_i=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_i=(0,1,0,0)$: $\frac1B\sum g = 0.25$, $\frac1B\sum g\hat z = -0.1118$, and

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = 0.8944\left(g_i - 0.25 + 0.1118\,\hat z_i\right) = (-0.3578,\ 0.6261,\ -0.1789,\ -0.0894). $$

The four components sum to zero, which must hold: adding a constant to every $z_i$ does not change $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, so the gradient has no component along $\mathbf{1}$.

Regularization: five interventions and what each does

(1) Intuition. Every regularizer restricts the function class or the trajectory. Dropout removes units at random so no unit can rely on a specific partner; weight decay shrinks parameters so the function stays smooth; label smoothing caps confidence; augmentation declares invariances you already believe; early stopping halts before the trajectory reaches the memorizing region.

(2) Formal definitions. With activation $\mathbf{h}$ and drop probability $p$: train with $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{h}/(1-p)$, $\mathbf{m}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p)$ elementwise; evaluate with $\mathbf{h}$ unchanged. Weight decay: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$, giving the update $\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow(1-\eta\lambda)\boldsymbol{\theta}-\eta\mathbf{g}$ (decoupled form, used in AdamW). Label smoothing with rate $\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}$: $\mathcal{L}=-(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})\log p_y-\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{K}\sum_k\log p_k$. Augmentation: sample $\mathbf{x}'=a(\mathbf{x})$ with $a$ from an invariance group and train on $(\mathbf{x}',y)$.

(3) Why it is built this way — and the arithmetic that makes each defensible. Dropout's $1/(1-p)$ factor exists so that $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{h}}]=\mathbf{h}$ and inference needs no correction (derived below). Weight decay is not merely "smaller weights": for a linear model it is exactly a prior, and for SGD-with-L2 it is equivalent to decay, but for adaptive optimizers the coupling between the decay and the second-moment estimate distorts the effective rate — which is the whole reason AdamW decouples it. Label smoothing does something sharper than "prevent overconfidence": it caps the achievable logit gap at $\log\!\left(K(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})/\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}\right)$, a finite number you can compute (Derivations, §4), which prevents the softmax from saturating and therefore keeps the gradient from vanishing. Augmentation is the strongest regularizer when you have a genuine invariance — the invariance is a fact about the task, not a modelling trick. Early stopping is the cheapest of the five and, for a quadratic loss, provably equivalent to L2 regularization with a particular $\lambda$.

(4) Worked micro-example. A unit with activation $h=2.0$ and $p=0.5$: during training it is kept with probability $0.5$, and when kept its value is $2.0/0.5=4.0$. The expectation is $0.5\times4.0+0.5\times0=2.0$, matching the inference value. The variance is $h^2p/(1-p)=4.0$ — a standard deviation of $2.0$ on a mean of $2.0$, which is a large perturbation and is exactly why dropout is a strong regularizer. For label smoothing with $K=10$, $\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}=0.1$: optimum $p_y=K(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})/(K-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})=9/9.9=0.9091$, $p_k=\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}/(K-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})=0.0101$, and the logit gap is capped at $\log 90=4.4998$ rather than diverging to infinity.

Overfitting, underfitting, double descent, and grokking

(1) Intuition. Underfitting is a model too simple to represent the pattern; overfitting is a model that represents the training set's noise as if it were pattern. The classical picture says error falls then rises as capacity grows. The modern picture says it falls, rises at the interpolation threshold, then falls again.

(2) Formal definitions. With train loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{tr}}$ and held-out loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$: underfitting is high $\mathcal{L}_{\text{tr}}$ and high $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$; overfitting is low $\mathcal{L}_{\text{tr}}$ and high $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$. Double descent (model-wise) is the empirical phenomenon that $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$ peaks near the capacity at which the model can just interpolate the training set, then decreases as capacity grows further; the sample-wise version peaks when the number of examples is just enough to interpolate. Grokking is the empirical phenomenon that on some small algorithmic tasks, validation accuracy jumps from chance to near-perfect long after training accuracy has saturated.

(3) Why it is built this way. The classical U-curve came from a bias-variance decomposition that assumes the model class is fixed and the estimator is the minimum-norm solution in a fixed basis; it fails once the model can interpolate, because then the variance term no longer grows the way the decomposition assumes. Double descent is a description, not yet a theory, and the mechanism is contested: candidate explanations include the behaviour of the pseudo-inverse solution as the design matrix becomes square, and the implicit regularization of SGD. Grokking is more contested still — it is reproducible on modular-arithmetic-style tasks and its relationship to weight decay, to the training data fraction, and to "phase transitions" in the network's internal algorithms is an active research topic.

(4) Worked micro-example. A 2-layer network on $n=100$ examples with $d=500$ inputs: the number of parameters exceeds $n$ by a factor of 50, so a minimum-norm interpolant exists, and the model can reach zero training loss with random labels. The classical capacity argument predicts catastrophic overfitting; what is observed is that the same model trained on real labels reaches near-zero training loss and still generalizes. That gap between prediction and observation is the subject of the next subsection.

Why overparameterized networks generalize at all

(1) Intuition. A model with far more parameters than data points should be able to memorize; the puzzle is that gradient descent's particular solution is a simple one. The optimizer is choosing among the many interpolants, and it is choosing simple ones.

(2) The four candidate accounts, with their status.

  • Flat minima. A wide basin is argued to be robust, since small parameter changes do not change the function. Status: historically influential (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in the 1990s; Keskar and coauthors in 2017 on sharp minima from large batches), but weakened by the fact that sharp minima generalize too — a reparametrization can make any minimum arbitrarily sharp without changing the function, so flatness in weight space is not obviously flatness in function space.
  • Implicit bias of the optimizer. Gradient descent does not pick a random interpolant: for separable linear classification it provably converges in direction to the max-margin solution, and for overdetermined linear regression to the minimum-norm solution. Status: theorems for linear and linearized settings; an active research direction for deep nonlinear nets.
  • Compression / minimum description length. A network whose weights and training set can be compressed to a short description has a generalization bound, and some overparameterized nets do compress enough for the bound to be non-vacuous. Status: real theorems, often loose bounds.
  • Ensembling and the role of noise. SGD's trajectory visits many parameter settings, and the predictor behaves like an average over them. Status: descriptive, partially quantified.

(3) Why it matters that none of these is settled. Because it changes what you should do when a model fails to generalize. If flatness were the mechanism you would monitor sharpness; if implicit bias were, you would control the optimizer's trajectory; if compression were, you would regularize description length. The honest position: all four have supporting evidence, none is a complete account.

(4) Worked micro-example. Take a 3-layer network with 100k parameters and 1k training examples. It can fit random labels perfectly (a memorization demonstration you can run in minutes), yet trained on real labels it generalizes. Same architecture, same optimizer, same number of parameters — the only difference is the label structure. Any theory of generalization that depends only on parameter count cannot distinguish these two cases, which is why the count-based explanations were abandoned.

Learning rate: finding, warmup, and schedules

(1) Intuition. The learning rate is the one hyperparameter that can turn a working architecture into a diverging one. Peak learning rate sets how far you move; warmup and decay set how that distance changes over training, and both encode information about the curvature of the loss at that point in training.

(2) Formal definition. A typical schedule is

$$ \eta_t = \begin{cases} \eta_{\text{peak}}\dfrac{t}{T_w} & t

a linear warmup to $\eta_{\text{peak}}$ over $T_w$ steps followed by cosine decay to (near) zero over the remaining $T-T_w$ steps.

(3) Why it is built this way. Warmup exists for three concrete reasons: (i) even a correct initialization produces gradient estimates whose variance is large early, and a full-size step in a random direction can leave the basin; (ii) Adam's second-moment estimate $\mathbf{v}$ starts at zero and is badly biased for the first few hundred steps, so the effective step size is artificially large until $\mathbf{v}$ fills in; (iii) post-LN transformers have gradient norms that grow with depth early in training, so large steps destabilize them. Decay exists because near the end of training you want to average over a neighbourhood rather than bounce around it — hence cosine, linear, or step decay. The choice of schedule mostly matters at the end: constant LR plateaus leave noise in the final weights, whereas decay lets the final phase act as an implicit ensemble.

(4) Worked micro-example. $\eta_{\text{peak}}=10^{-3}$, $T_w=2000$, $T=100{,}000$. At step 0 the LR is 0; at step 1000 it is $5\times10^{-4}$; at step 2000 it is $10^{-3}$; at step 51,000 it is $5\times10^{-4}$; at step 99,000 it is $10^{-6}$ and falling. A diverging run usually shows its failure in the first few hundred steps — which is precisely the window warmup covers.

Drag the warmup length and the peak learning rate and watch the schedule curve and the resulting step size update live. Watch what a short warmup does at the very start: the first steps are already at full size, which is the regime where a diverging run dies.

Mixed precision and loss scaling

(1) Intuition. Half-precision arithmetic is roughly twice as fast and half the memory, but its exponent range is narrow, so small gradients round to zero. Multiplying the loss by a large constant moves every gradient into the representable range, and you divide it back out before stepping.

(2) Formal definition. fp16 has a 5-bit exponent: normal range up to $65{,}504$ and down to $6.1\times10^{-5}$ (plus subnormals to $6.0\times10^{-8}$); bf16 has an 8-bit exponent like fp32 but a 7-bit mantissa, so its range is fine while its precision is about three decimal digits. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by $S$ before the backward pass, so every gradient is computed at $S\times$ its true value; you unscale (divide by $S$) before clipping or stepping. Dynamic scaling adjusts $S$: on an inf or NaN in the gradients, skip the step and halve $S$; after $N$ consecutive finite steps, double it.

(3) Why it is built this way. Underflow, not overflow, is the binding constraint: a gradient of $10^{-8}$ in fp16 is exactly zero, and a zero gradient is silently lost training signal. With $S=2^{16}=65{,}536$ the same gradient becomes $6.6\times10^{-4}$, comfortably representable, and the smallest representable gradient falls to $6.1\times10^{-5}/65{,}536\approx9.3\times10^{-10}$. You keep fp32 master weights because accumulating $10^5$ updates of size $10^{-5}$ into a 11-bit mantissa loses the updates entirely.

(4) Worked micro-example. A parameter at $1.0$ in fp16 has spacing $2^{-10}\approx9.8\times10^{-4}$; an update of $1\times10^{-5}$ rounds to zero. The same parameter in bf16 has spacing $2^{-8}\approx3.9\times10^{-3}$ — worse — which is why bf16 training always keeps fp32 master weights and casts only for the matmuls. bf16 needs no loss scaling at all because its exponent range matches fp32; that is why it displaced fp16 in large-scale training.

Debugging a training run

(1) Intuition. The loss curve, the gradient norm, and the activation histograms are the only three signals you get. Each failure mode has a signature, and you should be able to name the signature before you see it.

(2) The signatures, as a checklist.

Symptom Most likely cause First check
loss $\approx\log K$ and never moves zero LR, detached graph, frozen params gradient norm at step 1
loss spikes then recovers LR too high or one bad batch per-step grad norm, batch content
loss goes to NaN log(0), division by zero, exp overflow, unnormalized attention which layer produced the first non-finite value
loss decreases then plateaus high LR decayed too fast, capacity too low, label noise LR at the plateau, train-set loss
training loss falls, validation rises overfitting data size, regularization, augmentation
50%+ of activations exactly zero dead ReLUs pre-activation histogram
grad norm grows with depth init scale too large, no normalization per-layer grad norms, init gain

(3) Why it is built this way. Because each is cheap to instrument and expensive to guess at. A single-batch overfit test — train on 8 examples until the loss nears zero — separates "the model cannot learn" from "the pipeline is broken" in under a minute.

(4) Worked micro-example. At initialization with a uniform $K$-way classifier, the expected loss is $\log K$: $\log 10=2.303$ for ten classes, $\log 50{,}000\approx10.82$ for a 50k-token vocabulary (M14). A first-step loss far below $\log K$ means a leak (labels in the input, or a mis-shuffled batch); far above means the logits are badly scaled or the softmax is saturating.

Hyperparameter transfer: muP

(1) Intuition. If you tune the learning rate on a small model and reuse it on a model 16× wider, it is usually wrong. The "maximal update parametrization" (muP) is a set of width-dependent scalings under which the optimal learning rate stops depending on width, so a sweep done at width 256 transfers to width 4096.

(2) Formal definition. Let $m$ be the width and $\alpha=m/m_0$ the ratio to a tuned base width. muP prescribes:

  • Init: hidden weight variance $\propto 1/\text{fan\_in}$ at every width (He/Xavier already does this), so pre-activations stay $\Theta(1)$.
  • Readout: weight the final layer by $1/m$ rather than $1/\sqrt{m}$, so each hidden unit contributes $O(1/m)$ and the network output stays $\Theta(1)$ while individual features keep $\Theta(1)$ activations.
  • Learning rates: hidden-layer LR $\propto 1/\alpha$ and readout LR $\propto 1/\alpha$, so the function update per step is width-independent; input-embedding and normalization-parameter LRs stay width-independent.
  • Attention: scale logits by $1/d_{\text{head}}$ instead of $1/\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}$ (M12).

(3) Why it is built this way. Without the rescaling, the per-unit update stays constant while the number of units grows, so the aggregate change to a pre-activation grows like $\sqrt{m}$ and the largest stable learning rate shrinks accordingly. The recipe removes that dependence. Two caveats: the constants differ slightly between implementations (the mup package is the practical reference), and the transfer is empirically supported rather than guaranteed.

(4) Worked micro-example. Tune at $m_0=256$, finding $\eta^\star=3\times10^{-3}$. For $m=4096$, $\alpha=16$, so hidden and readout LRs become $3\times10^{-3}/16\approx1.9\times10^{-4}$ while the embedding LR stays $3\times10^{-3}$. A naive transfer (same LR everywhere) would be 16× too large for the hidden layers and would likely diverge.

The algorithm, step by step

One iteration of a modern training loop, with every intervention from this module in place:

  1. Fetch the batch $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})$, shuffle order fixed by a seeded sampler.
  2. Set the learning rate $\eta_t$ from the schedule (warmup, then cosine).
  3. Forward under autocast (bf16 or fp16 matmuls, fp32 accumulation and master weights), caching pre-activations.
  4. Compute the loss, multiply by the loss scale $S$ if using fp16.
  5. Backward (M07), producing gradients scaled by $S$ in every parameter.
  6. Unscale ($\mathbf{g}\leftarrow\mathbf{g}/S$), then clip by global norm: if $\|\mathbf{g}\|>c$, multiply all gradients by $c/\|\mathbf{g}\|$.
  7. Check finiteness. Any non-finite gradient means: halve $S$, skip this step, log the event.
  8. Optimizer step (AdamW: $\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}(1-\eta_t\lambda)-\eta_t\hat{\mathbf{m}}/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}+\varepsilon)$), then zero the gradients.
  9. Update state: schedule step counter, dynamic loss scale (double $S$ every $N$ finite steps), EMA of weights if used.
  10. Every $k$ steps: evaluate with running normalization statistics and log loss, gradient norm, and dead-unit fraction.

```text for step in range(total_steps): lr = schedule(step) # warmup then cosine X, Y = next(batches) with autocast(dtype=bf16): logits = model(X) # caches pre-activations loss = cross_entropy(logits, Y) * S loss.backward() unscale_(params, S) clip_grad_norm_(params, max_norm=c) if not all_finite(params): S /= 2; skip(); continue opt.step(lr, weight_decay) # decoupled decay opt.zero_grad() if step % N == 0 and step > 0: S = min(2*S, S_max) ```

Complexity per step: $O(3\,n\sum_l d_ld_{l-1})$ FLOPs (forward plus two backward passes' worth), plus $O(|\boldsymbol{\theta}|)$ for the optimizer update, plus $O(n\sum_l d_l)$ memory for cached activations.

Derivations

1. The depth scaling law

Take $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}=\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ with iid weights of variance $g^2/d_{\text{in}}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{(l)}=\phi(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})$, with inputs zero-mean and unit variance. Then

$$ \mathrm{Var}(z_j) = d_{\text{in}}\cdot\frac{g^2}{d_{\text{in}}}\cdot\mathrm{Var}(h) = g^2\mathrm{Var}(h), $$

and by the delta method for a zero-mean pre-activation, $\mathrm{Var}(\phi(z))\approx\mathbb{E}[\phi'(z)^2]\,\mathrm{Var}(z)$. Composing the two,

$$ \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{h}^{(L)}) \approx \left(g^2\,\mathbb{E}[\phi'^2]\right)^{L}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{h}^{(0)}), \qquad \rho := g\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\phi'^2]} . $$

The backward pass multiplies the same product of Jacobians (M07), so the gradient ratio obeys the same law:

$$ \frac{\|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\|}{\|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}\|} \approx \rho^{L}. $$

Three consequences.

  • Sigmoid. $\phi'\le 1/4$, and for pre-activations of standard deviation $\sigma_z\approx1.5$ the measured effective factor is $\rho\approx0.27$ — matching the $3.8\times10^{-12}$ at $L=20$ in the table above, since $0.27^{20}=3.6\times10^{-12}$. Vanishing is not caused by depth; it is caused by $\rho<1$ raised to a large power.
  • ReLU as a criticality condition. For ReLU, $\phi'\in\{0,1\}$ and (for symmetric pre-activations) $\mathbb{E}[\phi'^2]=\tfrac12$, so $\rho=g/\sqrt{2}$ and criticality $\rho=1$ requires $g=\sqrt{2}$, i.e. $\mathrm{Var}(W)=2/d_{\text{in}}$. That is exactly He initialization (M06), now derived a second way: not from variance preservation but from the edge of chaos.
  • Why the tanh column at gain 1 is flat. $\mathbb{E}[\tanh'^2]$ falls as saturation sets in, which lowers $\rho$ below 1; but the forward variance also shrinks, which reduces saturation. The system settles near $\rho\approx1$, which is why a gain-1 tanh stack neither vanishes nor explodes and why tanh nets of 20 layers can train while sigmoid nets of 20 cannot.

2. Batch normalization, backward, in full

Forward, for a batch of $n$ examples per feature (drop the feature index):

$$ \mu=\frac1n\sum_i z_i, \quad \sigma^2=\frac1n\sum_i(z_i-\mu)^2, \quad \hat z_i=\frac{z_i-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}}, \quad y_i=\gamma\hat z_i+\beta . $$

Let $g_i=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_i$. Five steps.

Step 1, the affine parameters.

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta}=\sum_i g_i, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\gamma}=\sum_i g_i\hat z_i . $$

Step 2, into the normalized value: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\hat z_i = g_i\gamma$.

Step 3, into the variance. Define $s=\sqrt{\sigma^2+\varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}$, so $\partial\hat z_i/\partial\sigma^2 = -\tfrac12 (z_i-\mu)s^{-3}$ and

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\sigma^2} = -\frac{1}{2s^3}\sum_i (g_i\gamma)(z_i-\mu). $$

Step 4, into the mean. $\mu$ enters both through $z_i-\mu$ and through $\sigma^2$:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mu} = \sum_i (g_i\gamma)\left(-\frac1s\right) + \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{\partial\sigma^2}{\partial\mu}, $$

and $\partial\sigma^2/\partial\mu = -\frac2n\sum_i(z_i-\mu)=0$, so the second term vanishes and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mu=-\frac{\gamma}{s}\sum_i g_i$.

Step 5, into the input.

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\hat z_i}\frac1s + \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{2(z_i-\mu)}{n} + \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mu}\cdot\frac1n . $$

Substituting steps 2–4 and collecting,

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\gamma}{s}\left[g_i - \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_j g_j}_{\text{mean of }g} - \hat z_i\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_j g_j\hat z_j}_{\text{mean of }g\hat z}\right]. $$

Both correction terms are consequences of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ being functions of the whole batch: changing one $z_i$ changes the statistics for every example, so every example's gradient carries a piece of every other example's. That is the mathematical form of the batch coupling described in the concepts section, and it is why the sum of the gradients is exactly zero — the shift-invariance check from the micro-example.

Three consequences. (i) Train/eval discrepancy: at evaluation the running estimates $\hat\mu,\hat\sigma^2$ replace the batch statistics, so the evaluated function differs from the trained one; a model can be correct on its training batches and wrong on single examples if the running estimates were poorly estimated. (ii) Batch-size dependence: the batch mean has standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so with $n=2$ the "normalized" value carries $\pm70\%$ noise on the mean and the gradients are correspondingly noisy; with $n=1$ the variance is zero, so the normalized value is 0 and the layer outputs a constant $\beta$. (iii) No free normalization for sequences: BN over a batch of variable-length sequences couples examples of different lengths, which is one reason transformers use layer norm.

Layer norm and RMSNorm. Layer norm applies the same formula over the feature axis for each example: $\mu_i=\frac1d\sum_j z_{ij}$. Nothing is shared across examples, so the function is identical at train and eval and defined for any batch size. RMSNorm drops the centring: $y_j=\gamma_jz_j/\sqrt{\frac1d\sum_kz_k^2+\varepsilon_{\text{bn}}}$, saving one reduction pass; its justification is that centring is absorbed by the following bias and that the empirical difference is small, which is why it is the default in several large model families.

Why pre-LN trains stably. With $\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}=\mathbf{h}^{(l)}+\mathbf{f}_l(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{h}^{(l)}))$, the residual stream is never normalized, so $\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}/\partial\mathbf{h}^{(0)}=\prod_l(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{J}_l)$ and the identity terms survive at every depth. With post-LN, $\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}=\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{h}^{(l)}+\mathbf{f}_l(\mathbf{h}^{(l)}))$, every multiplicative factor passes through a normalization Jacobian whose scale is $1/s$; since the residual-stream variance grows with depth, $s$ grows, and the gradient is scaled down accordingly. That is the mechanism behind the empirical finding that post-LN transformers require warmup and pre-LN ones largely do not.

3. Dropout: expectation, variance, and inference equivalence

Let $m_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p)$ iid, independent of $\mathbf{h}$, and define $\tilde h_i=m_ih_i/(1-p)$. Then

$$ \mathbb{E}[\tilde h_i] = \frac{h_i}{1-p}\,\mathbb{E}[m_i] = \frac{h_i}{1-p}(1-p) = h_i, $$

so the training-time expectation equals the inference-time value $h_i$ — that is the entire justification for the $1/(1-p)$ factor, and the reason no correction is needed at test time. (The original formulation omitted the factor and multiplied by $1-p$ at test time; the two are algebraically identical, and the "inverted" form is what frameworks implement.)

The variance: $\mathrm{Var}(m_i)=p(1-p)$, so

$$ \mathrm{Var}(\tilde h_i) = \frac{h_i^2}{(1-p)^2}p(1-p) = h_i^2\frac{p}{1-p}, $$

which at $p=0.5$ equals $h_i^2$: the perturbation is as large as the signal. For a linear layer, $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}]=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ and the variance of the pre-activation is $\frac{p}{1-p}\sum_j W_{ij}^2x_j^2$, which is why dropout acts like an adaptive L2 penalty on the weights scaled by the input magnitude. The "ensemble of $2^d$ subnetworks" reading is an interpretation of this expectation, not an exact equivalence for the full nonlinear network.

4. Label smoothing: the optimum and the finite gap

Minimize $\mathcal{L}=-(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})\log p_y-\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{K}\sum_k\log p_k$ over the softmax outputs, subject to $\sum_kp_k=1$. With multiplier $\lambda$,

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial p_j}\left[-(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})\log p_y-\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{K}\sum_k\log p_k+\lambda\left(\sum_kp_k-1\right)\right]=0 . $$

For $j=y$: $-(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})/p_y+\lambda=0$, so $p_y=(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})/\lambda$. For $j\ne y$: $-\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{Kp_j}+\lambda=0$, so $p_j=\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}/(K\lambda)$. Summing all $K$ and using $\sum p_j=1$:

$$ \lambda = (1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}) + \frac{(K-1)\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{K} = 1-\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{K}, $$

hence

$$ p_y^\star=\frac{K(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})}{K-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}, \qquad p_k^\star=\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}{K-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}}, \qquad \text{logit gap} = \log\frac{p_y^\star}{p_k^\star}=\log\frac{K(1-\varepsilon_{\text{ls}})}{\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}} . $$

For $K=10$, $\varepsilon_{\text{ls}}=0.1$: $p_y^\star=0.9091$, $p_k^\star=0.0101$, gap $=\log90=4.4998$ rather than infinity. The gradient on the correct logit at the optimum is therefore bounded away from zero, which is the concrete sense in which label smoothing prevents saturation.

Implementation from scratch

A diagnostic harness rather than a trainer: it measures the input gradient norm as a function of depth, with and without a residual connection, which is the measurement you would run to diagnose a model that will not train.

```python import numpy as np def make_net(L, d, gain, rng): # iid init with variance gain^2/d, so the weight spectral norm is about `gain` return [rng.standard_normal((d, d)) * gain / np.sqrt(d) for _ in range(L)] def forward(Ws, X, residual=False): hs, zs = [X], [] for W in Ws: z = hs[-1] @ W zs.append(z) a = np.tanh(z) hs.append(hs[-1] + a if residual else a) # residual: identity plus branch return hs, zs def backward(Ws, hs, zs, residual=False): dh = hs[-1].copy() # assume loss = 0.5 * ||h_L||^2 dW_norms = [0.0] * len(Ws) for l in range(len(Ws) - 1, -1, -1): dz = dh * (1.0 - np.tanh(zs[l]) ** 2) # through the tanh dW_norms[l] = np.linalg.norm(hs[l].T @ dz) / dh.shape[0] dh = dh + dz @ Ws[l].T if residual else dz @ Ws[l].T return dW_norms, np.linalg.norm(dh) rng = np.random.default_rng(0) d, B = 64, 32 X = rng.standard_normal((B, d)) print(f"{'L':>3} {'vanilla':>11} {'residual':>11}") for L in (4, 8, 12, 16, 20): Ws = make_net(L, d, 1.0, rng) hs, zs = forward(Ws, X) g_van = backward(Ws, hs, zs)[1] Ws = make_net(L, d, 0.2, rng) hs, zs = forward(Ws, X, residual=True) g_res = backward(Ws, hs, zs, residual=True)[1] print(f"{L:3d} {g_van:11.3e} {g_res:11.3e}") Ws = make_net(20, d, 0.5, rng) hs, zs = forward(Ws, X) norms, g0 = backward(Ws, hs, zs) print("subcritical L=20, |dW| every 4th layer:", " ".join(f"{n:.2e}" for n in norms[::4])) print(f"input gradient norm {g0:.2e}") ```

Common misconceptions

"Batch norm works because it keeps activations at unit variance."

Keeping the variance fixed is a precondition, not the mechanism. The mechanism is that the normalization makes each layer's input distribution independent of the previous layer's scale, so no layer has to adapt to another layer's drift, and the effective per-layer gain stops being a product of unknown factors. Two observations support the distinction: replacing BN with a fixed non-learned normalization of the same variance loses much of the benefit, and BN networks still train when the normalization is done approximately, as long as the scale decoupling holds.

"Dropout has to be rescaled at test time."

Only in the original formulation. Inverted dropout divides by $1-p$ during training, which makes the training-time expectation equal the test-time value, so the inference path is the identity. If you scale at test time and use inverted dropout, you shrink every activation by $1-p$ and quietly degrade the model.

"An overparameterized model must overfit."

A model large enough to memorize is also a model for which many interpolating solutions exist; which one gradient descent finds is decided by the optimizer's implicit bias, not by the parameter count. Networks with far more parameters than data points routinely reach zero training error while generalizing, and the same networks fit random labels perfectly — the capacity argument cannot tell the two cases apart.

"Networks generalize because they find flat minima."

Flatness in weight space is not invariant to reparametrization, so a minimum can be made arbitrarily sharp or flat without changing the function at all. Flat-minimum explanations correlate with generalization in some settings and fail in others. Treat it as one hypothesis among several, alongside implicit bias, compression, and the averaging effect of stochastic training.

"Mixed precision is about overflow."

It is about underflow. The failure mode that quietly destroys training is a gradient smaller than the smallest normal fp16 number ($6.1\times10^{-5}$) flushing to zero, which is why gradients are scaled up by $S$ before the backward pass and only then unscaled. Overflow is handled crudely and visibly (halve $S$); underflow is silent, which is what makes it dangerous.
