The Modern Architecture Zoo

What survived the transformer, what replaced it, and which claims are actually measured.

on this page

Why this module exists

The transformer of M12 is not what frontier models run. Between the 2017 block and a 2024 open-weight model, four things changed: the key/value cache was compressed (MQA, GQA, MLA), the attention pattern was restricted or re-derived (sliding windows, sparse patterns, linear attention, state-space models), the feed-forward network was sparsified (mixture-of-experts), and the training block was rebuilt around gated linear units and pre-normalization. Every one of those changes traces back to a specific line in the cost model you derived in M12 — usually the KV-cache term, sometimes the quadratic score matrix, occasionally just the parameter count. The reason to study this zoo carefully rather than as a list of acronyms is that it is where the field's claims live. "Linear attention is as good as softmax attention" is a strong empirical claim that has been true on some benchmarks and false at frontier scale. "MoE gives you 4× the parameters for free" is arithmetic, and the cost it hides is memory. "State-space models replace attention" is a hypothesis with real evidence behind it and no frontier-scale demonstration. The last section of this module explicitly separates what has been measured from what is speculation, and that habit is worth more than any single architecture in it.

Core concepts

The KV cache is the constraint everything else is fighting

Intuition. At inference the model recomputes nothing about the past — it stores every token's keys and values and appends to them. That store is the memory bill of a deployed model, and it grows with context, batch size, and layer count simultaneously.

Formal definition. From M12, with $L$ layers, $n_{kv}$ key/value heads, head dimension $d_h$, context $T$, and $b$ bytes per element:

$$ \text{cache} = 2\,L\,n_{kv}\,d_h\,T\,b . $$

For a LLaMA-2-7B-shaped model ($L=32$, $h=32$, $d_h=128$, fp16) this is $512$ KiB per token, $2.0$ GiB at a 4096-token context, and $64$ GiB at batch 32 — against 14 GB of weights. The weights are a fixed cost; the cache is a cost you pay per concurrent user.

Why it is built this way. There is no approximation in the cache: it stores exactly $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ for every processed token so that each new token costs one query against $T$ cached keys, $O(Td)$ instead of $O(T^2d)$. The consequence is that serving throughput is bound by cache bytes per token, not by FLOPs. Every architectural change in the next three sections is either an attack on n_kv, an attack on T, or an attack on the existence of the cache at all.

Worked micro-example. The same model with three cache layouts, at a 4096-token context and fp16:

Layout $n_{kv}$ Bytes/token Cache @ 4096 Max batch in 60 GB
MHA $32$ $512$ KiB $2.0$ GiB $30$
GQA-8 $8$ $128$ KiB $0.50$ GiB $120$
MQA $1$ $16$ KiB $64$ MiB $960$

The last column is the whole motivation. A model that can serve 30 sequences at 4096 tokens is a demo; one that can serve 120 is a product.

Drag the sequence length, head count, and KV-group count and watch the cache bar overtake the weights bar. Watch the ratio readout: at 32 KV heads and 8K context the cache is larger than the model for any batch above one — and dropping to 8 KV groups divides it by exactly four, which is the GQA trade in one drag.

MQA and GQA: sharing keys and values across heads

Intuition. Multi-head attention gives every head its own queries, keys, and values. But the queries are what make heads different — each head asks a different question. The keys and values are the data being read, and there is no strong reason each head needs a private copy of the data. Share one copy among all heads and the cache shrinks by the sharing factor.

Formal definition. Let $n_{kv}$ be the number of distinct key/value heads, with $1 \le n_{kv}\le h$ and $h$ a multiple of $n_{kv}$.

  • MHA ($n_{kv} = h$): each head has its own $\mathbf{W}_K^{(i)},\mathbf{W}_V^{(i)}$.
  • MQA ($n_{kv}=1$): all $h$ heads share a single $\mathbf{W}_K,\mathbf{W}_V$; each keeps its own $\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)}$.
  • GQA ($1 < n_{kv} < h$): heads are partitioned into $n_{kv}$ groups of $h/n_{kv}$; heads within a group share their key/value projections.

Query heads $h$ stay at $h$ in every case, so the score matrices are still $h$ separate $n\times n$ objects. Only $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are shared, so the cache becomes $2Ln_{kv}d_hTb$ and the reduction factor is $h/n_{kv}$.

Why it is built this way. The trade is asymmetric in exactly the way you want. Parameters for $\mathbf{W}_K,\mathbf{W}_V$ drop from $2d^2$ to $2d^2 h/n_{kv}$ (small: those are 2 of the 12 $d^2$ blocks in a layer), the compute barely changes (the score matrices still dominate), and the cache drops by $h/n_{kv}$ — a factor that directly multiplies serving capacity. Reported quality effects are small: Shazeer's MQA paper found it matches MHA on quality with a large inference speedup, and the GQA paper showed that a small number of groups (typically 8) recovers essentially all of MHA's quality while keeping most of MQA's memory advantage. Converting an existing MHA checkpoint to GQA requires uptraining (mean-pooling the K/V projections within each group is the standard initialization), which is cheap.

The failure mode of MQA is not accuracy but expressiveness of the key space: with one shared key head, all $h$ heads must agree on what makes a token findable. Empirically $n_{kv}=1$ loses a little quality; $n_{kv}=8$ loses almost none. That is why $n_{kv}$ is a number you will find in every modern config file, usually written num_key_value_heads.

Worked micro-example. $L=32$, $h=32$, $d_h=128$, $T=8192$, fp16.

Layout $n_{kv}$ bytes/token cache @ 8192 K/V parameters
MHA $32$ $512$ KiB $4.0$ GiB $2\cdot4096\cdot4096 = 33.6$M
GQA-8 $8$ $128$ KiB $1.0$ GiB $8.4$M
MQA $1$ $16$ KiB $128$ MiB $1.0$M

Sliding-window and sparse attention

Intuition. Most of what a token needs is nearby. Cap each query's attention to the last $w$ tokens and the cost becomes linear in $n$ — at the price of never being able to look further back than $w$ directly.

Formal definition. Sliding-window attention restricts the allowed set to $j \in [i-w+1, i]$ (unidirectional) or $|i-j|\le w/2$ (bidirectional):

$$ \mathbf{A}_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j\Big(\mathbf{s}_{ij} + \mathbf{M}_{ij}\Big),\qquad \mathbf{M}_{ij} = 0 \text{ if } |i-j| < w,\; -\infty\text{ otherwise}. $$

Cost per layer: $O(nwd)$ instead of $O(n^2d)$, and cache $O(wd)$ per layer with a rolling buffer. The composed receptive field over $L$ layers is

$$ r_L = 1 + L\,(w-1), $$

linear in depth, so a 32-layer model with $w=4096$ has a theoretical receptive field of 131k tokens even though no single layer sees more than 4096.

Why it is built this way. It encodes a real prior (locality) at a real cost saving, and it composes: stacked windowed layers give a receptive field that grows linearly with depth, exactly like a stack of convolutions. The known weakness is exact recall at distance: if token 1 must be copied at token 50,000, no window reaches it, and the information must be carried through the residual stream — which loses precision. The standard remedy is hybridization: most layers windowed, a few fully global (Longformer's design), or periodic global tokens (BigBird).

Sparse patterns generalize the idea: each query attends to a fixed-size set $\mathcal{N}(i)$ of keys defined by a pattern — strided (attend every $k$-th token, as in Child et al.'s Sparse Transformer), windowed, random, or block-sparse. Any pattern with $|\mathcal{N}(i)| = O(w)$ costs $O(nwd)$ and has a receptive field that grows with depth. The tradeoff is pattern design: a deterministic stride pattern can miss the token that matters, and random patterns need enough layers for the union of reachable sets to cover the sequence with high probability.

Worked micro-example. $n=32768$, $d=4096$, $w=4096$: full attention core $2n^2d = 8.8\times10^{12}$ MACs per layer; windowed $2nwd = 1.1\times10^{12}$ — an $8\times$ saving, because $n/w = 8$. The saving is exactly $n/w$, so windows only pay off when the context greatly exceeds the window; at $n = 2w$ you save $2\times$ and lose exact long-range access, which is rarely worth it.

Linear attention: the kernel view and the associative reordering

Intuition. Softmax attention compares each query to each key and normalizes across all of them — the normalization is what makes it non-decomposable. Drop the softmax normalization, replace $\exp(\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}\rangle)$ by a feature map $\phi(\mathbf{q})^\top\phi(\mathbf{k})$, and the sum over keys becomes factorizable. Then you can swap the order of summation: sum over keys first, once, and reuse that summary for every query. That is the whole idea, and it turns $O(n^2)$ into $O(n)$.

Formal definition. Standard attention, for one query:

$$ \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\sum_{s\le t}\exp\big(\langle\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s\rangle\big)\,\mathbf{v}_s}{\sum_{s\le t}\exp\big(\langle\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s\rangle\big)} . $$

Replace the exponential kernel with a feature map: $\exp(\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}\rangle)\approx\phi(\mathbf{q})^\top\phi(\mathbf{k})$ with $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_k}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi}$ applied elementwise and nonnegative (for $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{elu}(\mathbf{x})+1$ the approximation is exact for the "linear" kernel $\phi(\mathbf{q})^\top\phi(\mathbf{k}) = 1 + \langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}\rangle_+$). Then

$$ \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\sum_{s\le t}\big(\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\big)\mathbf{v}_s}{\sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)} = \frac{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\Big(\sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\mathbf{v}_s^\top\Big)}{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\Big(\sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\Big)} . $$

The inner product no longer depends on $s$ once it is pulled outside the sum — that is the reordering, and it is the entire derivation (done in full in Derivation 1). Define the running state

$$ \mathbf{S}_t = \sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\mathbf{v}_s^\top \in\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi\times d_v},\qquad \mathbf{z}_t = \sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi}, $$

which update as $\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} + \phi(\mathbf{k}_t)\mathbf{v}_t^\top$ and $\mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{z}_{t-1}+\phi(\mathbf{k}_t)$, and the output is

$$ \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{S}_t}{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{z}_t} . $$

Cost per token: $O(d_\phi d_v)$ — linear in $d$, independent of $n$. Total $O(nd_\phi d_v)$ against $O(n^2d)$, and memory $O(d_\phi d_v)$ for the state instead of $O(nd)$ for a cache.

Why it is built this way. Three properties are gained and one is lost, and it is worth being precise about which. Gained: (i) linear time and constant memory per token; (ii) a recurrent inference form with a fixed-size state, so no cache growth; (iii) parallel training via a chunked or scan formulation, so it does not inherit the RNN's serialization (M11). Lost: the softmax's normalization means attention weights are no longer guaranteed nonnegative-and-summing-to-one over an unbounded set, so the output is a ratio of two nonnegative quantities rather than a convex combination — this is a real expressiveness restriction (the "low-rank" or "spikiness" problem), and it is the honest reason linear attention has not matched softmax attention at frontier scale. Approximating $\exp$ by a finite feature map also introduces approximation error that grows with the sharpness of the true attention distribution — precisely the regime that long-context retrieval needs.

Worked micro-example. $n=8192$, $d=1024$, $d_\phi = d_v = d$: quadratic core $4n^2d = 2.75\times10^{11}$ FLOPs; linear $4nd^2 = 3.4\times10^{10}$ — an $8\times$ saving, exactly $n/(2d)$. Since the crossover is at $n=2d=2048$, the benefit is a factor $n/2048$: at 128k context it is $64\times$.

RWKV: linear attention with a decay, trained in parallel

Intuition. Take linear attention and make the decay from past to present explicit and per-channel, so that the state's contribution decays geometrically with distance. Then the state update is a scan — associative, so it parallelizes over the sequence during training, while inference remains a fixed-size recurrence.

Formal definition (RWKV-4 time mixing). With per-channel decay vector $\mathbf{w}$ (learned, and the same for all positions):

$$ \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\sum_{s\le t}e^{\mathbf{k}_s + \mathbf{w}\odot(t-s)}\odot\mathbf{v}_s}{\sum_{s\le t}e^{\mathbf{k}_s + \mathbf{w}\odot(t-s)}} . $$

The numerator is a running sum where each step multiplies the accumulator by $e^{\mathbf{w}}$ (a value $<1$ per channel when trained to decay) and adds $e^{\mathbf{k}_t}\mathbf{v}_t$:

$$ \mathbf{S}_t = e^{\mathbf{w}}\odot\mathbf{S}_{t-1} + e^{\mathbf{k}_t}\mathbf{v}_t . $$

Why it is built this way. The fixed geometric decay (a) makes the state's influence vanish for distant tokens, which bounds the effective context and therefore bounds gradient variance, and (b) makes the recurrence a linear recursion, so the whole sequence can be computed with a parallel scan or as a cumulative sum in the log domain — no sequential dependency during training. At inference the state is $O(d)$ per channel, so there is no cache at all. The result is the RNN's inference profile with the transformer's training profile. The honest caveat: the decay is not content-dependent in RWKV-4 (later versions add data-dependent gating), so it cannot represent "remember this particular token forever, forget that one" as sharply as attention can — the same expressiveness restriction as linear attention, in a different costume.

Worked micro-example. One channel, decay $w = -0.693$ per step (so $e^w = 0.5$): after 10 steps a contribution is attenuated by $0.5^{10} = 9.8\times10^{-4}$, after 20 by $9.5\times10^{-7}$. The effective context of that channel is about 20 steps — which is exactly the property that makes training stable, and exactly the property that makes long-range exact recall impossible. Per-channel decay vectors let different channels have different horizons, which is how RWKV-7B-scale models cover thousands of tokens: some channels decay at $0.9999$ per step.

State-space models: from a continuous ODE to a recurrence you can implement

Intuition. Start with a linear, time-invariant ODE describing a physical system — a damped oscillator, a leaky integrator, a filter. Sample it at intervals $\Delta$. You get a linear recurrence, which is a convolution, which can be computed in $O(n\log n)$ by FFT during training and in $O(1)$ per token at inference. This is the classical signal-processing route to sequence modelling, and it predates the transformer by decades.

Formal definition. The continuous system, for a scalar input $u(t)$ and output $y(t)$, with state $\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^N$:

$$ \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}u(t),\qquad y(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D}u(t). $$

Integrating over one step of length $\Delta$ with $u$ held constant (zero-order hold):

$$ \mathbf{x}_{k} = \bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \bar{\mathbf{B}}u_k,\qquad \bar{\mathbf{A}} = e^{\Delta\mathbf{A}},\quad \bar{\mathbf{B}} = \Big(\int_0^{\Delta}e^{s\mathbf{A}}\,ds\Big)\mathbf{B}. $$

For diagonal $\mathbf{A}$ (the case every efficient implementation uses) both are elementwise:

$$ \bar{A}_n = e^{\Delta A_n},\qquad \bar{B}_n = \frac{e^{\Delta A_n}-1}{A_n}B_n . $$

Unrolling gives a convolution:

$$ y_k = \sum_{s\le k}\underbrace{\mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\,k-s}\bar{\mathbf{B}}}_{\bar{K}_{k-s}}u_s + Du_k . $$

Why it is built this way. Because a linear time-invariant recurrence is a convolution, it can be computed three ways: sequentially in $O(n)$ (inference), as an explicit convolution in $O(n^2)$ (naive), or by FFT in $O(n\log n)$ (training over the whole sequence at once). The FFT route is what makes SSMs competitive with attention on training throughput, and the sequential route is what makes inference $O(1)$ per token. The design problem that S4 solved is what to choose for $\mathbf{A}$: a random $\mathbf{A}$ forgets the past exponentially and cannot remember long-range structure, so Gu et al. used a specific structured matrix (HiPPO) whose state is provably an optimal polynomial projection of the input history — the eigenvalues are chosen so that the recurrence integrates Legendre polynomials over a sliding window, rather than decaying arbitrarily.

Worked micro-example. $N=1$, $A = -1$, $\Delta = 0.1$: $\bar{A} = e^{-0.1} = 0.9048$, $\bar{B} = (e^{-0.1}-1)/(-1)\cdot B = 0.0952B$. So each step keeps $90.5\%$ of the state and writes $9.5\%$ of the input — a leaky integrator with a time constant of 10 steps. With $A = -0.01$ and $\Delta = 1$: $\bar{A} = 0.990$, a 100-step time constant. That is the entire mechanism of state-space memory: the spectrum of $\mathbf{A}$ plus $\Delta$ sets the horizon, and the reason Mamba adds input-dependence to $\Delta$ is so the model can choose the horizon per token.

S4: structured state spaces, and why the initialization is the contribution

Intuition. Any linear recurrence forgets at a rate set by the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$. Choose them badly (at random) and the state remembers only the last few steps. S4's contribution is a principled choice of $\mathbf{A}$: a fixed structured matrix whose state is mathematically an optimal polynomial approximation of the input's history, so the recurrence behaves like a well-designed filter bank rather than a random one.

Formal definition. S4 (Gu, Goel and Ré, 2021) fixes $\mathbf{A}$ to a HiPPO matrix, the best-known instance being HiPPO-LegS:

$$ A_{nk} = \begin{cases}-\sqrt{(2n+1)(2k+1)} & n > k\\ -(n+1) & n = k\\ 0 & n < k\end{cases} $$

and then parameterizes it, for trainability, as $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}_{\text{HiPPO}} - \mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^\top$ (a diagonal-plus-low-rank form, "DPLR") with $\mathbf{P}$ learned. Training computes the convolution kernel $\bar{K}_t = \mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^t\bar{\mathbf{B}}$ by a recurrence on the DPLR structure in $O(N\log n)$ time rather than $O(N^2)$ per step, then convolves with the input by FFT.

Why it is built this way. Two problems, two answers. (i) A random $\mathbf{A}$ cannot remember. The HiPPO derivation says: define an optimality criterion — the state should be the coefficients of the best $N$-term Legendre expansion of the history $u(0..t)$ — and solve for the $\mathbf{A}$ that makes the linear ODE do exactly that. The resulting spectrum has eigenvalues clustered so that different coordinates track different time scales, giving a memory that decays polynomially rather than exponentially. (ii) A dense $\mathbf{A}$ is too expensive. Naively computing $\bar{\mathbf{A}}^t$ is $O(N^2)$ per step; the DPLR structure plus the Cauchy-kernel trick reduces both training and the kernel computation to near-linear time.

Worked micro-example. The HiPPO-LegS matrix at $N=2$: $\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0\\ -\sqrt3 & -2\end{pmatrix}$ (from the formula: $A_{00}=-1$, $A_{10}=-\sqrt{(2\cdot1+1)(2\cdot0+1)}=-\sqrt3$, $A_{11}=-2$, $A_{01}=0$). Its eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-2$, i.e. two time constants in a 1:2 ratio; at $N=64$ the eigenvalues spread over a much wider range, so the state simultaneously holds fast and slow averages of the input. That spectrum — not the dimension $N$ alone — is what gives S4 its long-range memory.

Mamba: making the system selective

Intuition. An LTI system applies the same filter to every input. It cannot decide that this token is worth remembering for a thousand steps and that one for two. Attention can, because its weights depend on content. Mamba's move is to make the SSM's parameters functions of the input, so the recurrence becomes a gated linear recurrence with content-dependent timescales.

Formal definition (selective SSM). Keep $\mathbf{A}$ fixed and diagonal, and make the step size and input/output matrices functions of the current input:

$$ \Delta_t = \operatorname{softplus}\big(\mathbf{W}_\Delta\mathbf{u}_t\big),\qquad \mathbf{B}_t = \mathbf{W}_B\mathbf{u}_t,\qquad \mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{W}_C\mathbf{u}_t, $$ $$ \mathbf{x}_t = \bar{\mathbf{A}}_t\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \bar{\mathbf{B}}_t u_t,\qquad \bar{\mathbf{A}}_t = e^{\Delta_t\mathbf{A}},\quad \bar{\mathbf{B}}_t = \Delta_t\mathbf{B}_t, $$ $$ y_t = \mathbf{C}_t^\top\mathbf{x}_t + Du_t . $$

The $\Delta_t\mathbf{B}_t$ simplification (rather than the exact ZOH expression) is the standard choice; for scalar $A$ they differ by an $O(\Delta)$ factor absorbed into a learned projection.

Why it is built this way — and what selectivity means mechanically. Look at $\bar{\mathbf{A}}_t = e^{\Delta_t A}$ with $A < 0$. Small $\Delta_t$ gives $\bar{\mathbf{A}}_t\approx1$: the state is preserved, and the model can hold information across many steps. Large $\Delta_t$ gives $\bar{\mathbf{A}}_t\approx0$: the state is cleared, and the model resets to the current input. So $\Delta_t$ is precisely a learned forget gate — structurally the LSTM's $\mathbf{f}_t = e^{-\Delta a}$ (M11), with the gate value produced by a linear map of the input instead of a sigmoid. Selectivity is therefore not a mysterious new capability; it is the LSTM's gating, made parallel-trainable by the fact that the state transition is diagonal and scalar-times-identity rather than a full matrix.

The cost is that the recurrence is now time-varying, so it is no longer a single convolution and the FFT trick is unavailable. Mamba's answer is a hardware-aware scan: rather than materializing the $(T, d, N)$ intermediate states in HBM, the kernel fuses the recurrence and only writes the final output and final state; the backward pass recomputes the states rather than storing them. Complexity is $O(ndN)$ time and $O(dN)$ memory for the scan, against attention's $O(n^2d)$ and $O(nd)$ cache.

Worked micro-example. One channel, $A = -1$. If $\Delta_t = 0.01$ then $\bar{A}_t = e^{-0.01} = 0.990$: the state decays with a 100-step time constant, so this token is remembered. If $\Delta_t = 5$, then $\bar{A}_t = e^{-5} = 0.0067$: the state is wiped and replaced by $5B_tu_t$, so the model treats this token as a reset point. With $\mathbf{A}$ learned per channel, a single Mamba layer contains a bank of integrators whose time constants are re-selected at every step from the input — the closest thing in modern architectures to a content-addressed leaky integrator.

Hybrid stacks

Intuition. Attention is expensive per token but exact at recall; a selective SSM is cheap per token and excellent at aggregation but lossy at exact recall. Interleave them: a majority of cheap SSM layers doing the bulk processing, a minority of attention layers providing exact lookup.

Formal definition. A hybrid stack partitions the $L$ layers into $L_{\text{attn}}$ attention layers and $L_{\text{ssm}}$ recurrence layers, typically in a fixed periodic ratio (one attention layer every $k$ SSM layers) or with a single global-attention block placed periodically. Inference cost per token becomes $O\big((L_{\text{ssm}}dN + L_{\text{attn}}n d)\big)$ with a cache only for the attention layers, i.e. $2L_{\text{attn}}n_{kv}d_hTb$ bytes.

Why it is built this way. Because the two failure modes are complementary and the cost asymmetry is large. Published hybrids of this kind — Jamba's interleaving of Mamba and attention layers inside a mixture-of-experts model at roughly one attention layer per seven Mamba layers, and Griffin's gated-linear-recurrence-plus-local-attention design at a fixed recurrence-to-attention ratio — report that a small fraction of attention layers recovers most of the quality of a pure transformer at a fraction of the cache. The mechanism is consistent with the theory: exact copying is what the attention layers are for, and a few of them, positioned to be reachable from every token, suffice.

The honest framing: hybrids are a pragmatic compromise, not a demonstration that attention is replaceable. No frontier-scale model has shipped with zero attention layers and matched a pure-transformer baseline at equal compute, data and tuning effort. See the last section for how to read claims to the contrary.

Mixture of experts: sparsity in the parameter dimension

Intuition. A dense FFN applies all of its parameters to every token. But a given token needs only a few kinds of computation. Split the FFN into $E$ parallel "experts" and route each token to the top $k$: every token is still cheap, but the model has $E$ times more parameters to store knowledge in.

Formal definition. With router weights $\mathbf{W}_r\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times d}$ and $E$ expert FFNs $E_i$ (each a SwiGLU MLP):

$$ \mathbf{s} = \operatorname{softmax}\big(\mathbf{W}_r\mathbf{x}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{E},\qquad \mathcal{T} = \operatorname{TopK}(\mathbf{s},k),\qquad \mathbf{y} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}} g_i\,E_i(\mathbf{x}),\quad g_i = \frac{s_i}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}}s_j}\ \text{(Switch: } g_i = s_i\text{)} . $$

Capacity. To keep the computation a dense batched GEMM, each expert is allotted a fixed buffer of

$$ C = \operatorname{round}\Big(\text{capacity\_factor}\cdot\frac{T}{E}\Big) $$

token slots (capacity factor typically $1.0$–$1.25$). Tokens whose expert is full are dropped from that expert — they pass through the residual connection only. The dropped fraction is the price of static shapes; it is reported and tracked because a high drop rate means most tokens are receiving a partial model.

The load-balancing loss. A pure top-$k$ router collapses: the winning expert gets more gradient, becomes better, and wins more. Switch Transformer's auxiliary loss counteracts this. With $f_i$ the fraction of tokens whose top-1 choice is expert $i$, and $P_i$ the mean router probability for expert $i$,

$$ L_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\,E\sum_{i=1}^{E}f_iP_i . $$

At perfectly uniform routing, $f_i = P_i = 1/E$ for all $i$, so $L_{\text{aux}} = \alpha E\cdot E\cdot\frac{1}{E^2} = \alpha$: the minimum value is exactly $\alpha$, and it is scale-free in $E$. Note what this loss actually measures: if $P$ is uniform but $f$ is skewed, $\sum_if_iP_i = \frac1E\sum_if_i = \frac1E$ and the loss does not increase — because the soft probabilities are not the problem. The loss grows only when $f$ and $P$ are aligned, i.e. when the router is confidently and consistently sending everything to the same experts. Measured on a toy: uniform gives $0.0100$ at $\alpha=0.01$, a skewed $f=(0.5,0.2,0.1,\ldots)$ with uniform $P$ still gives $0.0100$, and a skewed $f$ with matching skewed $P$ gives $0.0247$. That is the precise sense in which this loss penalizes collapse rather than imbalance.

Why it is built this way — the arithmetic of the trade. Take a Mixtral-8×7B-shaped layer: $d=4096$, $d_{\text{ff}}=14336$, $E=8$, $k=2$. Per expert (SwiGLU, three matrices): $3\cdot4096\cdot14336 = 176.2$M parameters. Stored: $8\times176.2 = 1.41$B per layer. Active per token: $2\times176.2 = 352.3$M.

A dense FFN with the same per-token compute would need $d_{\text{ff}} = 2\times14336 = 28672$, i.e. $352.3$M parameters — identical active count, by construction, since equal active parameters means equal FLOPs. So the comparison is:

Parameters stored Parameters per token
Dense, equal FLOPs $352$M $352$M
MoE, $E=8$, $k=2$ $1.41$B ($4\times$) $352$M

The model gets 4× the parameters for the same per-token compute — and pays for it in memory: all 1.41B must be resident, so at batch 1 you are using $1/4$ of the parameters you have paid to store. The whole model at 32 layers is 45.1B stored and 13.4B active, against 46.7B / 12.9B reported for Mixtral — the small discrepancy is biases, norms and the embedding. Serving requires ~94 GB in bf16 (two 80 GB GPUs) to run at the speed of a 13B dense model. That is the MoE bargain stated precisely: FLOPs decouple from memory, and you are trading memory for quality. It is a good trade only when you have memory to spare and compute is the binding constraint — which is the situation for training, and often not the situation for serving.

What frontier models have converged on, measured versus speculative

What is measured (published configs, reproducible ablations, multiple independent labs):

  • Decoder-only, causal, pre-LN. RMSNorm rather than LayerNorm, placed inside the branch, with a final norm before the unembedding. This is essentially universal.
  • SwiGLU with $d_{\text{ff}}\approx\frac83 d$. The parameter-neutral choice derived in M12; the "huge FFN" of GPT-3's 4× has been superseded by the gated 8/3 form.
  • RoPE, with a base frequency raised from $10^4$ to $10^5$–$10^6$ for long context, sometimes combined with position interpolation or YaRN-style rescaling.
  • GQA with $n_{kv}\approx8$. Every current open-weight model family uses it; the KV-cache arithmetic is not optional at scale.
  • No biases in the linear layers, and often tied embeddings.
  • FlashAttention-class attention kernels — universally, since they are exact and faster.
  • MoE at the top end. Mixtral, DeepSeek-V2/V3, DBRX, Qwen-MoE and Grok all use sparse FFNs; the largest dense open models are a generation behind.
  • bf16 (and, in at least one reported case, fp8) training, with fp16 rarely used for large runs.

What is contested:

  • Whether attention can be removed. Hybrids are shipping; pure-SSM frontier models are not. The claim "SSMs replace attention" is a hypothesis with academic support and no frontier-scale demonstration at equal tuning.
  • Whether MoE is better. Comparisons at equal active parameters are expensive and rare, and MoE is harder to train (routing instability, load-balance tuning, expert collapse) and memory-hungry. The fair claim is "MoE is better per FLOP, if you have the memory".
  • Sparsity-induced emergence. Claims that sparse models acquire capabilities dense models do not are not well separated from the confound that sparse models are trained with more total parameters and often more data.
  • Long-context capability. "128k context" describes a window, not a capability; retrieval accuracy at 128k varies enormously by benchmark and by position within the window, and the positional scheme is only one of several determinants.
  • Latent attention (MLA). DeepSeek-V2 reports a 93.3% KV-cache reduction against MHA with quality maintained, and it is the most convincing published attack on the cache term — but it is one lab's result, and independent replication at scale is thin.

The algorithm, step by step

A Mamba block, then a mixture-of-experts FFN. Both are written at variable level, because these are the two parts of the modern zoo you are most likely to have to implement.

Mamba block, input $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times d}$, expansion factor $e$ (typically 2), inner width $d_{\text{in}}=ed$, state size $N$ (typically 16).

  1. Split into two branches. $\mathbf{X}_{\text{gate}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_1^\top$ and $\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_2^\top$, both $\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times d_{\text{in}}}$. One branch becomes the gate, the other the SSM input — the same gating structure as SwiGLU.
  2. Causal depthwise convolution. $\mathbf{U} \leftarrow \operatorname{conv1d}_{\text{depthwise}}(\mathbf{U}, \text{kernel}=4)$ then a nonlinearity. The short convolution gives each position a 4-token local context that the scan alone would not provide; it is causal, so it uses only past tokens.
  3. Project the SSM parameters. $\Delta = \operatorname{softplus}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{W}_\Delta^\top + \mathbf{b}_\Delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times d_{\text{in}}}$, $\mathbf{B}_t = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{W}_B^\top \in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times N}$, $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{W}_C^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times N}$. Note these are per token: that is the selectivity.
  4. Discretize. $\bar{\mathbf{A}}_t = \exp(\Delta_t\otimes\mathbf{A})$ with $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times N}$ fixed and structured (typically $\mathbf{A} = -\exp(\mathbf{A}_{\log})$, negative, diagonal per channel); $\bar{\mathbf{B}}_t = \Delta_t\otimes\mathbf{B}_t$.
  5. Scan. For $t = 1..T$: $\mathbf{h}_t = \bar{\mathbf{A}}_t\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \bar{\mathbf{B}}_t\odot\mathbf{u}_t$ (shape $B\times d_{\text{in}}\times N$), then $y_t = \langle\mathbf{C}_t,\mathbf{h}_t\rangle$ (contracting $N$).
  6. Gate and project. $\mathbf{Y} = \big(\mathbf{Y}\odot\operatorname{SiLU}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{gate}})\big)\mathbf{W}_3^\top$, back to $\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times d}$.
  7. Residual. $\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Y}$ (preceded by a norm, as in every modern block).

```text function mamba_block(X, W1, W2, Wconv, Wdelta, WB, WC, Alog, W3): G = X @ W1.T # (B, T, di) gate branch U = X @ W2.T # (B, T, di) ssm branch U = silu(causal_depthwise_conv1d(U, Wconv, k=4)) dt = softplus(U @ Wdelta.T + b_delta) # (B, T, di) per-token step size Bt = U @ WB.T # (B, T, N) Ct = U @ WC.T # (B, T, N) A = -exp(Alog) # (di, N) negative, so exp(dt*A) in (0,1) h = zeros(B, di, N) # the entire state: no (T, N) tensor for t in range(T): Abar = exp(dt[:, t, :, None] * A) # (B, di, N) <-- the forget gate Bbar = dt[:, t, :, None] * Bt[:, t, None, :] h = Abar * h + Bbar * U[:, t, :, None] Y[:, t] = einsum('bdn,bn->bd', h, Ct[:, t]) return X + (Y * silu(G)) @ W3.T ```

Complexity. The scan is $O(BTd_{\text{in}}N)$ multiply-accumulates — linear in sequence length, with $N=16$ so the constant is small. Memory is $O(Bd_{\text{in}}N)$ for the state plus $O(BTd_{\text{in}}3N)$ for the per-token $\bar{\mathbf{A}},\bar{\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C}$ if materialized; the hardware-aware kernel avoids that by recomputing them in the backward pass, exactly as FlashAttention recomputes attention tiles. Compare attention: $O(BT^2d)$ time and $O(BTn_{kv}d_h)$ cache.

Mixture-of-experts FFN, input $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}$, $E$ experts, top-$k$, capacity factor $\gamma$.

  1. Route. $\mathbf{s} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_r^\top)\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times E}$; take the top $k$ indices per token and renormalize their probabilities to get gates $g$.
  2. Bound the buffers. $C = \lceil \gamma T k/E\rceil$ slots per expert. Compute, for each expert, the list of tokens routed to it (the "permutation"), truncating at $C$; the excess tokens are dropped.
  3. Dispatch. Send each token's hidden state to its assigned experts (a gather, or an all-to-all across devices in expert parallelism).
  4. Compute. Each expert runs its own SwiGLU on its assigned tokens — one dense GEMM per expert, which is what makes this efficient despite the sparsity.
  5. Combine. Multiply each expert's output by its gate $g_i$ and scatter-add back to the token's position; dropped tokens contribute nothing (they pass through the residual).
  6. Auxiliary loss. Add $\alpha E\sum_i f_iP_i$ to the training objective, with $f$ computed from the routing decisions and $P$ from the soft router probabilities.

Complexity. Compute per token is $O(k\cdot 3dd_{\text{ff}})$ — independent of $E$. Memory is $O(E\cdot 3dd_{\text{ff}})$ for the experts — linear in $E$. Communication in expert parallelism is $O(Td)$ per all-to-all, twice (dispatch and combine).

Derivations

1. Linear attention: the associative reordering, done completely

Step 1 — start from the definition. For one query at position $t$, with a similarity function $\operatorname{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)$:

$$ \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\sum_{s\le t}\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s)\,\mathbf{v}_s}{\sum_{s\le t}\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s)} . $$

Step 2 — choose a factorizable similarity. Let $\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}) = \phi(\mathbf{q})^\top\phi(\mathbf{k})$ for some feature map $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_k}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi}$ with nonnegative outputs. (For $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{elu}(\mathbf{x})+1$ this gives $1+\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}\rangle$ when $\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}\rangle\ge0$, an exact "linear" kernel; for the softmax kernel one would need an infinite-dimensional $\phi$, which is why linear attention is an approximation.)

Step 3 — substitute and expand. The numerator becomes

$$ \sum_{s\le t}\big(\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\big)\mathbf{v}_s = \sum_{s\le t}\sum_{r=1}^{d_\phi}\phi_r(\mathbf{q}_t)\,\phi_r(\mathbf{k}_s)\,\mathbf{v}_s . $$

Each term is a scalar times a vector. Sum over $s$ first:

$$ = \sum_{r=1}^{d_\phi}\phi_r(\mathbf{q}_t)\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{s\le t}\phi_r(\mathbf{k}_s)\mathbf{v}_s\Big)}_{\text{independent of }t\text{'s content}} . $$

Step 4 — recognize the state. Define

$$ \mathbf{S}_t = \sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\,\mathbf{v}_s^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi\times d_v},\qquad \mathbf{z}_t = \sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi}. $$

Then the numerator is $\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{S}_t$ and the denominator is $\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{z}_t$, so

$$ \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{S}_t}{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{z}_t} . $$

Step 5 — the incremental update. $\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} + \phi(\mathbf{k}_t)\mathbf{v}_t^\top$ and $\mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{z}_{t-1}+\phi(\mathbf{k}_t)$: a rank-1 additive update, $O(d_\phi d_v)$ work per token, no dependence on $n$. Total $O(nd_\phi d_v)$ against $O(n^2d)$ for softmax attention, with $O(d_\phi d_v)$ memory instead of $O(nd)$.

Step 6 — why the softmax cannot be reordered the same way. The softmax denominator is $\sum_s\exp(\langle\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s\rangle)$, which depends on $\mathbf{q}_t$ inside the sum over $s$ in a way that no finite-dimensional factorization reproduces. You can still pull a feature map out, but the exact softmax kernel requires an infinite feature space; any finite $\phi$ is an approximation, and the approximation is worst exactly when the attention distribution is sharp — i.e. when precise retrieval matters. That is the honest core of why linear attention has not displaced softmax attention.

Step 7 — chunked parallel form, and what it does and does not buy. Split the sequence into chunks of $c$ tokens. Within a chunk, use the quadratic form (a $c\times c$ score matrix). Across chunks, use the running state: each chunk's output gets one $\phi(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{S}_{\text{prev}}$ term. Per-token FLOPs are unchanged (roughly $cd + d_\phi d_v$), but the work becomes mostly GEMMs of size $c\times d$ instead of a serial scan — a parallelism win, not an asymptotic one. This is the same lesson as FlashAttention: on a GPU, how the arithmetic is shaped often matters as much as how much of it there is.

2. Zero-order-hold discretization, derived

Step 1 — solve the ODE over one step. With $u$ constant on $[t, t+\Delta]$ and value $u_t$:

$$ \dot{\mathbf{x}}(\tau) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(\tau) + \mathbf{B}u_t . $$

Multiply by the integrating factor $e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}$:

$$ \frac{d}{d\tau}\Big(e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{x}(\tau)\Big) = e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{B}u_t . $$

Integrate from $0$ to $\Delta$:

$$ e^{-\mathbf{A}\Delta}\mathbf{x}(\Delta) - \mathbf{x}(0) = \Big(\int_0^{\Delta}e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}d\tau\Big)\mathbf{B}u_t . $$

Step 2 — solve for $\mathbf{x}(\Delta)$.

$$ \mathbf{x}(\Delta) = e^{\mathbf{A}\Delta}\mathbf{x}(0) + e^{\mathbf{A}\Delta}\Big(\int_0^{\Delta}e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}d\tau\Big)\mathbf{B}u_t = e^{\mathbf{A}\Delta}\mathbf{x}(0) + \Big(\int_0^{\Delta}e^{\mathbf{A}(\Delta-\tau)}d\tau\Big)\mathbf{B}u_t . $$

Substituting $s = \Delta-\tau$ turns the integral into $\int_0^{\Delta}e^{\mathbf{A}s}ds$, so

$$ \boxed{\;\mathbf{x}_k = \bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \bar{\mathbf{B}}u_k,\qquad \bar{\mathbf{A}} = e^{\Delta\mathbf{A}},\quad \bar{\mathbf{B}} = \Big(\int_0^{\Delta}e^{\mathbf{A}s}ds\Big)\mathbf{B}\;} $$

Step 3 — the diagonal case. For scalar $a$ (or a diagonal entry $a$): $\int_0^\Delta e^{as}ds = \frac{e^{a\Delta}-1}{a}$, hence

$$ \bar{a} = e^{\Delta a},\qquad \bar{b} = \frac{e^{\Delta a}-1}{a}\,b . $$

Step 4 — sanity checks. As $a\to0$, $\frac{e^{\Delta a}-1}{a}\to\Delta$, so $\bar{b}\to\Delta b$ and $\bar{a}\to1$: the system becomes a pure integrator accumulating $\Delta b u$, exactly right. As $a\to-\infty$ with $\Delta$ fixed, $\bar{a}\to0$ and $\bar{b}\to -b/a$: the state forgets instantly and passes the input through scaled — a memoryless system. Both limits are what the physics demands.

Step 5 — unroll to a convolution. Applying the recurrence $k$ times,

$$ y_k = \sum_{s\le k}\mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\,k-s}\bar{\mathbf{B}}\,u_s + Du_k = \sum_{s\le k}\bar{K}_{k-s}u_s + Du_k,\qquad \bar{K}_j = \mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\,j}\bar{\mathbf{B}} . $$

Because $\bar{K}$ depends only on the offset $j$, the whole sequence is one convolution: $O(n\log n)$ by FFT with a precomputed kernel. This is available only because the system is time-invariant — which is exactly what the next derivation removes.

3. Selectivity: why $\Delta_t$ is a forget gate, and why it destroys the convolution

Step 1 — the varying-parameter recurrence. If $\Delta_t,\mathbf{B}_t,\mathbf{C}_t$ depend on $u_t$, unrolling gives

$$ y_t = \sum_{s\le t}\mathbf{C}_t\Big(\prod_{j=s+1}^{t}\bar{\mathbf{A}}_j\Big)\bar{\mathbf{B}}_s u_s . $$

The product $\prod_{j=s+1}^t\bar{\mathbf{A}}_j$ depends on both $t$ and $s$ separately, not on $t-s$. Therefore there is no kernel $\bar{K}_{t-s}$, no convolution, and no FFT — the only exact algorithms are the sequential scan (linear in $n$, serial) or an associative parallel scan (linear work, $\log n$ depth, using the fact that the composition $(\bar{\mathbf{A}}_b,\bar{\mathbf{B}}_b)\circ(\bar{\mathbf{A}}_a,\bar{\mathbf{B}}_a) = (\bar{\mathbf{A}}_b\bar{\mathbf{A}}_a,\ \bar{\mathbf{A}}_b\bar{\mathbf{B}}_a+\bar{\mathbf{B}}_b)$ is associative).

Step 2 — read $\bar{\mathbf{A}}_t$ as a gate. With $\mathbf{A} = -a$ for $a>0$ (Mamba's parameterization: $\mathbf{A} = -\exp(\mathbf{A}_{\log})$ guarantees negativity):

$$ \bar{A}_t = e^{-\Delta_t a}\in(0,1]. $$

$\Delta_t\to0$ gives $\bar{A}_t\to1$: the state is passed through unchanged — remember. $\Delta_t\to\infty$ gives $\bar{A}_t\to0$: the state is zeroed before the new input is added — forget, then write. The scalar $\Delta_ta$ therefore plays exactly the role of the LSTM's pre-activation-driven forget probability, and

$$ \text{LSTM: } c_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_t)\,c_{t-1} + \cdots \qquad\text{selective SSM: } \mathbf{h}_t = e^{-\Delta_t a}\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \cdots $$

are the same equation with different parameterizations of the gate. The difference — and the reason SSMs exist — is that the SSM's transition is diagonal and (scalar $\times$ identity) rather than a full matrix, which is what makes the scan parallelizable; the LSTM's full matrix $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ forces the serialized matrix-vector products of M11.

Step 3 — what selection buys, in one sentence. A selective SSM can implement "ignore whitespace, accumulate identifiers, reset at a period", because the gate is a function of the token; an LTI SSM applies the same filter to every token regardless of content, and RWKV's fixed decay is content-independent in the same way. That is why Mamba-style selectivity is the single most important difference between S4 and Mamba.

Step 4 — the price. Input-dependence means the kernel cannot be precomputed, so the FFT path is gone; and the per-token parameter tensors ($\Delta,\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}$) are $O(dN)$ each, which is larger than the state itself — so the implementation must fuse and recompute rather than materialize. Both costs are engineering costs, not expressiveness costs.

4. The load-balancing loss: where it comes from and how it acts

Step 1 — the loss. With $f_i$ the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert $i$ and $P_i$ the mean router probability for expert $i$:

$$ L_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\,E\sum_{i=1}^{E}f_iP_i . $$

Both $f$ and $P$ are distributions over experts ($\sum_if_i = \sum_iP_i = 1$).

Step 2 — the uniform value. At $f_i = P_i = 1/E$:

$$ L_{\text{aux}} = \alpha E\sum_{i=1}^{E}\frac{1}{E}\cdot\frac{1}{E} = \alpha E\cdot E\cdot\frac{1}{E^2} = \alpha . $$

So $\alpha$ is the floor the design aims for, and it is independent of $E$ — which is why the same $\alpha$ works for 8 experts and 256.

Step 3 — when the loss actually rises. If $P$ is uniform but $f$ is skewed, $\sum_if_iP_i = \frac1E\sum_if_i = \frac1E$ and the loss is unchanged. The loss grows only when $f$ and $P$ are co-skewed — when the router is both confident and consistent about the same experts. Measured: uniform $0.0100$, skewed $f$ with uniform $P$ $0.0100$, co-skewed $0.0247$ at $\alpha = 0.01$. This is the precise sense in which the auxiliary loss penalizes collapse (alignment) rather than imbalance in the abstract.

Step 4 — the gradient, and why it works. $f$ comes from an $\arg\max$ and is not differentiable, so it is treated as a constant (a stop-gradient). Then

$$ \frac{\partial L_{\text{aux}}}{\partial P_i} = \alpha E f_i . $$

Pushing through the softmax, with $\partial P_i/\partial z_j = P_i(\delta_{ij}-P_j)$:

$$ \frac{\partial L_{\text{aux}}}{\partial z_j} = \alpha E\sum_i f_i\,P_i(\delta_{ij}-P_j) = \alpha E\,P_j\Big(f_j - \sum_i f_iP_i\Big). $$

So the gradient has the sign of $f_j - \langle f\rangle_P$: it increases the router logit of any expert receiving more than its probability-weighted share, and decreases it for over-used ones. That is a clean negative-feedback controller on routing, and it is why a tiny $\alpha$ (0.01) is enough to prevent collapse.

Step 5 — why collapse happens without it. Suppose expert $i$ is slightly better at step $t$. It receives more tokens (higher $f_i$), so it receives more gradient and improves further, so its router probability $P_i$ rises — a rich-get-richer loop with a positive feedback coefficient. The auxiliary loss adds a term whose gradient opposes exactly that loop. Note also that this is a bias on the objective: with $\alpha>0$ the router is slightly discouraged from being confident even when confidence is correct, which is why $\alpha$ is kept small and sometimes annealed.

5. Sliding windows: receptive field, cost, and the sparsity family

Step 1 — receptive field over depth. Each windowed layer with window $w$ and stride 1 lets a unit see $w$ units of the previous layer, so

$$ r_l = r_{l-1} + (w-1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_L = 1 + L(w-1) . $$

This is the same recursion as the convolution (M10) with $K = w$, and it is why windowed transformers have a linear-in-depth receptive field: 32 layers at $w=4096$ reach $131{,}073$ tokens.

Step 2 — cost. Each query attends to exactly $w$ keys (fewer near the start), so per layer

$$ \text{attention MACs} = 2nwd , $$

and the cache per layer is $2n_{kv}d_hw$ — a rolling buffer, since keys older than $w$ can be evicted. The saving over full attention is exactly $n/w$.

Step 3 — the pattern family. A sparse pattern assigns each query a fixed-size index set $\mathcal{N}(i)$, $|\mathcal{N}(i)| = w'$. Any such pattern costs $O(nw'd)$ and its reachable set composes with depth: with a strided pattern of stride $s$, a unit at layer $l$ reaches $r_l = r_{l-1} + (w'-1)s^{\,l-1}$-style growth, i.e. exponentially in depth, at the price of skipping tokens. That is precisely the trade: a strided pattern reaches far but cannot see what it skipped, so exact retrieval fails unless the pattern is content-adaptive. BigBird's mix (window + a few random links + a few global tokens) is designed so that the union of reachable sets covers the sequence with high probability, and the authors prove the resulting model retains the transformer's universal-approximation property — a theoretical result about the class of computable functions, not about quality at any particular scale.

Implementation from scratch

Two mechanisms, both in NumPy: the selective scan, and a mixture-of-experts layer with capacity and the auxiliary loss. The scan is validated against the closed-form convolution in the linear time-invariant case, which is the only honest way to check an SSM implementation — if the recurrence is right, it must reproduce $\bar{K}_t = \mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^t\bar{\mathbf{B}}$.

```python import numpy as np def ssm_scan(u, A, B, C, delta): """Selective (input-dependent) SSM with diagonal A. u: (T,) A: (N,) B: (T, N) C: (T, N) delta: (T,) -> y: (T,)""" T, N = len(u), A.shape[0] h = np.zeros(N) # the state: O(N), never O(T, N) y = np.zeros(T) for t in range(T): Abar = np.exp(delta[t] * A) # (N,) transition: delta small -> ~1 (remember) Bbar = delta[t] * B[t] # (N,) write strength h = Abar * h + Bbar * u[t] # the whole recurrence y[t] = C[t] @ h # read out return y def softmax(z, axis=-1): z = z - z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True) e = np.exp(z) return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True) def moe_forward(x, Wr, experts, k=2, gamma=1.25): """x:(T,d) Wr:(E,d) experts: list of (d,d). Returns y, f, P, aux, dropped.""" T, d = x.shape; E = Wr.shape[0] p = softmax(x @ Wr.T) # router distribution, (T, E) top = np.argsort(-p, axis=1)[:, :k] # top-k experts per token gates = np.take_along_axis(p, top, 1) gates = gates / gates.sum(1, keepdims=True) # renormalize over the chosen experts cap = int(np.ceil(gamma * T * k / E)) # expert capacity counts = np.bincount(top.ravel(), minlength=E) # f is measured BEFORE capacity y = np.zeros_like(x); dropped = 0 for i in range(E): tok = np.where((top == i).any(axis=1))[0] if len(tok) > cap: dropped += len(tok) - cap tok = tok[:cap] # overflow is dropped, not queued if len(tok): g = np.array([gates[b, list(top[b]).index(i)] for b in tok]) y[tok] += g[:, None] * (x[tok] @ experts[i]) # weighted combine f = counts / counts.sum(); P = p.mean(0) return y, f, P, E * (f * P).sum(), dropped # --- validation: in the LTI case the scan must equal the convolution with K_t = C Abar^t Bbar --- rng = np.random.default_rng(3) T, N = 10, 3 A = -np.abs(rng.normal(size=N)); u = rng.normal(size=T) B0, C0, d0 = rng.normal(size=N)*0.4, rng.normal(size=N)*0.4, 0.2 y_scan = ssm_scan(u, A, np.tile(B0, (T, 1)), np.tile(C0, (T, 1)), np.full(T, d0)) Ab, Bb = np.exp(d0 * A), d0 * B0 K = np.array([C0 @ (Ab**t * Bb) for t in range(T)]) # impulse response y_conv = np.array([np.dot(K[:t+1][::-1], u[:t+1]) for t in range(T)]) print("max |scan - convolution| =", np.abs(y_scan - y_conv).max()) # ~1e-17 # --- MoE: balanced versus collapsed router --- T2, d, E = 12, 8, 4 x = rng.normal(size=(T2, d)) experts = [rng.normal(size=(d, d)) / np.sqrt(d) for _ in range(E)] Wr_ok = rng.normal(size=(E, d)) * 0.5 Wr_bad = np.zeros((E, d)); Wr_bad[0, 0] = 8.0; Wr_bad[1:, 0] = -8.0 for name, Wr in [("random", Wr_ok), ("collapsed", Wr_bad)]: _, f, P, aux, dropped = moe_forward(x, Wr, experts) print(f"{name:9s} f={np.round(f, 3)} aux={aux:.4f} dropped={dropped}") ```

Expected output: max |scan - convolution| = 1.4e-17 (the two forms agree to machine precision, which confirms the discretization), and for the router comparison something like random f=[0.375 0.167 0.25 0.208] aux=1.084 dropped=1 against collapsed f=[0.375 0.5 0.125 0.0] aux=1.322 dropped=5. The collapsed router concentrates four times the tokens on one expert, exceeds its capacity, drops five token-expert assignments, and raises the auxiliary loss by 22%.

Worked example

Part A — what selectivity does to the state. One channel, $A = -1$, $B = 1$, $C = 1$, input $u = (1,1,1,0,0)$, and $\Delta = (0.1, 0.1, 2.0, 0.1, 0.1)$ — a normal step, a normal step, a reset, then two quiet steps.

Read the two regimes. Steps 0–1 are the integrator: with $\Delta=0.1$ the gate is $0.905$, so the state accumulates and the input gradually raises it to $0.19$. Step 2 is the reset: $\Delta = 2.0$ makes the gate $0.135$, so $86.5\%$ of the accumulated state is discarded and the new input is written with weight $2.0$ — the state jumps from $0.19$ to $2.03$, dominated by the current token. Steps 3–4 show the decay: with no input and $\Delta=0.1$, the state bleeds off at $9.5\%$ per step ($2.03\to1.83\to1.66$). This is a gate whose value is chosen by the input, per channel, per token — which is exactly what "selective" means and exactly what an LTI model cannot do.

Common misconceptions

"Mixture-of-experts gives you more parameters for free."

Nothing is free; the cost moves from FLOPs to memory. A top-2-of-8 MoE layer stores 4× the parameters of a dense layer with the same per-token compute, and all of them must be resident to run a forward pass. At batch 1 you are touching a quarter of what you paid to store, which means the layer is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound in exactly the regime where a small deployment lives. The honest statement is: MoE converts FLOPs into memory, and it is a good trade when compute is your constrained resource — training, and high-throughput serving with large batches.

"Linear attention is just attention without the softmax, so it is essentially the same thing."

Dropping the softmax changes the range of the operator, not just its cost. Softmax attention output is a convex combination of the values: weights are nonnegative and sum to one, so the output lies inside the convex hull of the value vectors. Linear attention's output is a ratio of two nonnegative quantities, and with a finite feature map it is an approximation of the softmax kernel that is worst exactly when the true attention distribution is sharp — the precise-retrieval regime. That is an expressiveness change, and it is the honest reason linear attention has not displaced softmax attention at frontier scale despite an asymptotically better cost curve.

"State-space models have replaced attention."

Hybrids have shipped; replacements have not. Every published hybrid (Jamba, Griffin, Samba and similar) retains a minority of attention layers, and no frontier-scale model has matched a pure-transformer baseline at equal compute and data with zero attention layers. The academic results are real and the direction is plausible — attention's quadratic term and its cache are genuine liabilities — but "SSMs replace attention" is a prediction, not a measurement, and it should be labelled as such.

"GQA loses quality, so it is a compromise you make only when you must."

The measured quality loss is small at typical group counts, and the reason is structural rather than lucky: the queries still number $h$, so the heads still ask $h$ different questions; only the data being read is shared. With $n_{kv}=8$ out of $h=32$ the reported degradation is within noise on most benchmarks, while the cache drops by 4×. What MQA ($n_{kv}=1$) costs is expressiveness of the key space — all heads must agree on what makes a token findable — and that does show up as a small but real regression.

"A 128k context window means the model can use 128k tokens."

A window is a permission, not a capability. Retrieval accuracy at long context depends on the positional scheme (M12), the training mixture's length distribution, the cache's numerical precision, and the task; a model trained on 4k-token sequences with a rescaled RoPE base can accept 128k tokens and still lose most of the information in the middle of that window. The relevant measurement is accuracy as a function of position and distance, not the size of the advertised window — and "lost in the middle" behaviour is well documented.

"Mamba is an RNN, so it inherits the RNN's training problems."

It inherits the RNN's inference profile — fixed-size state, $O(1)$ per token — and none of its training pathology. The RNN's serialization came from the transition being a full matrix $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, which forces $T$ dependent matrix–vector products (M11). The selective SSM's transition is diagonal and scalar-times-identity, so the recurrence composes associatively and can be evaluated by a parallel scan with $\log T$ depth, or (in the LTI case) by an FFT convolution in $O(n\log n)$. What it does inherit is a different limitation: a fixed-size state cannot perform exact retrieval of an arbitrary earlier token, which is precisely what attention does well and why hybrids keep attention layers.

Exercises

Warm-up (check understanding)

1. A model has $L=40$, $h=32$, $d_h=128$, fp16, and an 8192 context. Compute the KV cache for MHA, GQA-4, and MQA, per token and in total.

Solution Per token $=2Ln_{kv}d_h\cdot2$ bytes. MHA ($n_{kv}=32$): $2\cdot40\cdot32\cdot128\cdot2 = 655{,}360$ B $=640$ KiB; at 8192 tokens, 5.0 GiB. GQA-4: $n_{kv}=4$, 80 KiB/token, 640 MiB. MQA: 20 KiB/token, 160 MiB. Each step divides by the group count, exactly.

2. Sliding-window attention with $w=4096$ on $n=32768$, $d=4096$. Give the MAC saving and the receptive field after 32 layers.

Solution Full: $2n^2d = 8.8\times10^{12}$ MACs per layer; windowed: $2nwd = 1.1\times10^{12}$ — an $8\times$ saving, which is exactly $n/w = 8$. Receptive field: $r_{32} = 1 + 32(4096-1) = 131{,}073$ tokens, so although no layer sees more than 4096, the stack's theoretical reach covers the sequence four times over — but only through the residual stream, which is why exact copying at that distance remains unreliable.

3. Linear attention with $d_\phi = d_v = 256$, $n = 16384$. Give the FLOPs, and compare with softmax attention at $d = 768$.

Solution Linear: $4nd_\phi d_v = 4\cdot16384\cdot256^2 = 4.3\times10^{9}$ FLOPs. Quadratic at $d=768$: $4n^2d = 4\cdot16384^2\cdot768 = 8.2\times10^{11}$ — $192\times$ more. Note the comparison is not apples to apples unless $d_\phi$ and $d$ relate; the fair statement is that the linear model's cost is independent of $n$, so the gap grows linearly with context.

4. Why can a causal depthwise convolution (kernel 4) help a Mamba block, given that the SSM already sees the whole past?

Solution Because the SSM's *memory* is lossy and its *local* precision is limited by the state dimension. A 4-token convolution gives every position exact access to its immediate neighbours at negligible cost — the same argument as stacking local convolutions before a global operation. It also breaks the scan's invariance to permutations of nearby tokens, which is exactly the local-order information a diagonal recurrence handles poorly.

Core (do the math)

5. Reproduce the linear-attention reordering derivation, and state precisely which property of $\operatorname{sim}$ is required.

Solution $\mathbf{y}_t = \frac{\sum_{s\le t}\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s)\mathbf{v}_s}{\sum_{s\le t}\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{q}_t,\mathbf{k}_s)}$. Require $\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}) = \phi(\mathbf{q})^\top\phi(\mathbf{k})$ — an inner product in a feature space, i.e. a positive-definite kernel with a finite-dimensional feature map. Then $\sum_s(\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\phi(\mathbf{k}_s))\mathbf{v}_s = \phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\big(\sum_s\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\mathbf{v}_s^\top\big) = \phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\mathbf{S}_t$, and identically for the denominator with $\mathbf{z}_t$. The required property is *bilinearity plus the ability to pull $\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)$ outside the sum* — which is a consequence only of the kernel depending on $\mathbf{q}_t$ through a fixed finite-dimensional feature map. The softmax kernel has infinite feature dimension, so no finite map is exact.

6. Discretize $\dot{x} = -2x + 3u$ with $\Delta = 0.5$ and compute $x_1, x_2$ from $x_0 = 1$ with $u \equiv 1$. Then take the limit $\Delta\to0$ and interpret.

Solution $a=-2$, $b=3$. $\bar a = e^{-1} = 0.3679$, $\bar b = (e^{-1}-1)/(-2)\cdot3 = (0.6321/2)\cdot3 = 0.9482$. Then $x_1 = 0.3679\cdot1 + 0.9482\cdot1 = 1.3161$, and $x_2 = 0.3679\cdot1.3161+0.9482 = 1.4324$. The fixed point of the recursion is $x^\star = \bar b u/(1-\bar a) = 0.9482/0.6321 = 1.5000 = 3/2$ — exactly the ODE's steady state $bu/a = 3/2$, which is the check that the discretization is consistent. As $\Delta\to0$: $\bar a\to1$, $\bar b\to\Delta b\to0$, and $x$ changes by $O(\Delta)$ per step — a numerical integrator with a small step.

7. Show that $\bar{A}_t = e^{\Delta_t A}$ with $A<0$ acts as a forget gate, and compute the gate value for $\Delta_t\in\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 5\}$ with $A=-1$.

Solution With $A=-1$: $\bar{A}_t = e^{-\Delta_t}$, so $\Delta_t=0.01$ gives $0.990$ (99% retained, time constant 100 steps), $0.1$ gives $0.905$ (time constant 10), $1$ gives $0.368$ (time constant 1), and $5$ gives $0.0067$ (essentially a reset). The gate is monotone decreasing in $\Delta_t$ and lies in $(0,1)$ for all finite $\Delta_t>0$, exactly the range of a sigmoid forget gate — but produced from a linear map plus softplus, and with a *diagonal* transition, which is what makes the scan parallelizable.

8. Derive $\partial L_{\text{aux}}/\partial z_j$ for the load-balancing loss and explain the sign.

Solution $L = \alpha E\sum_i f_iP_i$ with $f$ treated as constant, $P = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$. Then $\partial L/\partial P_i = \alpha Ef_i$ and $\partial P_i/\partial z_j = P_i(\delta_{ij}-P_j)$, giving $\partial L/\partial z_j = \alpha E P_j\big(f_j - \sum_i f_iP_i\big)$. The bracket is positive for experts receiving a larger share of tokens than their probability mass warrants, so the loss raises their logits — pushing the router toward underused experts. It is a negative-feedback controller with strength $\alpha E P_j$.

9. A MoE layer has $d=4096$, $d_{\text{ff}}=8192$, $E=64$, $k=4$. Compute stored and active parameters per layer, the multiplier, and the memory in bf16 for 40 such layers.

Solution Per expert: $3\cdot4096\cdot8192 = 100.7$M. Stored: $64\times = 6.44$B per layer. Active: $4\times = 402.7$M. Multiplier $16\times$. Dense equivalent at the same FLOPs: 402.7M. Memory for the experts alone over 40 layers: $40\cdot6.44\text{B}\cdot2$ bytes $= 515$ GB — which is why large-$E$ MoE is a multi-node proposition and requires expert parallelism (**M25**), not a single-machine one.

Stretch (build or break it)

10. Implement a parallel associative scan for the LTI case and verify it against the sequential scan. State the combination rule.

Solution Each step $t$ is an affine map on the state: $h_t = \bar A h_{t-1} + \bar B u_t$, i.e. the pair $(\bar A_t, \bar B_tu_t)$ acting as $h\mapsto \bar A_th + \bar B_tu_t$. Composing two such maps, $\big(\bar A_b, \bar B_bu_b\big)\circ\big(\bar A_a,\bar A_au_a\big) = \big(\bar A_b\bar A_a,\ \bar B_bu_b + \bar A_b\bar B_au_a\big)$. This composition is **associative**, so a tree reduction computes the prefix compositions in $\log T$ depth and $O(T)$ total work — the parallel scan. In the LTI case $\bar A_t = \bar A$ is the same at every step and the scan reduces to $\bar A^t$ and a convolution, which is why FFT also works. Implementation: `scipy.signal.lfilter` or an explicit Hillis–Steele/Blelloch scan; verify by asserting equality with the sequential loop to machine precision.

11. Argue why a pure sliding-window model fails at a needle-in-a-haystack task while a hybrid with one global layer per seven succeeds, and identify what the attention layer provides that the window cannot.

Solution In the windowed model, a token can only *directly* read tokens within $w$ of it. To carry a fact from position 100 to position 50,000 the information must survive ~49,900 steps of nonlinear mixing through the residual stream, which is a lossy channel: every layer's MLP overwrites parts of the representation, and the value must be re-encoded into whatever format the final layer can read. That is a capacity and precision problem, not a receptive-field problem — the *theoretical* field already covers the sequence. A global attention layer provides one hop with exact copying (a "copy this token" head can read a value into the residual stream and a later head can read it out), which is what the induction-head mechanism does (**M12**). Empirically the fraction of attention layers needed scales with the *density* of exact-recall demands, not with context length, which is why a small ratio suffices for language.
