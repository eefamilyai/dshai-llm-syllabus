How to Use This Syllabus

Read this first. It decides whether the other twenty-nine modules work.

on this page

Why this module exists

People rarely fail at this material because a derivation is too hard. They fail because they study without a schedule, read instead of doing, never test themselves, and quit at the first plateau without knowing that plateaus are the normal shape of learning. This module fixes the logistics. It tells you what to do in a session, in what order, for how long, and how to tell whether it worked. Twenty minutes here saves you a hundred hours later.

What this course is, and what it is not

It is a first-principles curriculum. Every algorithm is derived rather than described. Every claim about cost or complexity is arithmetic you can check. Every module ends with things you must be able to do without notes.

It is not a video series you can watch passively, a tour of this month's papers, or a shortcut to a job. There is no shortcut, and any resource promising one is selling you the feeling of progress rather than the thing itself.

The intended outcome is specific: after finishing, you should be able to read a modern architecture paper and reconstruct why each design choice is there; implement a transformer and its training loop without copying; and diagnose a training run that is not working.

The shape of the material

Roughly 120,000 words across thirty modules in eight phases, with around fifty diagrams and eighteen interactive widgets. That sounds like a lot, and it is — but the phases are strictly ordered, and each one only depends on the ones before it.

The phases are:

  1. Mathematical foundations (M01–M04) — the four languages you need: linear algebra, calculus, probability, optimization.
  2. Machine learning bedrock (M05) — what learning from data even means, and how it is measured.
  3. The neural network core (M06–M09) — the neuron, the gradient, the training loop, and building it yourself.
  4. Architectures (M10–M13) — convolution, recurrence, attention, and what came after.
  5. Large language models (M14–M23) — tokens to pretraining to preference tuning to serving, evaluation, interpretability, retrieval, and multimodality.
  6. Systems and scale (M24–M26) — the GPU, distributed training, and the engineering that makes it all fit.
  7. Practice (M27–M29) — research method, a project ladder, and the open problems.

How to study: the mechanics

Retrieval beats rereading, decisively

Rereading a derivation produces a feeling of fluency that is not knowledge. Testing yourself produces the knowledge, and feels worse while it is happening. This is not a matter of taste; it is the most replicated finding in the learning-science literature.

Practically: at the end of every study session, close the material and write, from memory, the one thing you just learned — a derivation, a shape rule, an algorithm's invariant. Then check. The gap between what you wrote and what is correct is the only reliable signal of what you actually know.

The four-beat rhythm

Every concept in Core concepts follows the same four beats, deliberately:

  1. Intuition — a plain-language picture.
  2. Formal definition — the precise statement.
  3. Why it is built this way — the failure it prevents.
  4. Worked micro-example — small numbers, computed completely.

When you revise, do not reread. Reconstruct the four beats from the heading alone. If you can produce beat 3 — why it is built this way — you understand the concept. If you can only produce beat 2, you have memorised it.

Spacing

Keep a single list of every derivation in the course. Re-derive one item per day, chosen at random, taking about ten minutes. This costs you seventy hours over a year and is worth more than any amount of rereading. The mechanism is simple: the act of retrieving something that has become slightly hard is what consolidates it.

The whiteboard test

For any algorithm module, the test is: from a blank page, write the forward pass, the backward pass, and the update rule, with correct tensor shapes, in under fifteen minutes. Not "understand" it — reproduce it. The shape errors are where the real gaps live.

Implement, then break it

Every module has a Implementation from scratch section. Type it out — do not copy-paste — then deliberately break it: remove the scaling factor in attention, delete the residual connection, set the learning rate a hundred times too high. Watching a correct implementation fail in a specific way is what turns the design choices in your head from trivia into constraints.

The three-strike rule

When stuck, apply a limit: twenty minutes of your own effort, then a look at the relevant earlier module, then a look at the solution, then move on and return after a night's sleep. Most stuck-ness on this material is either a prerequisite you skipped or a plateau that resolves itself with time. Neither is solved by staring for four hours.

Time split

For every hour of study, spend roughly forty minutes doing (deriving on paper, writing code, answering exercises) and twenty minutes reading. If you find yourself at ninety-twenty in the reading direction, stop and pick up a pen.

Prerequisites check

This is a self-diagnostic, not an entrance exam. Answer before reading further, then check. The routing matters more than the score.

  1. Multiply a $3 \times 4$ matrix by a $4 \times 5$ matrix. What is the output shape, and how many scalar multiply-adds does it take?
  2. If $f(x) = (3x + 1)^2$, what is $f'(x)$?
  3. What does it mean for two random variables to be independent?
  4. If a coin has a 30% chance of heads and you flip it ten times, roughly how many heads do you expect, and with what spread?
  5. Where does the gradient of a function point, geometrically?
  6. In Python, what does np.array([[1,2],[3,4]]) @ np.array([1,1]) produce?
  7. What is the difference between a parameter and a hyperparameter?
  8. What does it mean for a function to be convex?
  9. Given a list of 1,000 numbers, how would you compute the mean in one pass?
  10. What is the derivative of $\ln x$, and why does it appear in loss functions?
Answers and routing 1. $3 \times 5$; $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 60$ multiply-adds. Missed this → **M01**. 2. $f'(x) = 6(3x+1)$. Missed this → **M02**. 3. $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$; knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Missed → **M03**. 4. Expect 3, standard deviation $\sqrt{10 \cdot 0.3 \cdot 0.7} \approx 1.45$. Missed → **M03**. 5. In the direction of steepest increase; $- \nabla f$ is steepest descent. Missed → **M02** / **M04**. 6. `[3 7]`. Missed → **M01**. 7. Parameters are learned from data; hyperparameters are chosen before training. Missed → **M05**. 8. Any point on the segment between two points of the graph lies above the graph. Missed → **M04**. 9. Accumulate a running sum and divide. Missed → you are not ready; fix the coding gap first. 10. $1/x$; it appears because log-probability turns products into sums. Missed → **M03**. **Routing.** Miss 1 or 6: start at M01. Miss 2 or 5: do M02 before anything else. Miss 3, 4, or 10: M03 first. Comfortable with all ten: start at M04 and treat M01–M03 as reference.

Learning objectives

After this module you can:

  • State what a session of study should contain, and how long each part takes.
  • Explain why retrieval practice beats rereading, in terms of the mechanism, not the slogan.
  • Apply the four-beat structure to revise any concept from its heading alone.
  • Choose a pacing track that fits your available hours and state what you are skipping.
  • Diagnose your own prerequisite gap and name the module that fixes it.
  • Design a self-assessment that is not fooled by familiarity.

Three pacing tracks

The material is the same; the depth of engagement differs.

Track Duration Hours/week What you do What you skip
Sprint 12 weeks 20–25 Every module, exercises from the Warm-up and Core tiers only, one capstone Stretch exercises, the systems phase compressed to reading
Standard 6 months 10–12 Every module, all exercises, two capstones, all implementations typed Optional further reading
Thorough 12 months 6–8 Every module twice — once for breadth, once for derivation drills — plus a from-scratch project per phase Nothing

Do the arithmetic before you start. Twelve weeks at ten hours is 120 hours, which does not cover this material at depth; pretending otherwise is how people quit in week seven. Pick a track you can actually sustain, and write the weekly slot in your calendar as an appointment you would not move.

Reading your own scores honestly

Your judgement of "do I know this" is noisy. If you self-test on ten questions and get eight right, the true rate could plausibly be anywhere from about 55% to 95%. Ten questions cannot resolve a 10% improvement — you need roughly a hundred items for that, and you need to vary the wording, because a question you have seen before measures recognition rather than knowledge.

This is why the Self-test sections are for calibration, not certification, and why the Checkpoint sections ask for production rather than recognition: write the derivation, run the experiment.

How to use the diagrams and widgets

The figures are arguments, not decoration. A well-made diagram states a claim about structure — where the gradient flows, what is cached, which matrix is shared — that the prose can only describe. Read the caption first, then look at the diagram, then check whether the diagram actually shows what the caption claims.

The interactive widgets are experiments. Each one is a small simulation with a control you can move. A worked example in prose shows you one outcome; a widget shows you the relationship. Where a widget exists, use it before reading the derivation — you will predict the result and be wrong, which is exactly the state in which the derivation lands.

Three worth doing first, when you reach them: drag the learning rate past the stability bound in M04's descent explorer and watch the trajectory explode; toggle the causal mask in M12's attention widget and watch the matrix become triangular; drag the temperature to 2.0 in M15 and watch the distribution flatten while the ranking never changes.

How to know you have learned something

Three levels, in increasing order of evidence:

  • Recognition — "that looks right." Worth nothing on its own.
  • Production — you can write it from a blank page. This is the level every Checkpoint targets.
  • Transfer — you can use it in a situation the module did not present. This is what the Stretch exercises are for, and it is what actually transfers to real work.

Only the third level survives a month. If you finish a module at level two, schedule a revisit in three weeks and test again.

Notation and conventions

One notation runs through all thirty modules. It is worth memorising now, because every derivation assumes it.

Symbol Meaning
$x$, $y$ scalars
$\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{y}$ vectors, bold lowercase
$\mathbf{W}$, $\mathbf{X}$ matrices, bold uppercase
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ $n$ rows, $d$ columns
$\mathbf{x}_i$ the $i$-th row or example
$x_j$ the $j$-th element of a vector
$\mathbf{W}_{ij}$ row $i$, column $j$
$\mathbf{W}^\top$ transpose
$\odot$ elementwise product
$\|\mathbf{x}\|_p$ the $p$-norm
$\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\rangle$ inner product
$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}$ gradient of the loss with respect to all parameters
$\partial f / \partial x$ partial derivative
$\sigma(\cdot)$ the sigmoid function
$\mathcal{L}$ the loss
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ all parameters collectively
$\eta$ the learning rate
$D$, $N$ the dataset, and its number of examples
$L$, $T$ number of layers, number of tokens

Two conventions that trip people up, both addressed where they first matter:

  • Matrix layout. PyTorch and NumPy use the numerator layout for Jacobians, so a gradient has the same shape as the thing it is the gradient of. M02 derives both conventions and shows a concrete case where mixing them silently flips a result.
  • Row versus column vectors. A batch of examples is stored as rows, so $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ mixes features and $\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ projects a column. Both appear in the literature; this syllabus uses the row-batch convention throughout and says so when a formula is transposed relative to a paper you may have read.

Common misconceptions

"I need to finish the maths before I can start"

You need enough maths to survive M01–M04 at the level the modules ask for, and no more. The rest arrives repeatedly, in context, when a derivation needs it. People who wait until they "know the maths" never start; the maths is learned by using it on something concrete.

"Watching a lecture is studying"

Watching is input. The retrieval, the writing, and the debugging are the studying. A lecture you cannot reproduce on a blank page the next day contributed approximately nothing, however good it was.

"If I understand it, I can implement it"

Understanding is necessary and not sufficient. Shape errors, dtype errors, and off-by-one indexing kill real implementations of things people understand perfectly. The gap closes only by typing code.

"The maths in these papers is too hard for me"

The maths in most architecture papers is matrix multiplication, a softmax, and the chain rule — all of which you will have by M12. What makes papers feel hard is missing context, not missing technique. M27 teaches the reading protocol for exactly this.

"Emergence means the model suddenly learned a new capability"

This is one of the most repeated claims in the field and one of the least well supported. Many apparent emergence curves are artefacts of the metric chosen — a discontinuous score over a continuous underlying capability. M16 covers the evidence honestly, and it is contested.

"Larger is always better"

Compute-optimal training, inference cost, and data quality all contradict this in different ways. M16 and M19 give the arithmetic.

Exercises

Warm-up (check understanding)

  1. Write the one-sentence version of the four-beat rhythm, and the test for whether you understood a concept rather than memorised it.
  2. State the time split you intend to use, and what the forty-minute "doing" half will contain for a maths module.

Core (do the math)

  1. You have ten hours a week for six months. Compute your total hours, then allocate them across the eight phases proportionally to the module week counts in the tables. Where is the plan impossible, and what do you cut?
  2. You self-test on fifteen items and score eleven. Compute the 95% confidence interval for your true rate. What sample size would halve the half-width?

Stretch (build or break it)

  1. Design a retrieval schedule for a single module that takes ten minutes a day for three weeks. Specify exactly which item you re-derive on which day, and justify the spacing rather than picking round numbers.
  2. Find a study technique you currently use that produces fluency without knowledge. Name it, state the mechanism by which it fails, and replace it with something that does not.
Solutions **1.** Intuition, formal definition, why it is built this way, worked micro-example. The test is beat 3: if you can explain the failure the design prevents, you understand it; if you can only recite the definition, you have memorised it. **2.** Roughly forty minutes doing to twenty minutes reading, per hour. For a maths module the doing half is: re-derive the previous session's theorem on blank paper, then attempt the Core exercises before reading their solutions. **3.** Six months at ten hours is about 260 hours. If you follow the module week counts (roughly 60 weeks at 10 h/week of nominal load), you are about 40% short. The honest cut is: compress phase 6 to reading plus one implementation, drop the Stretch tier, and keep phase 5 at full depth — it is where the LLM-specific value is. Do not cut phase 1; everything downstream decays without it. **4.** $\hat{p} = 11/15 = 0.733$. Standard error $= \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n} = \sqrt{0.733 \cdot 0.267/15} \approx 0.114$. The 95% interval is roughly $0.733 \pm 0.224$, i.e. 51% to 96%. To halve the half-width you need four times the items: about sixty. **5.** A defensible schedule: day 1 derive from notes; day 2 from notes; day 4 blank page; day 7 blank page; day 12 blank page; day 20 blank page. The gaps grow roughly by a factor of 1.6–2, because each retrieval should be performed at the edge of forgetting, not immediately after learning. Round numbers are fine as long as the gaps expand. **6.** Highlighting, rereading, and watching solutions being worked. They fail because the information is present in the environment, so no retrieval is required, and the ease of processing is misread as mastery. Replacement: close the material and produce the content, then compare.

Self-test

  1. Why does rereading produce false confidence?
  2. What is the practical test for whether you understood a concept rather than memorised it?
  3. Why should the review gaps expand rather than stay constant?
  4. What does the Checkpoint section of each module ask you to do, and why is that the right target?
  5. Why is a ten-question self-test a poor instrument for measuring improvement?
  6. What should you do in the first twenty minutes of being stuck?
  7. What is the intended reading-to-doing ratio, and what happens if you invert it?
  8. Name the three levels of knowing and state which one survives a month.
Answers 1. Because the material is in front of you, so no retrieval is required; the fluency of processing is misattributed to memory strength. 2. Beat 3 of the four-beat rhythm: explain what failure the design prevents, without notes. 3. Because consolidation happens when retrieval is effortful; performing it too soon tests working memory rather than long-term storage. 4. Produce the derivation or the code from a blank page with correct shapes — production, not recognition, is what transfers. 5. Fifteen binary items carry roughly $\pm 20$ percentage points at 95% confidence; a 10% improvement is invisible inside that noise. 6. Twenty minutes of your own effort, then check the prerequisite module, then read the solution, then move on and revisit after sleeping. 7. Roughly 40:20 doing to reading per hour. Inverted, you accumulate familiarity with no capability, and the gap surfaces later as an inability to implement. 8. Recognition, production, transfer. Transfer survives; recognition survives about a day.

Further reading

  • Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, Make It Stick (2014) — the accessible summary of the testing-effect and spacing literature. Extract: the specific study protocols, not the anecdotes.
  • Bjork & Bjork, "Desirable difficulties in theory and practice" — why making learning feel harder makes it stick. Extract: the distinction between storage strength and retrieval strength.
  • Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), on deliberate practice — the original paper. Extract: the definition (specific goal, immediate feedback, at the edge of ability), and note how often the popular version drops those three conditions.
  • Sweller, on cognitive load theory — why worked examples help novices and hurt experts. Extract: the expertise-reversal effect, which is why this syllabus gives solutions.
  • The Feynman Lectures, volume I, chapter 1 — not about learning science, but the best demonstration of the explanatory standard to aim for.

Checkpoint

You are done with this module when you can, without notes:

  • State the four beats and identify which one tests understanding.
  • Explain the mechanism by which rereading fails and retrieval works.
  • Give your own total available hours, computed, not estimated, and say which modules you are cutting.
  • Describe one study habit you are dropping and what replaces it.
  • Explain why a fifteen-item self-test cannot measure a 10% improvement.

Then open M01 and start. Do not read ahead in the module order — the dependencies are real, and skipping creates the specific confusion that makes people believe they are bad at this.
